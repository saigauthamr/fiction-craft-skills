# Story learn

The writer is here to learn. Teach well, then get out of the way. Do not write the story for them, and change nothing in the project.

## Steps

1. **Find the technique.** If the writer named one, use it. If they described a problem ("my villain is flat"), map it to the closest framework and say which one you are teaching and why. Load the matching skill through its hub (`plot`, `character`, `magic-worldbuilding`, `scene-craft`, `foundations`).
2. **Ask one question first**: what they already know about it, or what they are trying to do with it. Skip it if they already said.
3. **Explain in plain words**: the idea in a few short paragraphs, with its parts. One example from a well-known book or film, and one from the writer's own story if the project has one (`kb/`, `work/spec/`). Say when the technique does not apply, and when writers break it on purpose.
4. **Give a small exercise** on their own material, about ten minutes, that they do: "List what your magic can do, what it costs, and what stops it."
5. **Respond to their attempt**: what works, what does not yet, the next step. If it shows a gap in understanding, teach that gap, briefly.
6. **Close.** Offer to go deeper, move to a related technique, or apply it to the draft. Do not push them into a workflow.

## Rules

- Do not draft scenes, chapters, or dialogue for their book. A short invented illustration of a technique is fine.
- One technique at a time. If their question needs two, teach the first and offer the second.
- If the technique disagrees with other common advice, say so and say which to use when.
- Name the technique so they can find it again, whatever the craft-language setting: learning is the one place names are the point.
- Use the craft checks below as the questions a technique asks of a story.
