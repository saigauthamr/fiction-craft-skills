# Sanderson's Third Law

Before adding something new, expand what you already have. Depth beats breadth, and a few elegant rules that interact are worth more than a pile of finicky ones. The value of a world comes from how its ideas affect each other and how far each is explored, and density of ideas matters far less.

## Checks

- `magic.third.01` Before adding a new element, could something already in the story do this job: a changed rule, a second use of an existing magic, a branch of an existing group?
- `magic.third.02` For each core rule, have its consequences for economy, war, family, religion, or crime been followed?
- `magic.third.03` If a new element is added, is it linked to something already on the page?

## Examples

- Two religions can be written as branches of a single faith that split over doctrine before inventing an unrelated religion. The shared roots give conflict and texture for free.
- A single premise, such as a world where the dark lord already won, can support several books of exploration by asking what that does to prophecy, rulers, heroes, and everyday life.
- A game with ten thousand near-identical dungeons loses to one with fifty handcrafted ones.
- A draft that follows ten character arcs thins each of them. Cutting to a few focused arcs lets each develop.

## Cautions and exceptions

- The law does not forbid adding new things. Longer series can sustain more.
- Pressure from fans or publishers to multiply systems is a reason to apply the check, because a new system added for count alone rarely earns its place.
- Depth in the world is separate from depth shown on the page. Keep most of what you build as hidden structure, and show only what the scene needs.
- Limits stack: many flaws and costs on one system also break this law. See `magic-second-law`.
- When contrast helps, build from what differs and what stays the same between your world and ours rather than from a long list of new inventions.
