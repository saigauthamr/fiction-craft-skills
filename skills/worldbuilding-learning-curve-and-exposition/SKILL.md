# Learning Curve and Exposition

Every book asks the reader to learn something: who the people are, how the world works, what the rules are. That demand is the learning curve. You choose where it is steep and where it is shallow, and you pay for it in the reader's attention.

## Choosing the curve

- **Shallow curve.** Familiar settings and genre staples need little explanation. A wormhole in science fiction or a football game in a real-world story arrives pre-learned. Readers can go straight to character and plot.
- **Steep curve.** Heavy names, invented terms, and unfamiliar rules promise a demanding read. This is a legitimate choice when it is deliberate, and it suits readers who want to be dropped in.
- **Real-world settings** shift the cost. You skip explaining the world, but expert readers will notice errors, and a few wrong small details can make them distrust everything else. Cover gaps with a viewpoint character who does not know the domain, so the mistakes read as characterization, and use knowledgeable early readers to catch them.

## Signs the curve is unmanaged

- Many proper names or jargon terms in the first pages. A couple can set tone. Many make eyes glaze.
- The world is shown off rather than used. A prologue that begins at the dawn of time with a magic is the standard example.
- Early readers cannot say what questions they have, which suggests the world is too thin or too opaque.

## Delivering exposition

1. **Put a character with a problem in front of the mechanism.** A student who is dyslexic and writes magical spells in a system where mistakes in the written word matter shows the rules and earns sympathy at once.
2. **Convey the world through the character's eyes.** The reader should feel they are getting character and plot while the world arrives as a side effect.
3. **Make history matter to someone.** Where the character's attention goes, the reader's follows. If a piece of lore is not relevant to the character yet, hint at it with a brush-off and return later, or cut it.
4. **Make exposition visual or active** when you can, and pay it off soon so readers learn that attending is rewarded.
5. **Do not hide essentials in epigraphs or prologues.** Some readers skip them, and material not tied to a moment is forgotten easily.

## Checks

- `worldbuilding.curve.01` Count the new proper names and terms in the first chapter. Which could wait?
- `worldbuilding.curve.02` For each paragraph of explanation, who is thinking it, and why now?
- `worldbuilding.curve.03` Is the learning curve steep on purpose, and does the opening promise that?
