# Character Arc Types

An arc is a shape that a character's change follows. Knowing a handful of common shapes lets you pick one on purpose, see which one a draft is drifting into, and check that it has the parts that make it work. Keep a personal list of arcs you notice in books and films: what defines each, what makes it work, and which ones you like. The four below are a starting set.

## The four shapes

**1. Apprentice arc.** One character follows a master. The interest is in where the apprentice diverges: what each can do that the other cannot, and how the same skill is approached differently. It is also a way to separate two characters who have similar skills.
- Example: two sword-trained siblings, one taught to hold a line and one taught to break it. The apprentice's first real fight shows the master's method failing, and the reader sees the difference in how they fight.
- Plan it: list the shared skill, then write down one place the apprentice will choose differently and one thing the master cannot do.

**2. Flaw-to-fix arc.** The classic. Show early that the character is bad at something, such as pride, and show how it harms their life. Then they learn.
- A flaw usually has an upside, since hubris can look like self-confidence. Before the lesson, establish what the flaw costs them in opportunities, growth, or relationships, so the reader wants it fixed.
- Plan it: write the flaw, its upside, three concrete costs shown on the page, and the scene where the cost is largest.

**3. Upward and downward versions.** These share the flaw-to-fix setup and differ at the decisive moment.
- Upward: the character learns the lesson, reaches a decision point, and we see why they choose differently this time.
- Downward: the character learns the lesson and still refuses to apply it at the decisive moment. They know the flaw and hold on anyway.
- Plan it: write the decision point as a single choice, then write the reason the character gives themselves. That reason is the heart of the arc.

**4. Ignorance to knowledge arc.** A mystery arc told through the character, who learns about the world and about themselves. They need to change in order to use what they learn.
- Example: a detective finally learns there are guilty people they can know are guilty and never prove it. The fact is easy to state; the arc is what they must become to live with it.
- Plan it: list the pieces of information, and for each one name the change in the character that it demands.

## Checks

- `character.arc.01` Does the reader see the flaw hurting the character before the lesson arrives?
- `character.arc.02` Is the decisive choice explained by who the character has become, not by how many times they have tried?
- `character.arc.03` In a downward arc, does the character know the flaw at the key moment? Constant arrogance that simply causes failure is a different, weaker shape.
- `character.arc.04` Can you name which of the four shapes each major character is on, or say that they have none?

## Cautions and exceptions

- Avoid the pattern of failing three times and then succeeding. The satisfying part is the why: who the character has become and why this decision is believable.
- A character who does not change much, or who changes only a little each book, follows different rules. See `character-iconic-characters`.
- Arcs are easier to believe when the character is also active and likable. See `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
- How a flaw is shown to the reader on first meeting is covered in `character-presenting-a-character`.
- An arc can be the progress thread of a whole story. See `plot-types-of-progress`.

## Where other advice differs

General craft advice treats character change as gradual. It wants small shifts visible across many scenes and welcomes backsliding and partial breakthroughs that do not stick. This skill puts the weight on one decisive choice and warns against a run of failed tries followed by success. Default to this skill: build the arc around the decision point and the reason behind it, with the flaw's costs shown beforehand. Choose the other side in a long novel or series where a single decision would feel sudden, or where realism about relapse is the point. Then show the incremental movement and the slides back, and keep the final choice explained by who the character has become.
