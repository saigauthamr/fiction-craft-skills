# Sanderson's Second Law

Limitations are more interesting than powers. Instead of hunting for ever more original abilities, invest in the constraints. Constraints force strategy, and characters are most interesting at the places where they are weak.

## Checks

- `magic.second.01` List what the magic can do, what it costs the user, and what stops it, including who or what is stronger.
- `magic.second.02` If the first list is long and the others short, does the magic solve problems too easily?
- `magic.second.03` Do the most interesting scenes come from the costs and the limits?
- `magic.second.04` Is each power described with rules specific enough to make a tactical puzzle, not a generic effect?

A power described as "pushes metal" is generic. The same power with rules (it pushes directly away, pulls directly toward, and heavier things move less) becomes a tactical puzzle.

## Four kinds of constraint

- **Cost.** What using it takes: money, physical harm, years of study, a mystical price. The size of the cost shapes how often and how freely it is used.
- **Limitation.** What the magic can and cannot do. Characters work inside it and are not expected to get around it.
- **Flaw** (in the strict sense). Something the reader expects characters to fix or overcome, such as magic that used to work and now fails.
- **Hindrance.** A line characters could cross but would prefer not to, leaving open whether they should.

A flaw can turn into a hindrance when characters find that fixing it would make things worse.

## Match constraints to people and theme

- Choose limits that press on your protagonist's personal weakness. Someone who cannot fly well is more interesting than someone who can.
- Connect the constraint to the theme. A desert hero's relationship to water, tied to a magic that runs on water, carries the theme into the system.
- The richest stories sit where a flawed character meets a flawed magic.
- The strongest heroes face problems bigger than themselves. Give the powerful figure opponents who outclass them, and give the burden to someone who is not the most powerful.

## Cautions and exceptions

- Resource costs behave like bullets in a gun: readers sense the moments when supplies run low. Let characters voice how low things are, or give an exact number when you want countdown tension.
- Too many flaws smother a system. See `magic-third-law`.
- Constraints still need to be visible to the reader before the climax. See `magic-first-law`.
