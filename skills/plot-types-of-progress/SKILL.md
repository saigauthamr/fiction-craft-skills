# Types of Progress

The writer controls progress and time completely: a time skip can take a sentence and a key moment can take fifty pages. The job is to give the reader a **satisfying sense of progression**, not to force events. Failing at this produces filler or a rushed ending that feels like a deus ex machina.

## Match the progress to the climax

Decide what kind of progress the story measures, and make the climax resolve that kind.

1. **Information progress** (mystery). The plot rests on someone lacking information and the ending is a reveal. Progress is clues found and suspects eliminated. Signpost it with moments like the detective saying they are close, while the reader gets the information too.
2. **Relationship progress** (buddy comedy, romance). Start with people clearly not getting along, show moments of connection and occasional setbacks, then show them complementing each other. The climax is where they come together.

There are many more kinds; these two are the worked examples.

## When there are several threads

- Identify the **soul of the story**: the progress thread that drives the ending. Usually pay all threads off together.
- The climax is won by the mechanism of the true plot, not necessarily the one the trappings suggest. In one example, the McGuffin chase is the A plot but the climax is about friendship.
- A longer story can hold more than one soul and resolve one arc before the third act.
- Do not confuse the trappings of the story with what readers actually think it is about.

## Pacing

- External-obstacle stories (thrillers) pace differently from internal-struggle stories. Match pacing to the story and to your natural style.
- Vary the pace with breathers; constant intensity loses impact.
- To skip travel, set a small promise early that you will time jump, then jump at a structural break, such as the end of a chapter or "three weeks later".

## Diagnosing a stall

- A page-turner understands progress and how to dole it out. A boring or confusing book usually lacks progress signposts.
- A common cause is dropping a promised line for a more interesting subplot.
- Ask: what does the reader think this sequence is moving toward, and what signals tell them so?

## Checks

- `plot.progress.01` Name the kind of progress the story measures (information, relationship, skill, distance, or another) and check that the climax resolves that kind.
- `plot.progress.02` With several threads, name the soul of the story, the thread that drives the ending, and check that the climax turns on it and not on the trappings.
- `plot.progress.03` For this sequence, what does the reader think it is moving toward, and what signals tell them so?
- `plot.progress.04` Does the pace vary, with breathers between pushes, and are time skips set up at a structural break?
