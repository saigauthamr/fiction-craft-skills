# The Three-Part Worldbuilding Split

Organize world notes into three documents. Each answers a different question, and sorting ideas this way shows quickly what you have not yet thought about.

## The three parts

1. **Physical setting.** What would exist if no thinking beings had ever lived here? Prompts: geography and topography, geology, weather and climate, flora and fauna (food chains, biomes, adaptations), disease and viruses, natural disasters, astronomy and cosmology, rotation, tides, or a tidally locked planet. Domestication of plants and animals sits on the border with culture.
2. **Cultural setting.** Everything thinking beings create, and how they think and interact. Prompts: technology level and gaps between cultures, religion, food and food customs, social manners and taboos, law and government, languages and dialects, social roles such as gender and class, relationship to the natural world, traditions, education, fashion, building methods and materials, festivals.
3. **Magic.** Its own section because it touches both of the others. It acts on the physical world and shapes culture. The rules for designing it belong to a separate set of skills.

## How to use it

- Brainstorm a list under each heading and mark the items that your characters will run into.
- Follow chains of consequence between parts. Food customs shape social manners, available materials shape architecture, and architecture reflects technology level. One change in the physical setting often ripples through several cultural items.
- Look for the surprising item. A small custom, such as a gesture that is polite in one culture and a grave insult in another, can open a whole set of taboos you would not have invented from the top down. Real cultures are a good source for this.
- Check how living things interact. A believable ecosystem where creatures eat, avoid, or depend on each other makes a setting feel real.

## Checks

- `worldbuilding.split.01` Does each part (physical setting, cultural setting, magic) hold the items your characters will actually run into?
- `worldbuilding.split.02` Does a change in one part ripple through the others, such as food customs into manners or materials into architecture?
- `worldbuilding.split.03` Do the chosen items make each other more interesting, with everything else left vague on purpose?

## Cautions and exceptions

- The lists are long on purpose. Their size shows how overwhelming the possibilities are, which is the cause of the trap described in `worldbuilding-worldbuilders-disease`. Do not try to fill every prompt.
- Choose the items that influence your characters' decisions and the problems they face, and let the rest stay vague; see `worldbuilding-hollow-iceberg`.
- Pick items that make each other more interesting. A river that floods yearly matters more once a festival, a law, and a trade route depend on it.
- To keep the build tied to the story, begin from the ideas you combined with `worldbuilding-idea-intersection`.
