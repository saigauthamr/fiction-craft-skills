# Scale of Plot

The scope of a story should fit its length and the way it will be read. A plot too large for the page count feels rushed, and a plot too small drags.

## Steps

1. Pick the format: short story, novella, or novel.
2. Estimate the length, either from an outline or from your own sense of how the story grows.
3. Match the scope of the conflict to that length using the guides below.
4. Plan the chapters for the way the book will be read.

## Rules of thumb

- **Short fiction** can follow one person through a single problem, or cover a whole life quickly in a novella. A Christmas Carol is a novella.
- **Around 80,000 to 100,000 words** suits thrillers and romances. A reader can finish one in a sitting or two, so pace the book to pull them through.
- **Above about 120,000 words**, readers expect scope to escalate. Harry Potter grows from school grades to saving the world as the books lengthen. A long book can also gain scope by spanning generations or a whole life.
- **Above about 150,000 words**, the story needs heavier weight to hold attention. Dune runs about 170,000 to 180,000 words and Lord of the Rings about 150,000 per volume.
- **Epic length** is read over days or weeks, so plot for natural break points where a reader can stop and return.

## Chapter length

Short chapters pull readers along faster. Long chapters that work as complete units suit a book read in sessions. Shortening chapters in a climax speeds the reader through it.

## Checks

- `plot.scale.01` Does the central conflict fit the word count for the format and genre?
- `plot.scale.02` If the book is long, where does the scope widen, and is the widening signposted?
- `plot.scale.03` Where can a reader put the book down, and what pulls them back?

If you cannot judge length yet, write the story and note how the finished count compares to what you expected. The sense improves with practice.

## Cautions

These are figures from one author's experience and genre habits. Treat them as starting points. A long book with no escalation of scope tends to run out of momentum, which is the same problem `plot-yes-but-no-and` has when stretched. Use the outline and `plot-big-p-little-p` to see whether the large-scale and small-scale plots each have room to grow.
