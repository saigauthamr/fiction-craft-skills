# Plot Archetypes and Arcs

Most stories are built from a few recognizable plot types, each of which can serve as an arc: relationship, romance, mystery or information, heist, master and apprentice, revolution, cat and mouse. A novel usually layers several. Mistborn, for example, interlocks a heist, an apprenticeship, a mystery, and a romance.

## Choosing and combining

1. List the archetypes your story could use. Study many stories you like, distill them into types and subtypes, and note which ones excite you.
2. Decide which archetype carries the big P Plot and which ones belong to character arcs and other little p plots. See `plot-big-p-little-p`.
3. Try combinations, including swapping the genre of a familiar plot. Expect some failed tries before a combination works; the book that became Mistborn was a second attempt.
4. Choose the subtype that fits your intended feel. Two heist shapes are common. In one, the plan is thrown off by a disruption and the crew reuses what they built to recover. In the other, the plan runs brilliantly with small complications, and tension comes from one member who does not know the full plan.

## Shifting archetypes midway

A strong move is to change the dominant archetype when things go wrong and the scope grows. A heist can widen into a revolution. A whodunit can turn into cat and mouse, as in Knives Out, and The Fugitive runs cat and mouse over an underlying whodunit. Plan the shift so that the earlier archetype's setup is reused instead of dropped. Signal the change early enough that readers who came for the first shape are not blindsided.

## Every arc can go wrong

Each archetype has a failure version. An information arc can end with the characters reaching the wrong conclusion. A relationship arc can sour. Decide deliberately whether an arc is headed for success or failure, and promise accordingly.

## The betrayal arc

A betrayal is a relationship plot built to fail, and it needs the same care as a good twist. It should be surprising and also feel inevitable.

1. Show early that the two characters work well together. You may skip the getting-to-know-you phase and start them as established friends.
2. Let their motivations drift apart gradually. Show friction through actions and choices while the protagonist's attention still treats the friend as a good friend.
3. Give the eventual turn a solid motivation, so a reader can think "I should have seen that."

Readers follow the viewpoint character's attention. If the protagonist trusts the friend, readers will tend to wave away the cracks, so the cracks can be planted in plain sight.

Check: list the three or four moments where the betrayer's goal visibly diverges from the protagonist's. Would each read as innocent on a first pass and damning on a second?

## Cautions and exceptions

- Archetypes are one way to build a story, not a requirement.
- A pure single-archetype plot can work, but a mid-book shift of type is what makes some stories stand out.
- Do not shift archetype without foreshadowing. A promised quest that abruptly becomes something else can lose both the readers who wanted the quest and the readers who would have liked the new shape.
- For how to schedule multiple archetype arcs across a book, use `plot-outline-interwoven-cycles`. For the relationship arc as a kind of progress, see `plot-types-of-progress`.
