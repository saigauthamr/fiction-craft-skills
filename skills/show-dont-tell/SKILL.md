# Show, Don't Tell

Showing a quality through action is usually more memorable than stating it. Showing also costs more words. The rule has two levels.

## Apprentice level: show

If you are learning, default to showing. Instead of "he was a nervous type," have him tap his toes through the whole meeting. Instead of telling readers a frog is dangerous, let it explode when someone pokes it in the opening scene. This builds the habit of finding the concrete action behind a label, and it lets readers draw the conclusion themselves.

## Journeyman level: choose

An experienced writer decides case by case. Ask three questions.

1. **How much does this matter to the story?** A trait that shapes the plot or the character arc earns a dramatized moment. A passing detail does not.
2. **What kind of scene is this?** In a tense scene, spend words on the action. In a transition, a sentence of summary is fine.
3. **How important is the character?** A one-time character does not get paragraphs of demonstration. A line of telling is enough.

## When telling is the better tool

- Summary for long spans. A school term can pass in a sentence.
- A fact that is awkward to dramatize. Having a character notice out loud that she looks different from everyone around her is often clumsier than stating it plainly.
- Minor traits where the cost of a scene outweighs the gain.

## When showing earns its words

- Traits that matter over time and could be misread if stated once, such as a way of thinking that readers should come to understand through repeated behavior.
- The first appearance of a character or element you want readers to remember.

## Checks

- `scene.show.01` For each stated trait or emotion, does it matter enough to the story to be shown? If yes, find one specific action or detail; if no, leave the plain statement.
- `scene.show.02` Is a minor point being proved at length, such as a passing trait or a one-time character given a dramatized scene?
- `scene.show.03` Is the first appearance of anything the reader must remember shown, not only stated?

## Where other advice differs

Many craft guides treat a stated emotion or trait as a label to be replaced by behavior, almost without exception, with summary allowed mainly for logistics and transitions. This approach goes further toward telling: stating a trait is fine whenever a dramatized version costs more than the trait is worth, including for a minor trait of a minor character.

Default: use this approach to decide how much of the story's word budget a trait deserves, since that is a question of structure and character importance. When you have decided a moment deserves to be shown, or when you are editing a sentence that reads as a flat label, follow the stricter line-level advice: replace the label with a specific action, reaction, or thought. If a draft is short on words or the trait is minor, keep the plain statement.
