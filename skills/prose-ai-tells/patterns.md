# Patterns

Numbered strongest first. Patterns 1 to 5 and 13 to 15 justify an edit on one sighting. A pattern marked *weak alone* needs company from other tells in the same passage before you act. Every example below is invented for this library.

## A. Staging instead of stating

The strongest tells. They sound significant and add nothing.

### 1. Not X but Y

**Watch for:** it wasn't X, it was Y; not X but Y; not just X, but Y; the same contrast split over two sentences ("It wasn't fear. It was something older."); a tail like "..., not out of anger, but out of tiredness".
**Problem:** The negative half names something nobody claimed, so the positive half sounds larger. State the point directly.
**Before:**
> It wasn't anger she felt, but something quieter. It wasn't the storm that frightened her; it was the stillness after.
**After:**
> The stillness after the storm frightened her more than the storm had.
**Keep when:** a character corrects a belief that was actually stated ("I didn't come for the money." "Then what?"), or both halves carry information.

### 2. Closers that explain the beat

**Watch for:** a final line that says what the moment meant ("It was the end of something, and the beginning of something else."); "In that moment, she understood"; "And that was enough"; a row of fragments that repeat a point ("No warning. No sound. No mercy.").
**Problem:** The line asks the reader to pause on a meaning instead of adding a fact. A short line can land a beat when it carries something new.
**Before:**
> She folded the letter and put it in the drawer. It was the end of something, and the beginning of something else.
**After:**
> She folded the letter and put it in the drawer.
**Keep when:** the short line adds a fact or turns the scene ("The door was already open.").

### 3. Aphorisms in the narration

**Watch for:** "Silence, he understood, was the loudest answer"; "Hope is a dangerous thing"; X is the Y of Z; a general truth in place of this moment.
**Problem:** A saying dresses an ordinary point as wisdom. Replace it with what happens.
**Before:**
> Silence, he understood, was the loudest kind of answer.
**After:**
> He waited. She gave him nothing, and the clock went on ticking.
**Keep when:** a character says it, in voice, or the book's narrator uses sayings sparingly as part of an established style.

### 4. Arguing with no one

**Watch for:** "She was not the kind of woman who..."; "It wasn't that she didn't care"; a narrator rejecting a reading nobody offered.
**Problem:** The text defends against an objection that appears nowhere else.
**Before:**
> Ines did not run. She was not the kind of woman who ran. She walked, deliberately, toward the pier.
**After:**
> Ines walked toward the pier.

### 5. Forced triads

**Watch for:** three adjectives, three nouns, three parallel actions or beats, repeated throughout a passage.
**Problem:** Ideas arrive in threes to sound complete, whether the scene has three parts or not. Check that each item adds something. Keep three when there are three.
**Before:**
> The market was loud, crowded, and alive. She smelled fish, smoke, and rain.
**After:**
> The market was loud. She smelled fish and rain.
**Keep when:** three is a real count, or the rhythm is deliberate and rare.

## B. Rhythm by rule

### 6. Repeated sentence openings

**Watch for:** several sentences in a row starting with the same subject, usually *she* or *he*.
**Problem:** Repetition handled by habit instead of by ear. Merge sentences, change the subject, or begin with the action. Do not ban the word. Repeating an opening on purpose for rhythm is a device.
**Before:**
> She opened the door. She stepped inside. She looked at the table. She saw the key.
**After:**
> She opened the door and stepped inside. The key lay on the table.

### 7. Dashes as the universal connector (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** em dashes or spaced hyphens joining clauses throughout a passage.
**Problem:** A dash lets the writer skip choosing how two clauses relate. One dash is ordinary; a page full of them is not. Replace most with a comma, period, colon, or parentheses.
**Before:**
> The tide, which had been rising all night, finally turned. The guild — or what remained of it — gathered at the quay — silent, watching.
**After:**
> The tide, which had been rising all night, finally turned. The guild, or what remained of it, gathered silently at the quay.
**Keep when:** it marks an interruption in dialogue ("But I—"), or the writer's own sample uses dashes.

### 8. Stacked hedges (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** seemed, almost, somehow, a kind of, something like, as though, a sort of, piled on one perception.
**Problem:** Hedges are added until nothing in the scene is stated. Keep one when the point-of-view character truly cannot tell.
**Before:**
> It seemed almost as though the water was somehow moving of its own accord, in a way that felt vaguely wrong.
**After:**
> The water was moving by itself.

## C. Inflation

### 9. Narrated significance

**Watch for:** "little did she know"; "a moment that would change everything"; "she would remember this for the rest of her days"; "everything was about to change".
**Problem:** The narration announces importance instead of earning it. Show the consequence, or cut.
**Before:**
> Little did she know, this single decision would change the course of her life forever.
**After:**
> (Cut. Let the next scene show what the decision cost.)

### 10. Reaction riders

**Watch for:** an -ing phrase or appended clause that explains an action: "..., his silence speaking volumes"; "..., a smile that carried the weight of everything unsaid".
**Problem:** The rider tells the reader what to feel about a simple action. Keep the action.
**Before:**
> He shrugged, his indifference speaking volumes about how little he cared.
**After:**
> He shrugged.

### 11. Stock AI words (*weak alone, strong in groups*)

**Watch for:** tapestry, testament, symphony (of), dance (of), palpable, kaleidoscope, vibrant, nestled, delve, meticulous, quietly or softly used as an emotion marker, a sense of, a mix of X and Y.
**Problem:** Models use these far more than people do, especially together. A single use in the right place is fine.
**Before:**
> The village was a tapestry of colors, a testament to generations of craft.
**After:**
> The houses were painted blue and ochre, each one patched by the family before it.

## D. Leftovers

### 12. Chatbot wrapper

**Watch for:** "Here's the scene you asked for:"; "Certainly!"; "I hope this captures..."; "Would you like me to continue?"; a note after the prose about what was changed.
**Problem:** A drafting assistant's wrapper remains around delivered prose. Remove it and keep the prose.
**Before:**
> Here's a revised version of the scene! I've tightened the dialogue. Would you like me to continue?
**After:**
> (The scene alone.)

## E. Fiction habits

Habits that show up in machine-written scenes in particular. Patterns 13 to 15 act on one sighting. Patterns 16 to 20 need company.

### 13. Emotion named right after it is shown

**Watch for:** a gesture or reaction followed by a sentence that names the feeling ("Her hands shook. She was terrified.").
**Problem:** The second sentence repeats what the first already showed.
**Before:**
> Dev's hands shook as he opened the letter. He was terrified.
**After:**
> Dev's hands shook as he opened the letter.
**Keep when:** the narration adds a cause or a contradiction the gesture does not show ("His hands shook, though not from fear.").

### 14. "A flicker of" and "a mix of" emotions

**Watch for:** a flicker, flash, ghost, hint, or trace of an emotion or a smile; a mix, mixture, or cocktail of two feelings; "something like" a feeling.
**Problem:** The feeling arrives hedged and pre-packaged. Pick the specific thing the character does or notices.
**Before:**
> A flicker of hope crossed his face, mixed with a hint of fear.
**After:**
> He glanced at the door and did not ask her to stay.

### 15. Dialogue that states the subtext

**Watch for:** a character says outright what they feel or what the scene means; characters tell each other what both already know ("As you know, our father died").
**Problem:** The speech does the reader's work for them. Let the line carry the feeling without naming it.
**Before:**
> "I feel betrayed," his sister said, "because you promised you'd stay and you didn't."
**After:**
> "You said you'd stay."
**Keep when:** the character is the sort who says such things, or a plain statement is the event of the scene.

### 16. Stock body reactions (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** a breath she didn't know she was holding; a heart pounding or hammering; a shiver down the spine; white knuckles; a clenched jaw.
**Problem:** Each is common in published fiction too. A passage that leans on several is running on defaults.
**Before:**
> He let out a breath he didn't know he was holding, a shiver running down his spine.
**After:**
> He kept his eyes on the stairs and stopped breathing until the footsteps passed.

### 17. Stock atmosphere lines (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** the air thick with tension; silence stretching; "somewhere, a clock ticked"; the smell of ozone or petrichor.
**Problem:** Generic mood laid over the scene instead of a detail that belongs to this place.
**Before:**
> The air was thick with tension, and somewhere, a clock ticked.
**After:**
> The stove ticked as it cooled. Nobody reached for the kettle.

### 18. Tag and gesture filler (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** "said softly", "whispered", "voice barely above a whisper" repeated across a page; nodded, smiled, sighed between every pair of lines.
**Problem:** Beats that fill pauses without adding character. Cut them, or replace them with an action that belongs to the scene.

### 19. Stacked similes (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** two or more "like a..." or "as if..." comparisons in one paragraph.
**Problem:** Comparisons crowd each other and none lands. Keep the best one.

### 20. One register for every speaker (*weak alone*; whole-chapter pass only)

**Watch for:** every character speaks in the same polished, complete sentences of similar length and vocabulary.
**Problem:** Voices are interchangeable. Only judge this across a full chapter, never from a short passage.

### 21. Lines that answer each other in tidy pairs

**Watch for:** each line of an argument answers the one before it exactly, often reusing its wording ("The ledger is the guild's." / "The guild signed the ledger."); every question gets a direct answer; nobody interrupts, dodges, or leaves a line unanswered.
**Problem:** Real arguments slide past each other. People answer the question they wish they had been asked, change the subject, or go quiet. Tidy call and response reads as staged.
**Before:**
> "You moved the marker," Ines said.
> "The marker was already moving," Tomas said.
> "Markers do not move on their own."
> "This one did."
**After:**
> "You moved the marker."
> Tomas went on coiling the rope. "Tide's turning. You want a ride back or not?"
> "Tomas."
> "It's a long swim."
**Keep when:** the characters are trading lines on purpose, as banter, ritual, or a duel of wits, and the scene is built on that rhythm.

### 22. A cornered character explains their position in a tidy speech

**Watch for:** a character caught out, accused, or losing an argument responds with a complete, reasoned paragraph that lays out how things work and why they were right; the explanation reads as a briefing for the reader.
**Problem:** People under pressure deflect, appeal to loyalty or status, ask who told you, offer food, or attack. A clean explanation from the losing side is exposition wearing a character.
**Before:**
> "Every survey crew rounds the counts," the clerk said. "The guild knows it, the harbor knows it, and the ships expect it. A rounded count is a courtesy, not a lie, and no inspector has ever objected."
**After:**
> The clerk laughed, short. "Who's been showing you the counts?"
> "Nobody."
> "Your father know you're in the office this late?"
**Keep when:** the character is the kind who lectures when cornered, and the speech itself is the characterization.
