# Proactivity Toolkit

Readers bond with the character who makes the story happen and get frustrated with one who stalls it. Opening on a character taking action is one of the quickest ways to make them readable.

## Start with a want

- Give the character a want from the first scene, even a small one such as a cup of coffee. The want is what turns events into actions.
- A wooden or lifeless character is more often a motivation problem than a dialogue problem. Check whether the reader has been shown what the character wants and how their actions follow from it before rewriting lines.
- Give each character several wants, some of them in tension. Let those wants drive choices, so that when a desire shifts the shift makes sense.
- When a motivation changes, establish the new one on the page. A character who flips goals without setup breaks.
- Small wants can stand in for large ones. A hero who does not yet want to save the world can love their family, and that love makes the later choice believable.

## The reluctant hero

Heroes often have to be pushed into action. Plan where the push lands: commonly the end of the first act or the midpoint. Until then, plant a seed of initiative. The character solves a minor problem cleverly, or turns back to help one stranger. That is enough to mark them as someone who acts.

## When the villain is more active than the hero

Readers will bond with the villain if the villain drives events while the hero reacts. Raise the hero's proactivity with the techniques in this skill, give them their own plan, and let their choices cause the villain's setbacks. See `character-villain-and-antihero-patterns`.

## Restricted characters

- **External limits** such as prison: show them trying something, planning, or scheming. They can work up to bigger action over time.
- **Internal limits** such as depression are the hardest. Stasis is what fails, so give the state motion, even downward motion. Or accept that proactivity is unavailable and lean on the other two scales. Or give the character control over a small sphere where their choices matter.

## Restarting a stalled character

- Cut to another viewpoint who carries the action or drags the stalled one along.
- Save the last bit of growth for the end of the second act. Something the character has learned returns and prompts a final push into the third.

## Pairing proactive characters

Two characters with competing agendas make tension, especially when one is more capable and the audience suspects the other is right. In a cat-and-mouse chase both leads act, and the reader follows both.

## Checks

- `character.proactive.01` What does the character want in their first scene, and does an action follow from it?
- `character.proactive.02` If the character reads as wooden, has the reader been shown what they want before the dialogue is blamed?
- `character.proactive.03` For a reluctant hero, where does the push land, and what small act of initiative comes before it?
- `character.proactive.04` Is the villain driving events while the hero only reacts?
- `character.proactive.05` When a motivation changes, is the new one established on the page?

## Cautions and exceptions

- A deliberately cruel choice still counts as proactive unless fear has frozen the character. Letting someone drown is an action. It lowers relatability instead, so check `character-relatability-toolkit`.
- A character can be proactive in one domain and passive in another. See `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
- To find what a character wants, see `character-motivation-discovery-questions`.
- Not every story needs a driving lead. Slow-progress stories can run on quieter engines.
