# Outlining with Interwoven Plot Cycles

A plot cycle is one thread with its own promise, progress, and payoff. A book is several cycles woven together. The outline is a tool for tracking all those promises and their progress in one place.

## The method

1. **Identify the cycles.** Usually one internal arc per major character, plus the main external plot. A big book may have one main plot and two sub-main plots. Add any character-interaction plot, such as a romance, and any information plot, such as how the magic works. An apprenticeship is also a cycle, for example a character who must master a skill without a teacher.
2. **List each cycle's beats.** Start at the end, with the culminating payoff for that cycle, then work backward. Add signposts that show progress, backslides, and moments where it looks doomed.
3. **Fold the lists together.** Merge them into sections or parts. Mark the end of a part with a big complication or a major achievement.
4. **Order them as a long bullet list.** Use plot headings and keep the list unnumbered at first. Turn it into chapters while you write.

## Checks

- `plot.cycles.01` Does every cycle have a payoff, with visible progress between its promise and its payoff?
- `plot.cycles.02` Do the big payoffs land one after another, not all at once?
- `plot.cycles.03` Is any minor character's cycle better resolved earlier, so it does not compete with the lead at the climax?
- `plot.cycles.04` Is every promised thread present in the merged outline?

## Complexity and logistics

- Each added viewpoint multiplies the work. One viewpoint over a long book is easy. Two is hard. Two viewpoints in the same plot is manageable, while two on different worlds compounds the difficulty far more than doubling it.
- If armies or travelers move, keep a map or a distance sense so they do not teleport, unless established magic accounts for logistics.
- Use whatever tool suits you, such as a word processor with a navigation pane, a notebook, or dedicated writing software.

## For outline-first writers

Do the full pass before drafting. Treat the list as a map that can change. Expect the first merge of cycles to be wrong, and revise it as you learn what the draft needs.

## For discovery writers

You can still use the method. Write a draft or a section first, then list the cycles you actually wrote, work out their beats, and see which promises have no progress or payoff. This is also the stage to add setup and foreshadowing, which is easier to add later than to remove. Another option is a short, time-limited planning pass that identifies only the cycles and their endings, leaving the middle open.

New writers can try several approaches, including improvising a whole book, then settle on a mix that works for them. A writer who has a working method does not need to change it.

## Cautions

- Getting the convergence of several arcs right may take more than one draft.
- The outline is for tracking, not for locking in. A cycle that turns out dull can be reshaped or cut.
