# Kinds of Promises

A story makes several distinct promises at the start, and not all of them are obvious. Check each one against what the book actually delivers.

## The kinds

1. **Tone promise.** What feel to expect. It comes from opening events, descriptions, names, and prose style: an early joke for a comedy, a shocking death for grimdark. A prologue can carry the tone promise when the plot starts slowly, though the device is close to a cliche and is harder to make stand out.
2. **Plot promise.** The opening offers a small picture or metaphor of the main conflict. If the real conflict arrives late, lean on tone and character first, then bring in a hard problem.
3. **Character promise.** Show what the character wants, what they need, and why they cannot have it. Attached flaws and limits signal where the character will grow or fail.
4. **Series promise.** A structural or packaging promise between books: a stated number of books, a trilogy label, a named line of adventures, a promised ending.
5. **Ongoing promises.** Each new character, plot sequence, and chapter makes its own smaller promise.

## A chapter-level tool

Strong chapters often open with a "stealth thesis paragraph" that quietly says what the chapter is about, and close with a stinger that signals it was delivered.

## Checks

- `plot.promises.01` Which kinds of promise does the opening make (tone, plot, character, series), and which does it skip? Not every kind is required; mark each skip as deliberate or accidental.
- `plot.promises.02` From the first pages, could a reader say what feel to expect and what the main character wants?
- `plot.promises.03` Does the character promise show what the character wants, what they need, and why they cannot have it yet?
- `plot.promises.04` If the tone will switch (for example from thriller to travelogue), is the second tone promised at the start?
- `plot.promises.05` Does each new character, sequence, or chapter make its own small promise, and does the chapter signal it was delivered?

## Length and context

- Short stories must hit their promise harder and sooner, often in the first paragraph. There is no room for a prologue.
- Readers judge how long to give a book from its length. Established authors can trade on their name; newer writers cannot rely on that.
- First lines matter less than the first few pages and first chapter, though a good first line still helps.
- New promises can be made mid-story; the answer is "yes and yes, asterisk", depending on length and twist type.
- Promises are easier to write once the ending is known, so it is normal to revise the opening last.
