# Presenting a Character

Three linked things tell a reader who a character is. Motivation is what they want. Personality is how they go about getting it. Values are what they will do or give up to get it. Convey all three, and keep them connected so each explains the others.

## The three parts

1. **Motivation first.** It is the most important and usually grows out of what the story needs. Find it with `character-motivation-discovery-questions`.
2. **Personality as the surface over the motivation.** Ask how this person pursues the goal. Do they push, wait, hint, or never mention it? How do they show care: by looking after people, or by writing a check? Two characters with the same want should go after it differently.
3. **Values as the price.** Ask what they will sacrifice. A change in values is also what drives an arc, up or down, since a character who acquires a new value replaces the old motive.

## Showing it on the page

- Personality comes through dialogue and the first actions a character takes. Early choices set expectations.
- Do not let characters state their own motives to people who already know them. A butler telling the maid what both already know is the warning sign.
- Dialect and verbal habits work in small doses, and they should hint at something deeper about the person.
- To show intelligence, skip the stereotype of no contractions and rare words. Use precise word choice, references, interrupting, a clear structure of claim and evidence, and sentences that build.
- A quirk works best when it reinforces the core trait. A quirk that just sits on top feels tacked on.
- Use early scenes as promises about what the text treats as a flaw to overcome and what it treats as a trait to keep.

## Checks

- `character.presenting.01` Can you name the character's motivation, how their personality goes after it, and what they will give up for it?
- `character.presenting.02` Does any character state their own motive to someone who already knows it?
- `character.presenting.03` With dialogue tags and setting removed, could a reader tell who is speaking from the lines alone?
- `character.presenting.04` Does each quirk reinforce a core trait, instead of sitting on top of it?

A practice exercise: write a scene with three people whose wants conflict, remove the tags and setting, and rework wants and personalities until each speaker can be identified from their lines alone.

## Cautions and exceptions

- Every character begins as a stereotype or a mix of archetypes. Nuance arrives over the story, so do not demand full depth in the first scene.
- A main character can begin with a built-in contradiction. For side characters it is riskier, though it can be their defining trait.
- Outline plot and world heavily and characters lightly. Draft to find the personality and values, then rebuild the outline once you know them. Early versions that fail are normal.
- Background characters can be told apart by a visual signifier, a quirk that comes up naturally in conversation, or both. Give a quirk-defined character a viewpoint or a candid scene when you want them to grow past the type.
- In an ensemble, show each member reacting to the same event, and let the cast break from type over time.
- For characters who do not change, see `character-iconic-characters`.
