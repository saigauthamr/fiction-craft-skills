# Floating Outline for Dialogue Scenes

A floating outline is a short list of scene goals kept separate from the main outline. You write it just before the scene, then improvise the scene itself. All writing involves some discovery; the list gives that discovery a direction so the scene does not wander or only pass along facts.

Dialogue is usually a stronger place than narration to show what characters want, because people with different motives push against each other. Use both, but let the conversation carry the friction.

## Steps

1. **Write the purpose as one bullet.** Say what must happen and what gets in the way. Example: the general tries to recruit a neighboring king and is turned down because of who that king is.
2. **Ask what makes it interesting.** Add bullets for what the scene reveals about each speaker, any foreshadowing to plant, and what side characters present can show or react to.
3. **Freeform the scene.** Write it without checking the list line by line. The bullets are there to be remembered, and the dialogue can take surprising turns.
4. **Leave notes about small problems.** Put short bullets at the top of the scene for a later reread, or fix the problems immediately if they are quick.

## Checks

- `character.dialogue.01` Can you say in one sentence what each speaker wants from the other?
- `character.dialogue.02` Does the refusal, or the compromise, come from the character's own nature, so a different character would have answered differently?
- `character.dialogue.03` Does each reveal about a character come through what they say and how they push, not through the narrator explaining?
- `character.dialogue.04` Is there something here for a side character to do beyond listening?

## Cautions and exceptions

- Keep the list short. Its job is to point, and a long list turns improvisation back into a script.
- The list sits outside the main outline, so changing it does not mean the plot has changed.
- To choose which flaws and quirks to bring out, see `character-presenting-a-character`. For characters who stay fixed across a story and push against others, see `character-iconic-characters`.
- When the scene belongs to a character who drives events, see `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
