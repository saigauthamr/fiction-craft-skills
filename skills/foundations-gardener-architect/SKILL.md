# Gardener Versus Architect

Writers sit on a spectrum. An architect plans the structure first, like a house, then hangs the story on it. A gardener plants an idea, tends it as it grows, and sees what it becomes. This is also called outline writing versus discovery writing, or pantsing. Almost everyone lands somewhere between the ends, and strong writers disagree sharply: some insist on outlines, and some say outlines kill a story.

## Find your place

1. Try both ends on real projects. Outline one book thoroughly. Draft another with only a premise and a few anchor scenes.
2. Notice what happens to your energy. Some writers lose enthusiasm for a story once it is fully mapped, because the discovery was the fun. Others stall without a map.
3. Build a blended process from what you learn, such as a loose outline of the ending and key turns with the middle left open. Expect it to keep changing across your writing life.

## The typical risk at each end

- **Architects** tend to skip revision, because the next idea feels more exciting than polishing a book they already knew the shape of. Build revision in on purpose. See `foundations-revision-guide`.
- **Gardeners** tend to over-revise early chapters and never reach the end, since each pass reveals something new about the opening. Set a rule that you finish the draft before you rework the start.
- A gardener who discovers the story while writing will often need heavier structural revision afterward. That is the cost of the method, not a sign of failure.

## Gardeners and early feedback

A discovery writer's story is fragile while it is forming. Opinions from others can redirect it, and a book can drift from romance to vampires to thriller as different readers suggest trends. Hold off outside advice until the draft is done. See `foundations-writing-group-rules`.

## Checks

None. This skill is about the writer's process and mindset, not a check to run on a story.

## Cautions and exceptions

- A writer can be an outliner for one project and a discoverer for the next. A large cast changes the answer too. A book with many viewpoint characters may be easier to write in story arcs, one thread at a time, then splice the arcs together. That costs extra revision, because pacing breaks when the viewpoints are mixed. A book with only a few threads can usually be written straight through.
- An architect who is stuck on a blank middle and a gardener who has lost the plot are at opposite ends of the same problem. Borrow one tool from the other end instead of switching entirely.
