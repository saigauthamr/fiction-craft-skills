# Proactive, Relatable, Capable

Compelling characters are built from a mix of three attributes. Each is a sliding scale, and a character sits at some point on each one.

- **Proactive**: the character makes the plot move. This is the biggest single factor in whether a character is readable, because readers bond with whoever makes things happen.
- **Relatable**: the reader can step into the character's shoes and cares what happens to them. Without it the stakes drop, because nobody minds if the character dies.
- **Capable**: readers enjoy watching competent people do things competently.

Few characters max out all three, though some do. Details live in `character-proactivity-toolkit`, `character-relatability-toolkit`, and `character-capability-design`.

## Using the scales

1. Place your character on each scale at the start of the story, roughly low, middle, or high.
2. Use a rough rule of thumb, never applied rigidly: one scale high, one moderately low, one growing as the story goes. A character high on everything has nowhere to go.
3. Decide on purpose which scale is the place the character learns, which is already a strength, and which stays weak as a defining trait.
4. Remember that each scale has sub-areas. A person can be proactive about teaching and organizing a team and passive about saving the world in a video game. Place the character per domain when the answer matters.

A character can be strong on two scales and still work. The lead of a survival drama can be proactive and capable and among the least relatable people on the cast, and the story can still be built around him. It then needs the other scales or the supporting cast to carry the reader's warmth.

## Arcs as movement along the scales

An internal arc is a character ending at a different point on at least one scale.

- **Relatability rises** as the character works on a flaw and the reader cheers when it is overcome.
- **Capability rises** in apprentice stories, such as a farm boy learning the Force.
- **Proactivity rises** when the first act is about learning to act, or when the midpoint marks the turn from reacting to driving.
- A scale can also fall. A trusted helper who slides downward over a long series makes a painful, effective arc.

Purely internal arcs usually map onto these scales. External problems and relationship arcs are separate kinds and need their own handling.

## Checks

- `character.scales.01` Place the character low, middle, or high on proactive, relatable, and capable, per domain where it matters.
- `character.scales.02` Which scale is lowest? A passive character needs a want and a first action, a cold one a reason to be liked, an unimpressive one something they are visibly good at.
- `character.scales.03` Which scale does the character's arc move along, and is the movement shown on the page?
- `character.scales.04` Is any character high on all three scales with nowhere left to go?

## Cautions and exceptions

- Slow-progress stories can work. A quiet, drawing-room fantasy of manners does not need the drive of a spy thriller.
- Overpowered or divine characters are still read through these scales. Give them relatable wants and a clear limit, and use them sparingly as viewpoints. See `character-capability-design` and `character-flaws-restrictions-limitations`.
- No trait relates to every reader, so judge relatability against the readers you are writing for.
- Villains usually sit high on proactive and capable and low on relatable. See `character-villain-and-antihero-patterns`.
- For a first look at what makes a character memorable on the page, see `character-iconic-characters` and `character-presenting-a-character`.
