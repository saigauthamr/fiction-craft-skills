# Flaws, Restrictions, and Limitations

Strong characters have expertise and weaknesses in roughly equal measure. "Weakness" covers three different things, and mixing them up produces arcs that go nowhere or stories that cheat.

## The three kinds

1. **Flaw**: something the character needs to overcome. The arc is the overcoming. Examples are cowardice, pride, or an inability to trust.
2. **Restriction**: a constraint, imposed by the story or by the character, that they could get past but the audience does not want them to. A hero's moral code against killing is a restriction. Breaking it would be possible and would cost the reader something.
3. **Limitation**: something forced on the character against their will, often physical. A one-armed person who wants to play football has a limitation. The story is about working around it or living well despite it, and a cure would miss the point.

## Sorting a weakness

Ask three questions about each weakness you plan to give a character.

- Could the character change this by choosing to? If yes, it is a flaw or a restriction. If no, it is a limitation.
- Does the reader want it to change? If yes, it is a flaw and the arc should address it. If the reader wants it to hold, it is a restriction.
- Is the story about fixing it, bearing it, or testing it? Fixing points to a flaw, bearing points to a limitation, and testing points to a restriction.

Then match the handling. Flaws get an arc. Limitations get ingenuity and workarounds. Restrictions get pressure scenes where the easy way out is available and the character declines, or occasionally takes it. Some stories explore what happens once a restriction finally breaks.

## Using weakness to build relatability

Showing a flaw makes a character relatable early, because readers see something familiar in them. The effect decays. If the flaw blocks progress for too long without being addressed, it turns from an asset into a liability and readers lose patience. Plan when the flaw starts to move, and let the character show awareness of it or effort against it.

## Weakness for the very strong

An overpowered character becomes interesting through what they cannot do. Where raw capability would flatten the story, give such a figure a restriction or limitation that bears on what the plot demands. Pair it with a relatable want, as discussed in `character-capability-design`.

## Checks

- `character.weakness.01` For each weakness, is it a flaw, a restriction, or a limitation, and is it handled that way: an arc for a flaw, pressure scenes for a restriction, ingenuity for a limitation?
- `character.weakness.02` Is a limitation ever treated as a flaw the character should simply get over?
- `character.weakness.03` Does an unaddressed flaw block progress so long that readers lose patience?
- `character.weakness.04` For a very strong character, which restriction or limitation bears on what the plot demands?

## Cautions and exceptions

- Do not label a limitation a flaw. A character who "should just get over" a disability or an outside constraint reads as a lesson in blame.
- A trait that dominates the character and is repeated until it grates is a different problem. See `character-overused-trait-trap`.
- To see how flaws, restrictions, and limitations raise or lower each of the three scales, see `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
- Self-awareness helps with flaws. See `character-relatability-toolkit`.
