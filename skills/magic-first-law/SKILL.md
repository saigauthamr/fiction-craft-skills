# Sanderson's First Law

The author's ability to solve problems with magic in a satisfying way is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that magic. This is a plotting rule. The more the reader knows, the more the magic can be asked to do at a key moment.

## Checks

- `magic.first.01` Name the emotion the resolution should give, and ask whether the reader's understanding of the magic can support it.
- `magic.first.02` At the climax, could a reader have predicted the move from what they were shown? If they learn the rule only as it is used, expect a cheat reaction.
- `magic.first.03` If the magic is soft, does the resolution rest on what the characters do and want, not on the magic saving the day?
- `magic.first.04` If the magic is hard, were its pieces seeded earlier as tools that the characters now combine?

Soft magic can still frame a satisfying ending when the payoff is a character achievement unrelated to its rules. With hard magic, you may leave one step soft to leave room for later exploration.

## Making soft magic pay off

A soft power can be given a hard-structured payoff by promising a specific event. A mysterious wizard who announces he will return at dawn on a stated day gives the reader a firm expectation, and his arrival then lands as earned. A rescue by a similar power with no setup reads as a near deus ex machina.

## Seeding

- Authors usually under-seed. Add more clues than feels necessary, then trim if beta readers guess too much.
- Avoid info dumps. Give one small aspect of the magic per scene, keep other things happening in it, and build on it.
- Foreshadow without making characters look stupid by letting them hold the theory you want the reader to hold, then let that theory fail while a deeper layer remains.
- A red herring can help. Let readers settle on an expected explanation, then break it near the climax so they scramble with the characters.

## Cautions and exceptions

- Early in a story, a rescue by a newly arrived character is acceptable because its job is introduction. The same device in the final third reads as deus ex machina.
- Deliberately unsatisfying endings can serve a different emotion, provided you choose them on purpose.
- The reverse failure is arbitrary trouble from the author, where things get worse for no reason the reader could trace. Readers accept some, but too much shows the hand of the author. Prefer complications that come from choices, consequences, or clues that were present all along.
- A big event with no explanation can still satisfy if the real emotional payoff is elsewhere, such as a romance carrying a story that also has a vampire plot.
- See `magic-hard-and-soft` for placing your system, and `plot-promise-progress-payoff` for the broader setup and payoff structure.
