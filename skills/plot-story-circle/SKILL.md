# The Story Circle

The Story Circle is a simplified hero's journey. An ordinary person who expects life to stay the same is pushed into an adventure, is tested and changed, and comes home different, usually improving their society. Because the shape returns to its start, the change is visible: the character could not do something at the beginning and now has mastered it. That contrast is where much of the payoff comes from.

## The steps

1. **Rest.** The character is at the top of the circle, wanting more or content. A farm boy and a comfortable homebody both fit.
2. **The call and the refusal.** A need arrives that the character feels unable to meet, and they resist it.
3. **Crossing the threshold.** An event forces them in and removes the way back, such as the family dying.
4. **The road of trials.** They are tested and shown to lack skill or knowledge. This covers roughly the first half of the middle of the story.
5. **The meeting with the darkness.** A direct confrontation with something hard, often a parental figure or the character's own shadow.
6. **Transformation and price.** The character changes and pays for the gain, the "elixir" of knowledge or skill.
7. **Master of two worlds.** They command both the inner and the outer sides of the adventure.
8. **Return.** They go home changed and bring the gain back.

## Using it

- Run it on your own story and mark which steps are present. A missing step is a question to answer, not an error.
- Track the circle of the character's growth, which can differ from the external goal. Destroying the superweapon is the goal; the farm boy becoming a trained fighter is the arc the signposts should follow.
- Circles can nest. One book can be a full circle that is also only the first threshold of a larger arc, and a sequel can be a failure and a retry.
- The shape can turn tragic. The hero refuses the last step, refuses the elixir or the return, and fails or dies. A flaw that is never let go drives that version.

## Checks

- `plot.circle.01` Mark which steps of the circle are present. For each missing step, decide whether it is missing on purpose.
- `plot.circle.02` Name the character's growth circle separately from the external goal, and check that the signposts track the growth.
- `plot.circle.03` If a step stopped fitting the story, was it dropped instead of forced?
- `plot.circle.04` If the story ends tragically, which step does the character refuse, and what flaw drives the refusal?

## Cautions and exceptions

- The model is vague enough to fit almost any story, which is why it is hard to avoid and why it tells you little by itself. Dodging it on purpose usually still leaves "the character learns and gets better".
- The older monomyth version is criticized by folklorists for weak sourcing, unacknowledged borrowing, forcing every tale into one thesis, and ignoring women. The useful lesson is that a theory like this can try too hard.
- A famous film series shows the danger of a checklist: bending a character's origin to hit a monomyth beat made the story worse. If a step stops fitting what you are writing, drop the step.
- Neither following nor avoiding the model should be a goal. Tell the story you want, and accept shared ancestry with other works.
- For a first book, leaning on an established shape is fine. Characters and setting stretch it toward your own.
