# Scene and Sequel

Every scene that ends with a change is followed by a sequel: a beat where the characters respond to the new situation. Books alternate between the two. The alternation does not have to be rigid, and one chapter can hold both.

## The three steps

1. **Scene.** Rising action that reaches a conclusion. Something is won, lost, learned, or broken.
2. **Sequel.** Time for the characters to take in the result: grieve, argue, regroup, decide. The new status quo becomes real to them and to the reader.
3. **Spin up.** The decision or the pressure made in the sequel launches the next scene.

## Using it

- **Control pacing with response time.** A long, solid sequel slows the book and deepens character. A short sequel keeps the pace high. The amount of room characters get to respond is the dial.
- **Check a dragging stretch.** Are there several sequels in a row with no scene between them? Nothing is changing, so the response has nothing to respond to.
- **Check a numbing stretch.** Are there several scenes in a row with no sequel? The reader never gets to feel the consequences, so each event weighs less than the last.
- **Make the sequel carry something.** A sequel can hold a quiet revelation, a joke, a relationship beat, or a hook into the next scene. It is a place for the character arc, so it should not be filler.
- **Mix the sizes.** A fast adventure can run bursts of action separated by brief relaxation. A big epic can afford long sequels after major events.

## Checks

- `scene.sequel.01` After each scene that ends with a change, is there a sequel where the characters take in the result, sized to the pace you want?
- `scene.sequel.02` Is a dragging stretch a run of sequels with no scene between them?
- `scene.sequel.03` Is a numbing stretch a run of scenes with no sequel to feel the consequences?
- `scene.sequel.04` Does each sequel carry something (a revelation, a joke, a relationship beat), and does its decision launch the next scene?

## Cautions and exceptions

- Book length changes the right ratio. A short thriller that readers finish in one or two sittings can run very short sequels that end on the next explosion; this is much of what people mean by a page-turner.
- Tension has diminishing returns. Even a high-octane story usually benefits from one real slowdown near the middle or just before the end.
- Scene and sequel is a diagnostic. It does not require every scene to be followed by a labeled reaction section. Sometimes a single paragraph of response is enough.
- Use `plot-promise-progress-payoff` to decide what the scene itself should deliver.
