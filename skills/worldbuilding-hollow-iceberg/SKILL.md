# The Hollow Iceberg

The common advice says the reader sees the tip and the great bulk of worldbuilding lies below the surface. The risk is that a writer with a huge submerged mass wants to show it, and the result is info dumps. The alternative is a hollow iceberg. Build in depth only what you truly know and what informs your characters and their decisions. For everything else, suggest that something is there.

## Steps

1. **Research the part that matters.** Find what shapes character choices and plot, and do real work on it. This can happen before drafting or after, as long as you keep returning to the story.
2. **Evoke the rest.** Give one or two lines with a light hook, such as a passing reference to a distant war or a regional dish. Readers sense depth, and the hook can seed later books.
3. **Use one vivid detail.** Describe a single detail of a place in sharp terms and let readers fill in the rest. A room with bullet holes in the wall, a flickering light, and a dead ceiling fan implies a poor neighborhood without any explanation.
4. **Lean on what readers know.** Jump gates, faster-than-light travel, and dragons need no mechanics. Explaining how a dragon's wings could lift its mass invites boredom or arguments.
5. **Make long description earn its place.** If a paragraph is heavy on world detail, write it well enough that readers enjoy it.

## Checks

- `worldbuilding.iceberg.01` Is worldbuilding keeping you from drafting, measured against your own deadline?
- `worldbuilding.iceberg.02` Is each piece of world detail on the page there because it supports stakes, character, or plot, or because you invested time in it?
- `worldbuilding.iceberg.03` Is the world built deeply only where it shapes choices and plot, with a passing hook for everything else?

For drafting pace, about one normal-length book a year is a good target and one every two years is acceptable; past that, move time from building to writing. The symptom of detail that does not serve the story is paragraphs of explanation that do not match the storytelling and make readers ask why this matters.

## Too little worldbuilding

The sign is reader confusion. Early readers will tell you where they could not tell what questions to ask. Some confusion at the start is acceptable if dynamic storytelling carries readers until later realizations connect it.

## Cautions and exceptions

- There is no wrong way to write a book. A dense, worldbuilding-first opening can work, and it costs some readers while winning others. Step two of the test is not a failure if that density is the story you are telling.
- Extras can be valid. Songs and histories that delight you and your readers have a place when they match your goals.
- If explaining something does not move the plot, half the audience will want more and half will be bored, so decide on purpose.
