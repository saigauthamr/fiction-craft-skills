# Part 1. Setting up and moving through a story

Read these first. Onboarding decides who plans, drafts, and revises; the rest are the stages every story moves through, in order. Each stage adapts to those settings.

## 1. story-onboard

*Use to set up a story project, including "I'm not a writer; write it for me", or change stage ownership, craft language, or check-ins. Inspects existing material, offers presets, and shows the settings change for confirmation before writing.*

Sets up a Story project, or changes how one works, by asking a few questions and recording the answers in the project's instructions file. Every other story skill reads those answers. You never write anything until the writer confirms what you showed them.

#### Steps

1. **Look first.** Run the inspector script in the `story-next` skill folder on the project folder, with its full path; do not `cd`. Read the project's instructions file (`AGENTS.md`, `CLAUDE.md`). Note the `setup` line and any current settings.
2. **Say what you found** in three to five plain lines: is it empty, does it hold chapters or notes, does it have story facts already, and is it set up, and how.
3. **Existing material comes first.** If the inspector reports existing material, or an established knowledge base with no setup (`setup: none` and `established_kb: True`), the writer's files must be brought in before anything else. Say so in one line and hand off to `story-adopt`. Come back here afterward.
4. **Ask one section at a time**, recommended answer first, so the writer can accept with a number or "go". Skip any section the writer has already answered in what they said.
   - **How we split the work.** Offer the presets as numbered options, one plain line each:
     1. AI writes, you direct: you make the decisions, the AI plans, drafts, and revises.
     2. AI drafts, you revise: the AI plans and drafts, you rework the prose.
     3. You outline, AI drafts: you give the plan or beats, the AI writes the prose, you revise.
     4. AI outlines, you write: the AI lays out what each chapter and scene needs, you write it, the AI checks it.
     5. You write, AI checks: you do everything, the AI questions, coaches, and checks, and never writes your prose.
     6. Design only: the AI helps you design the world, cast, and plot, and there is no prose.

     Recommend from what the writer said: "I'm not a writer" points to 1, "I write" points to 5, "I just want to build the world" points to 6. If nothing points anywhere, recommend 5 and say the AI's role can grow later.
   - **Adjust a stage?** Only if the writer wants something between the presets. The three stages are planning, drafting, and revising. Drafting can also be traded scene by scene. If a combination can't work, the setup script says which rule it breaks: explain it in plain words and offer the nearest one that works. The two rules are: no prose means no drafting and no revising, and the AI can only revise prose it drafted.
   - **How much craft talk.** No craft terms, one-line explanations, or full terms. Recommend the preset's default: none when the AI writes, full when the writer does, brief otherwise.
   - **Check-ins.** Only when the AI drafts: every chapter, every three chapters (recommended), or every part of the book.
5. **Show before writing.** Run the setup script next to this file with its full path, the folder, the chosen settings, and `--dry-run`:

   `python3 scaffold.py <folder> --preset <name> [--plan X --draft X --revise X --craft-language X --check-in X] --dry-run`

   Tell the writer, in plain words, the settings line it prints, which folders it would create, and which file it would change. Offer to show the full block. If they want changes, go back to the section that changes.
6. **Write only after a yes.** Run the same command without `--dry-run`. Report what was created and changed in two or three lines.
7. **Name the first step** in one line, from the inspector's state: usually `story-develop` for a new idea, or the step `story-next` would recommend. Do not start it.

#### Changing how we work later

Run the same steps on a set-up project. State the current settings in one line, ask only what the writer wants to change, show the `--dry-run` diff, and write after a yes. Only the block changes. Story facts, plans, drafts, and every other line of the instructions file stay exactly as they are.

#### First-build setups

The inspector reports `v1-director` or `v1-standard` for a project set up by an earlier version of this library.

- `v1-director` becomes "AI writes, you direct". Say so and confirm.
- `v1-standard` becomes "You write, AI checks", unless the drafts look AI-drafted (the writer used the old drafting workflow, or says so). Then ask which preset fits.

To migrate with the same meaning, use `--keep` in place of the preset options; to migrate to a different preset, pass it as usual. Show the dry run first either way.

#### Problems the inspector reports

- `malformed`: say exactly what is wrong (the inspector's detail line). If the block has no end marker, the writer must fix it by hand; do not guess where it ends. Otherwise, rewriting the block with the chosen settings fixes it.
- `duplicate` or `conflicting`: explain that there is more than one block, and that `AGENTS.md` wins. With the writer's approval, re-run with `--repair`, which keeps one block and leaves a pointer in the other file.
- Exit code 3 ("existing project"): the folder has an established knowledge base and no setup. Hand off to `story-adopt`. Use `--force` only if the writer, after hearing why, insists.

#### Rules

- Nothing is written before the writer confirms what you showed them.
- One section at a time; at most one question per message unless the writer asked for all of them.
- Plain words. Do not teach craft here.
- Never touch `kb/`, `work/`, or any text outside the block. The project's own rules in its instructions file stay exactly as written, and they take priority over the defaults the block describes.

## 2. story-next

*Use when a writer asks what to do next, where to start, or says they are stuck. Inspects project state and ownership, recommends one available next step, and stops without starting it.*

Recommends one next step. The inspector works out the step from the project's files and settings; your job is to explain it in plain words and hand off. You never start the step yourself.

A specific request ("write the next scene", "check chapter 4") is not a question for this skill. It goes straight to the skill that does that job.

#### Steps

1. **Run the inspector** next to this file, with its full path and the project folder; do not `cd`. Read its `next_step`, `next_skill`, `next_owner`, and `next_reason` lines, and the settings lines.
2. **Ask only what the files can't tell you**, at most two or three short questions, and only if the answer would change the recommendation. For example, whether there is writing somewhere outside this folder. Most of the time, ask nothing.
3. **Recommend** in this shape, using the craft language the settings name:

   > Recommended: <the step, in plain words>
   > Why: <the reason, from the inspector's facts, in one line>
   > First step: <what happens next, and who does it>

4. **Stop.** Do not start the step. Offer to hand off if the writer wants.

#### Fitting the recommendation to who owns the step

The inspector's `next_owner` line says who acts.

- `ai`: offer to run `next_skill`.
- `writer`: the step is the writer's own work. Say what they would do and how the library supports it, for example: "Write chapter 3 from its beats. When it's done, ask me to check it." Never offer to do a stage the writer owns.
- `alternate`: the next scene is whoever's turn it is; say so.
- `writer-decides`: the step is a decision only the writer can make. Lay out what needs deciding (the pending facts, the chapters awaiting check-in) as a short numbered list with a recommended answer each, and wait.

#### Special cases

- **Not set up, or set up by an earlier version** (`setup` is `none`, `malformed`, `duplicate`, or starts with `v1`): recommend `story-onboard`. For a `v1` setup, also say in one line how the project currently works, from its settings lines.
- **`conflicting`**: the settings in `AGENTS.md` are used. Mention in one line that `CLAUDE.md` also has a block, and that `story-onboard` can tidy it up. Then make the recommendation as usual.
- **Design complete** (design-only projects): say the design is approved and finished, and offer `story-worldbuild`, `story-cast`, or `story-plan` to refine it. Never suggest drafting.
- **The recommended skill is not installed** (`next_reason` says so): say so plainly, and offer the nearest installed skill or a plain next action. Never hand off to a skill that is not installed.

#### Rules

- One recommendation. Mention an alternative only if the writer's own words point to it.
- If `promises_oldest_unpaid` names a promise and the step is to plan or draft, add one line naming it in plain words and how long it has waited. Skip it otherwise.
- Use the inspector's facts; do not guess at things the files can't show.
- Change nothing in the project.

## 3. story-adopt

*Use to bring existing chapters, notes, Word files, spreadsheets, or another writing tool's project into this layout. Shows an import plan, preserves originals and provenance, resumes safely, and proposes facts for approval.*

Bring a writer's existing material into a Story project. The writer's files are the source of truth. You organize copies and propose facts; they decide what is true. Adopt does not decide how the writer and the AI split the work: `story-onboard` does that afterward.

#### Rules that never bend

- **Originals are never modified, moved, or deleted.** Everything is copied or converted into new files.
- **Show the plan and wait for approval** before anything is written. A "looks fine" is approval; silence is not.
- **Nothing you mine is canon.** Proposed facts wait in a queue until the writer confirms each one.
- **Follow the project's own conventions.** If the instructions file names a place or pattern for something (open questions, chapter files, voice files), use it. Never create a second register or a competing layout.

#### Steps

1. **Plan.** Run the script next to this file, with its full path; do not `cd`. Nothing is written.

   `python3 adopt.py plan <project-folder> --json <plan-file>`

   Put the plan file outside the project. Read the plan to the writer in plain words: what will be created and where, what already exists, what is left in place and why. Unsupported formats (old `.doc`, PDFs, Scrivener projects) are never touched; ask the writer to export them to `.docx` or `.txt` if they want them in. Point out anything the plan marks as already imported, changed since it was last imported, or sharing a name with another file.
2. **Approval.** Ask whether to go ahead. The writer may drop items or change a destination; edit the plan file's `items` to match (for example, to follow a chapter-naming pattern their instructions specify). Do not apply until they agree.
3. **Apply.**

   `python3 adopt.py apply <project-folder> <plan-file> --yes`

   Report what the script printed. Exit code 5 means some items failed and the rest were imported: name each failure and its reason, and say that re-running Adopt retries only those. Exit code 3 means an existing file changed: stop and tell the writer exactly which.
4. **Mine facts.** The script converts and files; you read what was imported (chapters in `work/drafts/`, notes in `work/brainstorm/imported/`) and propose facts. Put every proposal in the project's own register if it names one, otherwise in `work/brainstorm/adopt-facts.md`, grouped by the page each fact would go to. One line per fact:

   `- [pending major] (established) <the fact, in the writer's words as far as possible>. Source: <file>, "<quote of fifteen words or fewer>". Goes to: kb/characters/<name>.md`

   - **Major or minor.** Major: anything about plot, rules, motive, relationships, or the timeline. Minor: texture, background, names of unimportant things.
   - **What kind of statement it is.** This matters most:
     - `(established)`: the narrative states it as true.
     - `(claimed by <who>)`: a character says or believes it. A claim is not a fact about the world. A character can lie or be wrong, so never file a claim as established.
     - `(planned)`: the writer's notes say it, but no chapter shows it yet.
   - **Only what the text supports.** Do not fill gaps. A guess about someone's job, family, or past is not a fact; leave it out and ask the writer if it matters.
   - **Contradictions.** When sources disagree, record one line that names both versions and both sources, starting `[pending major] conflict:`. Never pick one yourself.
5. **Review with the writer.** Walk through the queue, most important first. For each line the writer says confirm, correct, or drop.
   - On confirm or correct: write the fact into its `kb/` page and remove the line from the queue.
   - On drop: change the line to start with `[rejected]` and keep it, so it is never proposed again.

   Imported spreadsheet tables that need your confirmation carry a "Status: needs review" line, and nothing else is marked; `python3 adopt.py review <project-folder>` lists them. Delete that line only when the writer has confirmed the table; trust is the default, so nothing replaces it. Never confirm on the writer's behalf.
6. **Hand back.** Say what exists now and hand off to `story-onboard`, which sets up how the writer wants to work. If there are chapters but no spec, mention that a spec and beats can be worked out from them (below).

#### Optional: a spec and beats from existing chapters

Offer this after an import when the writer has chapters but no plan. Ask first.

1. Read every imported chapter and any notes. Summarize each chapter in a paragraph: who is in it, what happens, what changes. These become that chapter's beats.
2. Propose a spec in the sections `story-plan` uses: premise, promises, characters, rules of the world, progress, arcs, ending, open questions. Mark each line as **stated in the text** (with the chapter and a short quote) or **inferred**.
3. Do not invent an ending. An unwritten ending is an open question.
4. Confirm one section at a time. Save nothing unconfirmed.
5. Save the confirmed spec to `work/spec/spec.md` and the summaries as beats files in `work/beats/`, one per chapter, each marked "reconstructed from the draft".

#### Chapter cards (long stories)

When the import brings in five or more chapters, offer to build a card for each (see Chapter cards in `story-continue`), so later work can read the story in tiers. Cards are derived and never change a chapter. Ask first.

#### An established project

If the plan shows a populated `kb/`, the project already exists. The plan may have nothing to import; applying it then only records the adoption in `work/adopt/provenance.md`, which lets `story-onboard` set the project up. Knowledge-base pages, style files, the open-questions register, tool folders, and git history are left alone. An existing `work/outline/` counts as the project's beats; do not move it.

#### What you tell the writer at the end

Only what the script printed and what you can see on disk: how many items were imported and where, what failed and why, what was left in place, what is waiting in the fact queue, any contradictions, and that the originals are exactly as they were.

## 4. story-develop

*Use to grill a story idea, poke holes in a premise, or decide the story before planning. Asks questions or offers choices according to who leads, records settled decisions, and ends with a summary for confirmation.*

Sharpen a story idea until nothing important is left assumed. The writer decides; you ask, recommend, and keep the record. Do not write prose, a spec, or beats here; `story-plan` writes the spec afterward.

#### Before the first question

1. **Read the settings** in the project's instructions file (the Story project block): who owns planning, and how much craft language to use. If there is no valid block, work as if the writer leads and uses full craft terms, and mention that `story-onboard` can set this up.
2. **Read the project's own rules** in the instructions file and any `kb/README.md`. If they name where decisions, terms, or open questions go, use those places; the locations below are only defaults. Never create a second register.
3. **Read what exists**: `kb/vocab.md`, the `kb/` pages, `work/spec/`, `work/brainstorm/`. Never ask about something already written down. If the story is already worked out, say in three lines what is settled and ask only about what the project leaves open.

#### Two ways to run it

**When the writer owns planning: questions.** Work the idea as a tree. Find the frontier (every open question whose prerequisites are settled) and ask it in one round, numbered, with your recommended answer and one line of why for each:

```
Q1 - <short title>: <the question, with options if there are any>
Recommended: <your answer, and why>
```

Wait for the answers, recompute the frontier, and ask the next round. If the writer discovers the story as they write, ask only what the story cannot do without (the premise, what the main character wants, what the opening promises) and record the rest as open.

**When the AI owns planning: choices.** Run rounds of at most four choices, each with a recommended default and one line on what each option does to the story, so the writer can answer with a number or "go". Make the options in a round differ in kind, not only in detail, and label each as the usual pick, a less usual one, or an unusual one. Include at least one unusual option, so a round is never three variations of the obvious answer. The usual order is the idea (restate theirs, or offer three two-line premises), tone and how much danger is real, the main character, the central problem or power and what it costs, the opposing force with a plan of its own, and how the ending should feel. Skip any round they have already answered. Fill in what they do not care about (names, minor characters, texture) and list it under "Defaults I picked" for them to veto. Anything touching the plot, the rules, a motive, a relationship, or the ending needs their explicit choice. When they say "use the defaults", those are your choices, not theirs: mark them as yours.

After a round about rules or characters, offer once: "Want to tinker with this? Say 'lab'." Send rules and powers to `story-worldbuild`, characters to `story-cast`. Never push it.

#### What to ask about

Shape the questions with the craft checks listed under "Craft checks" below, not generic prompts. With `craft-language: full`, name the idea you are using ("the character promise", "the Big P Plot"); with `brief`, add a one-line explanation; with `none`, ask the plain question and name nothing. Never show skill names to the writer.

#### Language and contradictions

- When the writer uses a story term loosely, or two terms for one thing, stop and settle it: "You've called it both a marker and a buoy. Are they the same?"
- When something they say contradicts the project, say so at once and ask which is right.
- Test boundaries with a concrete scenario: "If a marker fixes a channel's route, what happens when the tide has already moved it?"

#### Record as you go

Write each answer down when it is settled, not at the end. The writer settling it is the approval; your defaults are not settled until the writer approves them.

- **Terms**: an entry in `kb/vocab.md` with the canonical name, a short definition, and aliases. No record of where or when it was decided.
- **Facts about people, places, rules, events**: the matching `kb/` page, one concept per page. When a placeholder name is settled, rename the page and update every mention.
- **Decisions about the book itself** (genre, tone, form, what it is about, its promises): a running list in `work/brainstorm/develop-decisions.md`, not `kb/`.
- **Open questions and rejected options**: the project's own register, or `work/brainstorm/` if it has none.

Never record a guess or a rule the writer did not state.

#### Finishing

The session is done when the frontier is empty: every branch visited, nothing silently assumed.

1. Summarize: the premise in a sentence or two, the settled decisions, your defaults still awaiting a yes, and the open questions.
2. Ask the writer to confirm. If they disagree, treat it as new answers and keep going.
3. After they confirm, save the summary to `work/brainstorm/develop-summary.md` and record any defaults they approved.
4. Name the next step: the spec, with `story-plan`. Do not start it.

## 5. story-plan

*Use for a story spec, chapter beats, an outline, or a promise ledger. Drafts plans when the AI owns planning and coaches when the writer does; records craft-check evidence and saves only after approval.*

Two documents: the **spec**, which records what is settled about the whole story, and the **beats**, one file per chapter saying what each chapter does. Both are saved only after the writer approves them. Planning records decisions; it does not make them for the writer.

#### Before you start

1. **Read the settings** in the project's Story project block: who owns planning, and how much craft language to use. No valid block: plan as if the writer owns it, and mention `story-onboard`.
2. **Read the project's own rules** in its instructions file. A spec format, a folder, chapter numbering, a naming pattern, or several voice files per part of the book all take priority over the defaults here.
3. **Read what exists**: `work/brainstorm/develop-summary.md` and the decisions list if there is one, `kb/vocab.md`, the `kb/` pages the plan needs (in a large knowledge base, read its index first), and any existing spec and beats.
4. If almost nothing is settled, say so and recommend `story-develop` first. A plan built on guesses is worse than none.

#### Who does the planning

- **The AI owns planning.** Draft the document, show it, take corrections, and save it only after the writer approves.
- **The writer owns planning.** Do not write the plan for them. Offer the template below, ask the questions the checks raise, and when they have written it (or dictated it, which you record in their words, adding nothing), check it and report what you found. Save their words, not yours.

#### The spec

Sections: premise (the book in one or two sentences, then what happens chapter to chapter if the writer has said), promises, characters (want, need, what stops them, where they sit on the three scales; link their `kb/` pages), rules of the world (costs and limits included), progress (the kind the story measures, and its soul), arcs, ending (what it must pay off and how it should feel), and open questions. Leave out a section the story does not need.

Every line is one of three kinds:

- **Settled**: the writer said it or it is in `kb/`. Unmarked.
- **Proposed**: you inferred or suggested it. Start the line with "Proposed:".
- **Open**: nobody has decided. List it under open questions.

Never present a proposal as settled, and never fill a gap to make a section look complete.

**The promise ledger.** With the spec, keep `work/spec/promise-ledger.md`: one entry per promise the story makes, with where it is made, where it advances, and where it pays off. If the project already tracks promises its own way, follow that and skip the format below. Otherwise write one line per promise: `- P03 | planted ch2 | open | the promise in a few words`. The status is `open` (made, not yet moved), `advanced` (moved on the page, not paid), `paid`, or `dropped` (given up on purpose, with the reason in the text). `planted` is the chapter that makes it, or for a promise not yet written the chapter the beats plan it for. A promise with no payoff is either a gap to list or a deliberate choice to note as `dropped`, with why.

#### The beats

Ask how far to plan: the whole book, one part, or the next few chapters (the default for a writer who discovers the story is a few chapters). One file per chapter in `work/beats/`, named `NNN-slug.md` unless the project names a pattern:

- **Dateline or header**, if the project's chapters open with one.
- **Chapter goal**: what this chapter changes, in one sentence.
- **Viewpoint and tense**, and which voice file applies, if it varies.
- **Promises carried**: which promises from the ledger it makes, advances, or pays off.
- **Scenes**, in order: who wants what, what stands in the way, how it turns out.
- **Turn (a note for the writer, not a line for the page)**: what each scene means for the story. Whoever drafts uses it to aim the scene and never states it in the prose.
- **Hook out**.
- **Facts to check**: the `kb/` pages the draft will lean on.
- **Checks applied**: one line per applicable check from the beats list below, `<id>: <what it found in this chapter>`, or `<id>: n/a, <reason>`, or `<id>: deliberate, <what the story is doing instead>`.

In saved files, spell out each complete check ID on its own line; do not use ranges or family-only labels.

Keep a chapter's beats to about a page. Update the promise ledger as beats are planned.

#### Before showing a plan

Run the checks for the document (below). Every applicable check gets a specific note about this story or `n/a` with a reason; a note that would fit any story is not a check. Then show the writer what you found: coverage gaps in the ledger, scenes that carry no promise, a middle that repeats itself, a length that does not fit, and anything contradicting `kb/`. Say what you fixed and what needs their decision.

#### Confirming and changing

Show the document (section by section if it is long), point out every proposal, gap, and open question, and take corrections. Save only after the writer approves. A saved plan can change later: show the change as a diff, add a line to a "Changes" list at the bottom saying what changed and why, and name the beats and drafts it touches.

Say what comes next (beats after a spec, drafting after beats) and stop.

## 6. story-draft

*Use to draft a chapter or scene, take a turn, or act on "go" for a planned chapter. Follows drafting ownership and project voice; coaches when the writer drafts. Queues new facts for approval and supports bounded autopilot.*

Write prose only when the settings say the AI drafts. The draft is working material: the writer accepts it, changes it, or throws it away.

#### Before anything

1. **Read the settings** in the project's Story project block: who owns drafting, and how much craft language to use. No valid block: do not draft; offer a scene list and mention `story-onboard`.
2. **Read the project's own rules first.** They may name the voice and style files for each part of the book, chapter length, chapter naming and numbering, a header or dateline every chapter opens with, and where open questions go. Follow them over anything here. If they name the files to load for drafting, load those and no more.
3. **Read the chapter's beats**, the `kb/` pages its "facts to check" names, and the chapter before it. Do not load the whole knowledge base.
4. **Read the voice file and its sample passage last**, just before you write, for how the story sounds and not only what the sheet says. Where the writer has chapters of their own, those pages are the voice. Never reuse a sentence or an image from the voice file or its sample: they show the register, and a reader would meet them twice.

#### By who owns drafting

- **`ai`**: draft the chapter (below).
- **`alternate`**: write the next scene only, continuing from where the writer's last scene ends, in the writer's voice as their pages show it. Then hand the next scene back to them.
- **`writer`**: do not write the chapter. Offer a scene list for it (who wants what, what is in the way, how it turns out, the hook out), the questions under "Before you write a scene", the questions the craft checks below raise, or a line or two of illustration if the writer asks how something could go. Then offer to check their draft when it is done.
  If they want the AI to draft after all, that is a settings change: offer `story-onboard`, which shows the change and asks first. Never change the settings yourself.
- **`none`** (design only): there is no prose stage. Say so, and offer to refine the design instead.

#### When inputs are missing

- **No beats for this chapter, and the AI owns planning:** do not draft blind. First work out what this chapter is for, from the project itself: any timeline or phase plan in `kb/`, the spec, the promise ledger, the open questions, and the previous chapter's hook out. Write a working plan of a few lines to `work/brainstorm/draft-plan-NNN.md`: what the chapter changes, what it sets up or pays off, the main scenes, and the hook out. A chapter that changes nothing and sets nothing up is the failure to avoid. If the project does not say what comes next, or two directions are equally supported, ask one question with the two directions as choices, or offer `story-plan`, instead of picking one quietly. Then draft from the working plan. Show the plan and every assumption at the top of your hand-over, and offer to plan properly afterward. Do not refuse.
- **No beats, and the writer owns planning:** ask for the chapter's beats in a few bullets, offering a scene-list template. If the writer says "just write it", draft with listed assumptions as above.
- **Nothing exists at all:** ask one question about the story and offer `story-develop`.
- **No voice or style file, and no earlier chapters:** ask for a paragraph of the writer's own writing or the voice they want, or offer `story-voice`, and wait.

#### Reading a long story

Once the story has five or more chapters, read it in tiers instead of loading every chapter:

1. The chapter in hand and its beats, in full.
2. The chapters just before and just after it, in full.
3. Every other chapter as its card in `work/cards/`. Open the prose only when a card is missing or stale, when the task needs the exact wording, or when a card disagrees with another source. The prose wins: correct the card from it.

Run `cards.py report <project folder>` from the `story-continue` skill folder (full path, no `cd`) to see missing and stale cards. A card that agrees with everything else is enough: do not open its chapter just to be thorough. A card points to its chapter; a fact that is only on a card is not settled.

Writing a card is housekeeping, not a fix. When you had to open a chapter because its card was missing or stale, rewrite the card from the chapter and stamp it (see Chapter cards in `story-continue`), and say so in one line. Never change a chapter to match its card.

#### Drafting a chapter

- Follow the beats in order: the goal, each scene and how it turns out, the hook out. If a beat does not work on the page, say so and propose a change; do not skip it quietly.
- Stay inside `kb/`. Never contradict a settled fact. Where the beats need a detail that is not settled, write something plausible and list it as a new fact.
- Keep the project's chapter length, viewpoint, tense, and voice file for this part of the book.
- Apply the craft checks below while you write. Avoid the machine-prose patterns listed among them.
- Draft one chapter. Do not run ahead without the writer's go-ahead.

The beats decide what happens. The four sections below decide how it reads, and then "Read it back" comes before any hand-over.

#### Before you write a scene

Decide how the scene is lived before you write a word of it. For each scene, settle these in your notes or your reasoning, never on the page:

- **Want and dread.** What the viewpoint character is trying to get or avoid here, and what they are afraid will happen instead.
- **In and out.** The state they arrive in (the last scene, the hour, their body) and how they leave it changed.
- **What they have wrong.** A belief, a misreading of someone, a thing they are not looking at.
- **What only they would notice.** Two or three specifics of this place and these people that come from the character's trade, history, and want. If the first one to arrive would fit any story, throw it out and take the next.
- **Where the words go.** The moment the scene turns gets the room. Start as late as the scene allows and stop once the change has happened.
- **One thing the beats did not predict:** a remark, an object, or a reaction that is right for these people and was not in the plan.

If an earlier chapter exists, note how it opened and how it closed, and do both differently here.

#### Narration

Prose with the faults taken out is clean, not alive. In first person and close third the narration is the viewpoint character's experience, not a camera's record.

- **Keep their mind on the page.** What they notice arrives with what they make of it, in their words, with no "she thought" in front. Let them work things out, rationalize, remember what the thing in front of them calls up, and be wrong. A scene of only actions, objects, and speech reads as filmed.
- **A thought is not an explanation.** An explanation is yours and tells the reader what the scene means: leave it out. A thought is the character's, can be mistaken, and leads to what they do next: keep it.
- **Carry feeling through attention and action:** what it makes them notice, avoid, do badly, or think about instead. Not a label, and not a report from the body (chest, breath, pulse, stomach). When the character would know what they feel, they can say so plainly in their own words.
- **Pick the detail that could only be here** and make it do a job: show who is looking, change what someone does, carry history. One that works beats three that decorate.
- **Say the exact thing.** The literal sentence first; a comparison only when it shows what the literal one cannot, taken from the character's world. Describe what is there and what happens, not what is absent ("it did not rush"). If you cannot say what a sentence claims or picture what it shows, cut it.
- **Change speed.** Summarize or skip the routine, and slow down where the scene turns. A chapter in which every moment gets equal care has no shape.
- **Let it be as loud as it is:** funny, blunt, ugly, or angry when the moment is. Restraint in every line is a habit too.

None of this is a quota: one thought where it counts, not one in every paragraph. A project whose voice file asks for a narrator who stays outside the characters keeps that, and gets its life from what it selects. `prose-craft` has the reasoning and a worked before-and-after for each point; load it when a scene will not come alive.

#### How people talk

Drafted dialogue fails in one predictable way: everyone takes turns delivering clear, complete, reasonable lines. People do not talk like that, least of all in a conflict.

- **Cornered people deflect.** They ask who told you, appeal to family or rank, change the subject, offer food, go quiet, or attack. They do not explain their position in a tidy speech, unless lecturing is who they are.
- **Lines do not answer each other neatly.** People answer the question they wish they had been asked, or none. They do not pick up the last speaker's words and hand them back. Some questions get no answer at all.
- **People interrupt and leave things unsaid.** A line can break off. A pause can be the answer.
- **Each speaker sounds like themself.** Give them their own vocabulary, sentence length, and things they avoid saying. Cover the names: you should still know who is talking.
- **Facts come out contested and in fragments**, as accusations, half-denials, and slips, never as a briefing for the reader. If the reader must learn how something works, let it arrive in pieces that someone is fighting over.
- **Nobody states the subtext.** "I feel betrayed because you promised" becomes "You said you'd stay."
- **Not everyone is oblique.** Dodging is one behavior among several. Some people say the blunt thing, badly or too loudly, and some scenes need the plain sentence said. If every speaker deflects with the same dry wit, that is one register again.
- **Tags:** "said", or none. Rapid exchanges can stay untagged when the voices and pressure are clear.
- **Action only where it does work.** Put an action between lines where something changes: information lands, a lie is tested, power shifts, restraint fails, or a decision turns. It earns its place when the reaction changes the next beat. A nod, sip, or look away that anyone could do, or a task step with no human pressure in it, is filler. Do not spread beats evenly, and do not "fix" flat dialogue by adding a gesture or an emotion label to every line. A plain "He said nothing" is fine.

#### Leave the meaning to the reader

- Show what happens. Do not add a sentence that names the lesson, the significance, or the feeling just shown. This rules out the narrator's captions, not the character's thoughts.
- The beats' turn is your aim, not your text. Never put it on the page, not even paraphrased.
- End a scene where it has changed: on a line, an action begun, a decision, a question left open. Not on a summary of what it meant, and not on a quiet image every time.

#### Read it back

Before handing over, read the chapter once as its reader would, start to finish. Then, in one pass:

1. **Find the passage a reader would skim, and the moment the chapter turns.** If the turn is not the most alive passage in the chapter, write it again from inside the character: what they want, what they see, what they think it means. Do the same for the passage a reader would skim, or cut it. Write the passage again whole; polishing its words does not fix a passage that was never imagined.
2. **Test the lines that sound best.** For each striking image and each closing line, say what it literally states. If it states nothing, or the picture is impossible, put the exact thing in its place.
3. **Check that it adds up.** Follow the hours, the positions, and the objects through the chapter: what time it is, who stands where and in what order, who is holding what, what each person can see. A lamp handed away is not carried on, a shift that began at seven did not end at seven, and a count has to match the people named. Fix each slip on the page.
4. **Count your habits.** Run `habits.py` from the `story-check` skill folder (full path, no `cd`) on the chapter. It lists, with line numbers, the comparisons, negated narration, body reports, hedges, and one-line paragraphs in the narration, and any run of sentences opening with the same word. Where one habit is carrying the page, keep the instances that do real work and rewrite the rest. The counts are a prompt to look and have no passing number.
5. **Check the borrowings.** Look in the chapter for each example line in the voice file and each sentence of its sample. One that was reused, even with a word changed, comes out. The opening and the ending differ in kind from the previous chapter's.

Leave this pass out of the hand-over. The hand-over says what the chapter does, not that it was improved.

#### Autopilot (the AI drafts and revises)

When the settings give the AI both drafting and revising, keep going chapter by chapter until the check-in the settings name (`check-in`: every chapter, every three chapters, or every part of the book). For each chapter:

1. **Draft** it as above.
2. **Check it silently** following `story-check`, including the dialogue and over-explanation pass and the text checker (`check_text.py` in the `story-check` skill folder, run with its full path on your chapter, adding `--words MIN-MAX` when the beats or the project's rules state a length range; fix real slips such as leaked wrapper text, an unclosed quote, a duplicated paragraph, a last line that stops mid-sentence, or a loop of repeated text, and bring a chapter that misses the stated length back into range). Keep the findings as a working note in `work/critique-reports/`; do not show the raw report.
3. **Revise** what must be fixed, following `story-revise`, and delete each finding from the working note once fixed, or mark it `[resolved] declined: <reason>` when you keep the text on purpose: keep the earlier version in `work/drafts/_previous/`, change as little as each finding needs, and re-check your own new text so one habit is not swapped for another. At most two passes.
4. **Continuity**: compare the chapter with `kb/` and the earlier chapters. If it contradicts a settled fact and you cannot fix it inside the chapter, stop and ask.
5. **Queue the new facts** as pending, major or minor, as described below.
6. Set the chapter's status line to `<!-- status: awaiting check-in -->`.

You may draft later chapters on top of pending **minor** facts; list those dependencies on the cards. A pending **major** fact stops autopilot until the writer decides. Stop earlier, too, when you need a decision, the plan needs to change, two revision passes did not clear a must-fix, or a chapter would cross a line the writer set.

**At the check-in**, show one card per chapter, five to eight plain lines: where it is saved; what happens; what changed; which promises moved; the new facts needing a yes or no, each with your recommended answer; what you fixed in your own check, in one line; any decision you need. Use the craft language the settings name.

**When the writer answers:**
- An approved fact goes into its `kb/` page, and its queue line is removed.
- A rejected fact: change its queue line to start with `[rejected]` (it is never proposed again), and set every chapter that used it to `<!-- status: needs revision: <the rejected fact> -->`. Revising those comes first, through `story-revise`.
- An accepted chapter is trusted, and trust is the default, so accepting it means deleting its status line and deleting its open findings from the check report (or marking a kept one `[resolved] declined: accepted as is`), and deleting the report file when nothing is left in it. Do this only on the writer's word, and record nothing else: no date, no "accepted by".
- If `work/drafts/_delivered/` holds a copy of that chapter, compare it with the accepted chapter using `diff_chapter.py` in the `story-edits` skill folder (full path, no `cd`). Unchanged: delete the copy now. Edited: keep it, because `story-edits` learns from the writer's changes and then deletes it.

#### Saving and handing over

Save the chapter to `work/drafts/` with the project's naming, or the beats file's `NNN-slug.md`. Its first line is `<!-- status: draft -->`. Never remove that line yourself; only the writer's approval does, and a chapter with no status line is trusted.

Also save an exact copy of the chapter, as handed over, to `work/drafts/_delivered/` under the same file name, replacing any earlier copy. It is how the writer's later edits can be told from your text. Keep it only until those edits have been learned from or the chapter is accepted unchanged.

Then write or refresh the chapter's card (see Chapter cards in `story-continue`) when the story has five or more chapters or the chapter already has a card.

If the project keeps a promise ledger, update it: mark each promise the chapter moved `advanced` or `paid`, and add a line for each new promise the chapter made (the line format is in `story-plan`). Change nothing else in it.

Then tell the writer, in the craft language the settings name:

1. Where the chapter is saved.
2. **New facts the draft invented**, one line each. Add each as `- [pending major] <fact> (chapter N)` or `- [pending minor] <fact> (chapter N)`. Major means plot, rules, motive, relationships, or timeline; minor means texture and background. Never write them into `kb/`. Never re-propose a fact marked `[rejected]`. If the project's instructions name one register for open or review items (for example a single open-questions file), use it for these too: pending facts are open items, and a second register is never created. Only when the project names none, use `work/brainstorm/pending-facts.md`.
3. Anywhere you departed from the beats, and why.
4. Any settled fact that was strained.
5. The assumptions list, if inputs were missing.
6. The next step: a check, then revision.

Never describe the chapter as finished or good. Describe what you did.

## 7. story-check

*Use to check, review, or critique a chapter, test dialogue, or give a reader's report. Cites evidence against the plan, craft checks, and machine-prose patterns. Never edits the chapter.*

Find what is not working and say where, with evidence. Never fix anything here. A check that rewrites is a revision, and the writer should see the findings first. Checking is available in every setting, whoever wrote the chapter.

#### Before you check

1. **Read the settings** in the project's Story project block, for the craft language to use. No valid block: use full terms.
2. **Read the project's own rules.** Any review checklist, voice rules, chapter format, or length range it names is part of the check. Where it names several voice files (for example one per part of the book), use the one that applies to this chapter.
3. **Read the chapter, its beats, the spec, and the `kb/` pages the beats name.** Without a spec or beats, check against the frameworks, the prose patterns, and the chapter's own internal consistency, and say what you could not check.
4. **Run the text checker first**, with its full path and the chapter file or the folder of chapters: `python3 check_text.py <chapter or folder>`. If the beats or the project's own rules state a length range for a chapter, add `--words MIN-MAX` (for example `--words 1800-2500`). It reports leaked assistant text, stray formatting, unbalanced quotes, duplicated paragraphs, encoding damage, whitespace problems, a last line that stops mid-sentence, a long phrase repeated three or more times, and a chapter outside the stated length, with line numbers. Treat each warning as something to look at, not a verdict: a real slip becomes a finding, a deliberate choice does not. A chapter that stops mid-sentence is probably cut off, so say that first. A repeated phrase may be a deliberate refrain, song, or repeated document header. A length warning is a mismatch with the plan, not a flaw in the prose: say which one the writer should change.
5. **Run the habit counter**, the same way: `python3 habits.py <chapter or folder>`. For English prose it lists, with line numbers, the comparisons, negated narration, body reports, hedges, and one-line paragraphs in the narration, runs of sentences that open with the same word, and each chapter's first and last lines. It has no passing number and it is never a finding by itself. Use it to see what your ear misses: one habit carrying a page, or several chapters that open or close the same way.
6. **Read the whole chapter before noting anything.**

#### Reading a long story

Once the story has five or more chapters, read it in tiers instead of loading every chapter:

1. The chapter in hand and its beats, in full.
2. The chapters just before and just after it, in full.
3. Every other chapter as its card in `work/cards/`. Open the prose only when a card is missing or stale, when the task needs the exact wording, or when a card disagrees with another source. The prose wins: correct the card from it.

Run `cards.py report <project folder>` from the `story-continue` skill folder (full path, no `cd`) to see missing and stale cards. A card that agrees with everything else is enough: do not open its chapter just to be thorough. A card points to its chapter; a fact that is only on a card is not settled.

Writing a card is housekeeping, not a fix. When you had to open a chapter because its card was missing or stale, rewrite the card from the chapter and stamp it (see Chapter cards in `story-continue`), and say so in one line. Never change a chapter to match its card.

#### Two kinds of report

**A check report** (the default, and what "check" or "review" means): run the craft checks below that apply. Skip any that do not, silently. Save the report to `work/critique-reports/` with the chapter's file name plus `-check` (or the project's own convention). One finding per list item:

```
- [open] <must fix | should fix | note>: <short title>. Check: <id>. Where: <chapter, scene or paragraph>. Evidence: "<short quote>". Fix: <what to do, in a sentence or two>.
```

Three kinds of finding have no framework check: a contradiction with `kb/` or an earlier chapter uses `Check: continuity`, a gap between the chapter and its beats or spec uses `Check: plan`, and a real warning from the text checker uses `Check: text`. Never borrow an unrelated ID to fill the field.

Rank findings by how much they hurt the chapter. End with the top three priorities and one thing the chapter does well that should be kept. A finding is removed from the report when it is fixed; one kept on purpose becomes `[resolved] declined: <reason>`, handled by `story-revise`.

**A reader's report** ("is it any good?", "be a reader"): read as a skeptical reader and report in six short parts: where I got bored, where I got confused, where I stopped believing it, what I saw coming too early, what worked, and the one passage I would read aloud to someone beside the one I skimmed, both quoted. Give only the three biggest problems, each with a one-line example. Then the twist test: what you think the story's biggest secret is and how sure you are, and which clue gave it away if you guessed it early. Run the craft checks silently to find the problems; say them in the reader's words. Do not save a file unless the writer asks for one.

#### Dialogue and over-explanation

Give these their own pass, because they are what makes drafted fiction read as machine-written.

- **Dialogue.** Do lines answer each other in tidy pairs, or echo each other's wording? Does a cornered character explain their position in a tidy speech? Does anyone dodge, interrupt, change the subject, or leave a question unanswered? Do the speakers sound different from each other? Does a line state its own subtext? Does the action between lines change the next beat, or is it a nod, sip, or look away that anyone could do, or a task step with no pressure in it? Do not flag untagged rapid dialogue that is clear, or a plain stated silence, as defects.
- **Over-explanation.** Does the narration say what a beat means after showing it, name an emotion right after showing it, announce that a moment matters, or close a scene by explaining it? Does a beat's planned turn appear in the prose as a sentence, when it should only shape the scene?

Use the matching prose-pattern checks for each finding. Quote the line.

#### Flat prose

A chapter can be free of every fault above and still read as written by nobody. Give this its own pass too. It looks for what is missing, so it cannot be done by scanning for bad lines: read each scene and ask what it was like to be the viewpoint character in it.

- **The mind.** Where does the viewpoint character want, judge, or think something through? A scene of only actions, objects, and speech is filmed, not told. Are the reflections the character's own (partial, possibly wrong, leading somewhere), or captions?
- **Feeling.** Is it carried by labels and reports from the body, or by what the character notices, avoids, and does?
- **Detail and exactness.** Could the main details sit in any story? Does any line sound meaningful and state nothing, or show a picture that cannot be pictured? Is description made of denials and reflex comparisons? Look at the lines the habit counter lists.
- **Proportion.** Does the moment that turns each scene get the room, or does every moment get the same care?
- **Range.** Is everything quiet, oblique, and controlled?
- **Across chapters.** Does this chapter open and close the way the last ones did, and lean on the same devices?

Quote the passage a reader would skim and the passage that is most alive, and say whether the chapter's turn is the alive one. A flat-prose finding names the passage and what is missing from it. Its fix is to write the passage again from inside the character, never a list of words to swap. If the project's voice file asks for a narrator who stays outside the characters, that is the style and not a finding.

#### Talking to the writer

- In the file, every finding carries its check ID. In conversation, never show IDs or skill names: say the problem in the craft language the settings name (with `none`, plain words only).
- A framework the story breaks on purpose (for example, soft magic that is never meant to solve the plot) is not a defect. Do not report it, unless the break fails at what the story is trying to do instead.

#### Rules

- Evidence for every finding. No evidence, no finding.
- Quote briefly. Do not paste paragraphs.
- Separate a problem in the chapter from a disagreement with the beats or spec; say which it is.
- Never edit the chapter, the plan, or `kb/`. The only file you write is the report.
- Do not propose cutting style-bearing material (dialogue and its rhythm, inner thoughts, observational detail, deliberate ambiguity or silence, rhythm-bearing repetition) unless you can say what it fails to do. Slack is not a finding just because it is not plot.
- No scores and no flattery. Say plainly what you could not check.

## 8. story-revise

*Use to revise a chapter, apply review notes, or resolve rejected facts. The AI revises its own drafts with previous versions preserved and changes re-checked; when the writer owns revision, it gives notes and leaves prose unchanged.*

Act on a check report, or on chapters flagged for revision. Whoever owns revising does the changing; you never change prose the settings say is the writer's.

#### Before you revise

1. **Read the settings** (who owns revising and drafting, the craft language) and the project's own revision conventions.
2. **Chapters marked `needs revision` come first.** Their status line says why (usually a rejected fact, or a change made by `story-steer`). Remove the rejected fact or apply the change, and nothing else.
3. **If `work/drafts/_delivered/` holds a copy of the chapter that differs from it, the writer has edited the chapter.** Run `story-edits` first, so your revision never overwrites what they changed.
4. Otherwise read the check report, the chapter, its beats, and the `kb/` pages it leans on. With no check report, recommend `story-check` first.
5. List the open findings with your recommendation for each (fix, skip, or ask) and let the writer choose, unless the AI fully owns drafting and revising and this is the silent fix inside autopilot. Never fix what the writer did not agree to.

#### Reading a long story

Once the story has five or more chapters, read it in tiers instead of loading every chapter:

1. The chapter in hand and its beats, in full.
2. The chapters just before and just after it, in full.
3. Every other chapter as its card in `work/cards/`. Open the prose only when a card is missing or stale, when the task needs the exact wording, or when a card disagrees with another source. The prose wins: correct the card from it.

Run `cards.py report <project folder>` from the `story-continue` skill folder (full path, no `cd`) to see missing and stale cards. A card that agrees with everything else is enough: do not open its chapter just to be thorough. A card points to its chapter; a fact that is only on a card is not settled.

Writing a card is housekeeping, not a fix. When you had to open a chapter because its card was missing or stale, rewrite the card from the chapter and stamp it (see Chapter cards in `story-continue`), and say so in one line. Never change a chapter to match its card.

#### By who owns revising

- **`ai`**: revise the AI's own text (below). When drafting alternates, revise only the passages the AI wrote; the writer's passages get notes.
- **`writer`**: do not change the chapter. Give the writer each finding as a short note: where, what is wrong, and one way to fix it, in the craft language the settings name. For a flat passage, give the questions to answer about the moment (what the character wants, sees, and has wrong), not replacement prose. Offer to check again when they have revised.
- **`none`** (design only): there is no prose to revise.

#### Revising

1. **Keep the earlier version.** Copy the chapter to `work/drafts/_previous/` with a version number before changing it. Never overwrite the only copy.
2. Work in priority order, must-fix first.
3. Change as little as the finding needs. Keep the voice and the settled facts. If a fix needs a new fact, queue it as pending; never write it into `kb/`.
4. **A flat passage is the exception to changing little.** When the finding is that a passage has no mind in it, a generic detail, a line that states nothing, or the same weight as everything around it, swapping words will not fix it, because the passage was never imagined. Go back into the moment: what the viewpoint character wants, what they see, what they think it means, what they have wrong. Then write that passage again whole, with the same events and facts, at about the same length, in the project's voice. Leave the rest of the chapter alone.
5. In the check report, delete each finding once it is fixed. A finding you or the writer decide not to fix becomes `[resolved] declined: <reason>`. Only a finding deferred to later stays `[open]`, with "deferred" and why. Delete the report file when no findings are left.
6. **Re-check your own new text** against the finding that prompted it and with the craft checks below. Do not swap one habit for a close cousin of it, such as replacing a named emotion with a stock gesture or a report from the body, or a cut explanation with a comparison. Run `habits.py` from the `story-check` skill folder (full path, no `cd`) on the revised chapter and compare its counts with the earlier version's: a revision that removes one habit and doubles another has not helped.
7. Set the chapter's status line to `<!-- status: draft -->` (or `awaiting check-in` inside autopilot). Never remove a chapter's status line yourself; only the writer's word does that.
8. Replace the chapter's copy in `work/drafts/_delivered/` with the text you are handing over.
9. Rewrite the chapter's card (see Chapter cards in `story-continue`) if it has one, or if the story has five or more chapters.

#### Protect style-bearing material

Some of what looks like slack is the voice. Leave these alone unless a finding shows one failing at its own job:

- dialogue, including its rhythm and what it leaves unsaid
- inner thoughts
- digressive texture and observational detail
- deliberate ambiguity, silence, or flirtation
- repetition that carries rhythm

You may fix wording, punctuation, transcription noise, and paragraph breaks. Never compress concrete material into summary, and never "tighten" a passage by replacing its specific detail with a general statement. When a fix would remove protected material, say which one and what the fix gains, and let the writer decide.

#### Handing over

Say what changed and what did not, and offer another check. Repeat check and revise until the writer says the chapter is done; never decide that yourself.

#### Rules

- Never delete the earlier version.
- Never flatten concrete detail into summary.
- Never delete a finding you did not fix.
- Report what the revision does, not that it is better.

## 9. story-edits

*Use after the writer has edited a chapter the AI drafted and wants the AI to learn from those edits. Compares the delivered text with the edited chapter, groups the changes by what they did, asks about the ones it cannot explain, and saves approved lessons to the voice file.*

The writer edited a chapter you drafted. Their changes are the clearest statement of taste they will give you. Your job is to read them, understand what each one was for, and carry the confirmed lessons into every later chapter. You never change the chapter.

#### Before you start

1. **Read the settings** (who drafts, the craft language) and the project's own conventions, especially where its voice files live. If the project has no voice file, ask where lessons should go before proposing any.
2. **Find the chapters.** The inspector's `chapters_edited_since_delivery` line lists them; or the writer names one. Each needs a copy in `work/drafts/_delivered/`. With none, say there is nothing to compare and stop.
3. **Run `diff_chapter.py`** from this skill's folder with its full path, giving the delivered copy and then the current chapter. Do not `cd`. It reports kept, rewritten, cut, and added paragraphs with line numbers. If it says `unchanged`, delete the delivered copy, say the chapter came through untouched, and stop.

#### Reading the changes

Read each changed paragraph beside the one it replaced. Then sort the changes by what they did, not by where they are. Typical groups:

- **Cut explanation**: a line that said what a beat meant, named a feeling, or restated the scene.
- **Tightened or loosened dialogue**: shorter speeches, more evasion, a different register for a speaker.
- **Concrete detail**: something specific added, or something generic replaced.
- **Rhythm and length**: sentences joined or split, a scene shortened or extended.
- **Word choice and voice**: a word or phrase swapped for the writer's own.
- **Story content**: a fact, name, order of events, or outcome changed.
- **Small fixes**: typos and punctuation. Do not draw lessons from these, and do not ask about them.

Pair a cut with an addition only when the diff reports them as one rewritten paragraph, or they sit in the same place. A cut in one scene and an addition in another are two separate changes, and you do not know they are related.

Before asking anything, decide which groups you can explain. A group explains itself when at least two changes point the same way (five cut explanations in one chapter is a preference, not an accident). A change that stands alone, with nothing else in the chapter pointing the same way, is never a lesson on its own: an added line, a rewrite, or a cut with no match. Those are exactly what to ask about, along with two changes that seem to pull in opposite directions.

#### Asking

Ask at most three questions in one round, each with your best reading and a recommended answer, in the craft language the settings name. Never ask about small fixes, and never ask the writer to justify taste. Wait for the answers.

#### Lessons

Turn what is now clear into short lessons. A lesson needs at least two changes pointing the same way, or the writer's confirmation of one: one line each, written as an instruction to the drafter, specific enough to act on and general enough to reuse.

- Good: "Cut the sentence after a gesture that says what the gesture meant."
- Too vague: "Be more subtle."
- Too narrow: "Remove the line about the ferry."

Mark a lesson that only applies to part of the book (one character, one phase, one kind of scene) with that scope. Show the lessons as a numbered list with the evidence for each in one short phrase, and let the writer keep, change, or drop each.

**Story content the writer changed** is theirs, so it is already decided. Offer to update the matching `kb/` page and the beats; do it only on their yes.

#### Saving

When the writer has answered:

1. Add each approved lesson to the project's own voice file, one line, unmarked. Trust is the default, so approved material carries no marker and no record of who approved it or when. If the voice file already says the same thing, tighten that line instead of adding another.
2. Apply any approved `kb/` or beats updates.
3. Delete the chapter's copy in `work/drafts/_delivered/`.
4. Record each lesson the writer declined as `- [rejected] lesson: <the lesson>` in the project's single register for open items (or `work/brainstorm/pending-facts.md` only if it names none), so it is never proposed again. Add nothing else about the lessons.
5. If the chapter has a card in `work/cards/`, rewrite it from the edited chapter and stamp it (see Chapter cards in `story-continue`).

Never edit the chapter. Once the writer has answered, keep only the voice-file lines and any `[rejected]` line: no delivered copy, no diff, no note about who approved what.

#### Rules

- A lesson is a pattern across changes, not a single edit. One cut is not a rule, and a lone addition is a question, not a lesson.
- Learn the writer's preferences, not your own. Never use a lesson to push a style on them.
- Do not argue about taste, and do not turn the lessons into a critique of the chapter.
- If the changes contradict a lesson already in the voice file, say so and ask which wins.
- Show no skill names and no check IDs to the writer.

## 13. story-worldbuild

*Use to design or stress-test magic, powers, or world rules. Builds an ability table of costs, limits, and counters, hunts exploits, and tests what-ifs. Saves rules only after approval.*

Build the part of the world the story needs, and test it the way a game designer tests a ruleset: a system the story can lean on without cheating. A world is a means to a story, so stop when the story has what it needs.

#### Before you start

Read the settings for the craft language, the project's own rules for where world notes live, and what exists in `kb/` and the spec. Work with an existing world; never start over. Ask what the writer has (a magic idea, a setting, a character, only a feeling) and what story they hope to tell, even loosely.

#### Build

1. **Sort the ideas** into setting, character, and plot, find the holes, and combine ideas across them. Ask only about the gaps the story needs.
2. **Cover the three parts** (physical setting, cultural setting, magic) at the depth the story will use. Follow the chains of consequence between them.
3. **Set the dial** from mysterious to rule-bound, and say what each end lets the story do. A deliberately mysterious system is a valid choice: then it must not solve the plot.
4. **The ability table**, for anything with rules:

   | Ability | What it does | What it costs | What stops it | What counters it |
   | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

- **What-ifs.** Three or four scenarios of three to five lines: two users clash, a villain uses it, someone reaches the limit. Show the outcomes and let the writer adjust.

## 14. story-cast

*Use to design a cast, villain, or relationship map, or distinguish similar characters. Builds sheets with wants, needs, three scales, weaknesses, voice, and arcs; gives the opposition its own plan. Saves only after approval.*

Characters as builds. Offer builds to choose among and make the chosen ones work on the page. Never ask the writer to invent from nothing.

#### Before you start

Read the settings for the craft language, the project's rules for where character pages live, and what exists: the spec, `kb/characters/`, and drafted chapters.

#### The build sheet

For each main character, short:

- **Role** in one line.
- **Wants, needs, what stops them**, a line each.
- **Three sliders, 1 to 5:** how much they act, how easy they are to root for, how good they are at things. Offer recommended settings for the role; the writer adjusts.
- **Weakness**, and which kind: something they must overcome, a rule they hold to, or something they cannot do.
- **Voice tags**: three short tags for how they talk ("blunt, funny, never explains"), so no two sound alike.
- **Arc**: offer the shapes it could take, a line each, and let the writer choose.

Offer two or three builds at a time. Make them differ in kind, not in surface details, label each as the usual pick or a less usual one, and include one unusual build.

#### The opposition

Give the opposing force a plan that would run even if the hero never existed, its own reason, and a moment where the reader sees it choose. Offer three versions that differ in what the opposition wants, not only in how it acts.

#### Relationships

A small table: who trusts whom, who wants what from whom, who is lying to whom. Fill it in and ask the writer to correct it.

#### Sameness check

## 15. story-voice

*Use to choose or change a story's voice, viewpoint, or tense. Shows original demo passages from the writer's story, supports choices and blends, and saves a voice sheet and a sample passage only after approval.*

The writer does not need writing samples. They hear options and choose. A voice is easier to follow from a passage than from a description, so the result is a short voice sheet and a sample passage the writer has approved.

#### Steps

1. **Read** the settings, the project's own rules (an existing voice file, or several for different parts of the book, takes priority; adjust it rather than replacing it), and the spec for premise, hero, and tone.
2. **If the writer already has chapters of their own, start from those.** Their pages are the voice. Describe what the pages do (step 6) and ask what they would change, instead of offering demos.
3. **Offer four voices** as demo passages of about a hundred and twenty words each, all showing the same moment from the writer's own story. Pick a moment with something in it to notice, a thought, and a line or two of speech. Label each by feel ("dry and understated", "warm and talky", "tense and spare", "lush"). Make the four differ in kind: how close the narration sits to the character's thoughts, how much it tells outright, how long the sentences run, how funny it is, and what it stops to notice. Four moods of one voice is not a choice. Recommend one, and say why it fits the tone the writer chose. The demos are illustrations, not story facts.
4. **Let them choose or adjust.** Pick one, blend two, or ask for "like 2 but drier". Rewrite the demo and show it again. Two adjustment rounds, then settle.
5. **Pick how the story is told**, with plain labels and a default: close on one character at a time, past tense (default); several characters in turn; a storyteller who sees everything. Use the craft checks below to say what each choice costs (for example, which kinds of secret it can keep).
6. **Write the sample passage.** In the chosen voice, write a different moment from the story, about three hundred words, that holds what a chapter will need: a place seen by the viewpoint character, something they think through, a short exchange, and one sentence of summary that covers time. Show it. This is where a voice that worked for a hundred words shows whether it holds; adjust it with the writer until they say it sounds right. It is an illustration, not a story fact, and nothing in it is settled.
7. **A favorite book is a fine description.** Describe its qualities in your own words: sentence length, humor, pace, how much description. Do not imitate or quote it.
8. **Save after approval.** The voice sheet goes to `kb/styles/voice.md` or the file the project names, and the sample passage to `kb/samples/` or the place the project names, with the sheet saying where it is.

#### The voice sheet

A dozen lines at most, each one something a drafter can act on:

- who tells it, and in what tense
- how close the narration sits to the character: whose words and opinions color it, and how thoughts appear on the page
- what this narrator notices (their trade, their obsessions) and what they pass over
- sentence and paragraph length, and how much of a scene is dialogue
- how funny it is, and where the humor comes from
- how much description, and how often a comparison is used and from what part of the character's world
- how feeling is carried: stated plainly, shown in what people do, or held back
- how people talk: how much they dodge and interrupt, how blunt they get, how different the speakers sound
- two or three things this voice never does
- where the sample passage is, with the note that drafts match its sound and never reuse its sentences or images

Do not put example sentences on the sheet. They get copied into chapters. The sample passage carries the sound.

#### When the voice changes later

Update the sheet, write a new sample passage, and mark drafted chapters that now differ with `<!-- status: needs revision: voice changed -->`. Never rewrite chapters here.

## 17. foundations-chef-not-cook

*Use when a writer is following craft advice mechanically, a rule worked for someone else but not for them, or they cannot tell why a scene fails. Chef not cook is a way to learn tools by understanding what each one does, so you can diagnose problems and adapt advice to your own book.*

A cook follows a recipe, gets the expected dish, and is stuck when it comes out wrong. A chef knows what each ingredient does, tastes the result, works out what went wrong, and adjusts. Aim to be the chef: learn why a technique works, so you can choose it, change it, or drop it.

#### How to work as a chef

1. **Learn the tool and its job.** For any piece of advice, ask what effect it produces on the reader. "Open with action" is a recipe. "Give the reader a reason to keep reading in the first pages" is the purpose, and many openings can serve it.
2. **Try it on your own pages.** Apply the tool, then read the result as a reader would. Did the intended effect happen?
3. **Diagnose when it fails.** Name which ingredient is off: too much, too little, wrong place, or wrong tool for this book. Change one thing and test again.
4. **Borrow widely.** Take methods from many writers and styles, and build your own process from what works. Decide the effect you want, then find the means. Do not settle for the effect other people say you should want.

#### Checks

- `foundations.chef.01` What is this rule or technique trying to make happen for the reader?
- `foundations.chef.02` Is that effect something this story needs right now?
- `foundations.chef.03` If the story breaks the rule, what does it do to get the effect another way?

If you cannot answer the first question, you are cooking.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- All craft advice is only as good as its ability to help you. Some of it works for most writers and will be wrong for you, or right for you only years from now. Test it, keep what helps, and set the rest aside.
- Advice from successful writers is a sample of what worked for them. A single path that worked once does not prove it is the best path, which is why learning many tools beats copying one routine.
- Learning by doing teaches more than being taught. Finishing drafts is where the tools become understood. See `foundations-gardener-architect` for choosing a drafting process.

## 18. foundations-gardener-architect

*Use when a writer asks whether to outline or just start writing, stalls partway through a draft, loses interest after planning, or has a story that wanders. Gardener versus architect (discovery writer versus outline writer) gives a spectrum, the typical risks at each end, and how to build a blended process.*

Writers sit on a spectrum. An architect plans the structure first, like a house, then hangs the story on it. A gardener plants an idea, tends it as it grows, and sees what it becomes. This is also called outline writing versus discovery writing, or pantsing. Almost everyone lands somewhere between the ends, and strong writers disagree sharply: some insist on outlines, and some say outlines kill a story.

#### Find your place

1. Try both ends on real projects. Outline one book thoroughly. Draft another with only a premise and a few anchor scenes.
2. Notice what happens to your energy. Some writers lose enthusiasm for a story once it is fully mapped, because the discovery was the fun. Others stall without a map.
3. Build a blended process from what you learn, such as a loose outline of the ending and key turns with the middle left open. Expect it to keep changing across your writing life.

#### The typical risk at each end

- **Architects** tend to skip revision, because the next idea feels more exciting than polishing a book they already knew the shape of. Build revision in on purpose. See `foundations-revision-guide`.
- **Gardeners** tend to over-revise early chapters and never reach the end, since each pass reveals something new about the opening. Set a rule that you finish the draft before you rework the start.
- A gardener who discovers the story while writing will often need heavier structural revision afterward. That is the cost of the method, not a sign of failure.

#### Gardeners and early feedback

A discovery writer's story is fragile while it is forming. Opinions from others can redirect it, and a book can drift from romance to vampires to thriller as different readers suggest trends. Hold off outside advice until the draft is done. See `foundations-writing-group-rules`.

#### Checks

None. This skill is about the writer's process and mindset, not a check to run on a story.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A writer can be an outliner for one project and a discoverer for the next. A large cast changes the answer too. A book with many viewpoint characters may be easier to write in story arcs, one thread at a time, then splice the arcs together. That costs extra revision, because pacing breaks when the viewpoints are mixed. A book with only a few threads can usually be written straight through.
- An architect who is stuck on a blank middle and a gardener who has lost the plot are at opposite ends of the same problem. Borrow one tool from the other end instead of switching entirely.

## 19. foundations-artist-mindset

*Use when a writer second-guesses a draft because of what they think readers or trends want, feels the book is ugly and wants to quit, or revises to please everyone. The artist mindset keeps story decisions in the hands of the drafting writer, and treats early books as practice that improves the writer.*

There are two roles in a writing life, and they should not trade places. While you draft, you are the artist, and the artist decides what is best for the story and its characters. Questions about what might be popular belong to a later, separate stage and should not steer the drafting. This skill covers only the artist's side, the part that shapes the pages.

#### Keep the artist in charge of the draft

1. **Decide by story.** At each choice, ask what this book and these characters need. Do not ask what a hypothetical audience would like.
2. **Write the book you want to write.** Chasing a trend means writing toward a moving target, and the market will have moved by the time you finish. Your enthusiasm shows on the page.
3. **Do not compromise the work in revision to chase approval.** When you revise, fix what is wrong for the story. A change made only to look more marketable usually weakens the book.
4. **Let the two roles talk without letting one dictate.** It is fine to notice what kind of stories excite you or what is missing in the books you read, and then let the artist write something in that space. The outside view informs the choice of project and never overrules the content of the draft.

#### Early books are practice

- The product of your writing time is you. Each book makes you more skilled and more empathetic, whatever happens to the manuscript.
- Treat the first several novels as scales on a piano. Your job is to write enough to learn your own process.
- If your skill falls short of your vision and the draft looks ugly, keep going. The gap is normal, and finishing teaches what stopping cannot.
- Weak points you force yourself to learn, such as revision, can become strengths.

#### Checks

None. This skill is about the writer's process and mindset, not a check to run on a story.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- The split between artist and outside concerns is deliberately exaggerated to make the point. Real writers blend them, and for some the advice is wrong.
- Do not read this as permission to ignore readers. Feedback on a finished draft is valuable. See `foundations-alpha-beta-readers`. The point is the order: draft for the story, then listen.
- Ideas are cheap and execution is what counts. Readers and editors can tell years of practice, so protect time for practice. See `foundations-chef-not-cook`.

## 20. foundations-writing-group-rules

*Use when a writer joins a critique group, gets conflicting feedback, feels a group is steering their book, or has to give feedback on someone else's pages. Writing-group rules protect a draft from early advice, set how to receive criticism, and keep feedback descriptive instead of prescriptive.*

Groups can be excellent, and groups that stay together tend to lift each other up. They can also ruin a book. These rules let you use a group without letting it take over.

#### When receiving feedback

1. **Time it.** If you are a discovery writer, wait on advice about a book until the draft is finished and you have started another. Early comments can hijack a story that has not settled.
2. **Stay quiet and write it down.** Do not defend, explain, or ask many questions. Defending teaches readers to soften what they say, and explaining hides how an unaided reader experiences the pages.
3. **Let it sit.** Collect feedback on a finished book while you write something new. Return months later in a revision mindset.
4. **Turn it into a plan.** Sort the notes into a revision guide and expect to use perhaps a third of them. See `foundations-revision-guide`.
5. **Retest.** After revising, find fresh readers to check that the fixes worked. People who saw the old version cannot tell you.

#### When giving feedback

- Be descriptive, not prescriptive. Say what you felt and where you were confused, bored, lost, or excited.
- Do not try to fix the book. A suggestion like adding a comic sidekick comes from your taste, and the writer may take it even though it does not fit. Readers are good at noticing that something is wrong and unreliable about the cause.
- Point to places. "I lost track of who was speaking in the market scene" can be used. "The dialogue is bad" cannot.

#### Checks

- `foundations.feedback.01` Is each note descriptive (what the reader felt, and where) instead of prescriptive (how to fix it)?
- `foundations.feedback.02` Does each note point to a specific place in the text?
- `foundations.feedback.03` Does any suggestion come from the reviewer's taste instead of the book's own promises?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Groups are not for everyone. If a group leaves you unable to write, step back.
- A moderator who keeps the room to these rules protects the writers, since the failure comes from a few strong opinions overriding the author's own vision.
- A writer who is a natural outliner can take feedback earlier with less risk. Gardeners should be the most careful. See `foundations-gardener-architect`.
- Trusted early readers on a finished draft work somewhat differently. See `foundations-alpha-beta-readers`.

#### Where other advice differs

Some editing guidance says useful feedback names a structural action, such as cut, move, combine, or seed earlier, along with what the current version costs the reader. These rules say to describe your reaction and leave the fix to the writer.

Default to the descriptive approach in a peer group, where mixed experience and strong opinions can steer a book. Choose the action-naming approach when the writer asks for a developmental edit, asks outright what you would change, or hires you as an editor. Even then, state the reader cost first, so the writer can judge whether your fix suits the book.

## 21. foundations-revision-guide

*Use when a writer has feedback or a finished draft and does not know where to start revising, feels overwhelmed by notes, or keeps tinkering without progress. A revision guide turns feedback into a ranked, targeted list of fixes, like a programmer's bug list, so each pass has a goal.*

Revision is a skill as hard as drafting a book, and it can be learned. Many of the best writers are better revisers than drafters. A revision guide gives the work shape by turning scattered feedback into a prioritized list of goals you can tick off.

#### Building the guide

1. **Gather the feedback.** Collect notes from readers and your own observations after a cooling-off period away from the book.
2. **Rank the problems.** Put the most important first. A character who does not work outranks a clumsy sentence, because fixing a core problem changes everything built on it.
3. **Plan the top item.** Write specifically how you will fix it. Name the places to plant foreshadowing, the scenes to add, the scenes to cut, and the chapters affected.
4. **Add a second goal if there is room.** Do not try to fix everything in one pass.
5. **Revise and check items off.** The visible progress keeps motivation up. If the guide runs out, read it again for new problems.

Example: three readers say the middle drags. Your guide entry is not "fix the middle". It is: the reader never learns what Mara wants after chapter 9, so add a small goal in chapter 10, let the chase pay off a promise in chapter 14, and cut the second travel scene.

#### Learning to revise

- Revision instincts build across several novels, so it is hard to feel good at it after only one book. Expect to improve with practice.
- Try a change, then ask why it worked or did not. That is how the instinct forms.
- Reading a draft beside its final version can help, but doing the revision yourself teaches more.

#### Checks

- `foundations.revision.01` Are the problems ranked, with core problems (a character that does not work, a broken promise) ahead of line-level ones?
- `foundations.revision.02` Does the top item have a specific plan: where to plant setup, which scenes to add or cut, which chapters change?
- `foundations.revision.03` Is this pass limited to one or two goals?
- `foundations.revision.04` Is any note being applied as a fix without agreement that it points at a real problem?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- The guide suits outliners most, since it works like their plan. Discovery writers can use it too, and should expect bigger structural items near the top. See `foundations-gardener-architect`.
- Feedback on its own is a list of symptoms. Use only the pieces that you agree point at a real problem, and expect to discard many. See `foundations-writing-group-rules`.
- Do not endlessly revise chapter one. Eighty first chapters do not add up to a book. Finish the draft, then revise by priority.

## 22. foundations-alpha-beta-readers

*Use when a writer is deciding who should read a finished draft, how to use readers' reactions, or whether one reader's strong opinion should change the book. Alpha and beta readers are two kinds of early reader with different jobs, and the skill explains how to weigh what each reports.*

Two kinds of early reader do different jobs on a finished draft.

#### The two roles

- **Alpha readers** are a few trusted people with deep knowledge of writing and a long relationship with you. They may tell you what to change, and you ask for their judgment.
- **Beta readers** are a test audience drawn from the people likely to enjoy this kind of book. They report how the book felt and where it dragged, confused, or excited them. They do not tell you how to fix it.

#### Using them

1. Give the draft only when you cannot see how another pass alone would improve it. Another draft that only shuffles things sideways is the signal to ask for readers.
2. Ask betas for reactions. Where did you want to stop reading? What did you expect to happen? Who did you like? Mark the page where each reaction occurred.
3. Look for patterns across readers. Three people stalling in the same chapter is data. One person's complaint is a data point.
4. Find the cause yourself. A reader may say the book is slow and ask for an action scene. The cause could instead be that a promise was dropped or a tangent runs too long. Treat the reader as a reliable alarm and an unreliable mechanic.
5. Feed what you decide to keep into a revision guide. See `foundations-revision-guide`.

#### Checks

- `foundations.readers.01` Across readers, which reactions form a pattern (several readers stalling or lost in the same place), and which are single data points?
- `foundations.readers.02` For each pattern, what is the cause in the story, as opposed to the fix a reader suggested?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Do not weigh any single beta reader too heavily.
- Not every writer likes beta readers. Some rely on a few trusted alpha readers alone, and learning revision after only one book is hard, so lean on those trusted people while your instinct builds.
- Keep your own process. If readers make a draft worse each time, change who you ask or when.
- Discovery writers should get feedback only on finished drafts. See `foundations-gardener-architect` and `foundations-writing-group-rules` for how to receive notes without defending.

## 24. plot-promise-progress-payoff

*Sanderson's Promise, Progress, Payoff model of plot. Load to diagnose why a story, chapter, or sequence feels boring or pointless, to check whether an ending satisfies, or to plan a plot around what the reader is led to expect.*

A plot works when three jobs line up. The opening and early chapters **promise** what kind of story this is and what it will deliver. The middle shows **progress**, signposted so the reader feels the story moving toward those promises. The ending **pays off** the promises. Most plot frameworks are built on this idea.

#### The three jobs

1. **Promise.** Tone, plot, and character expectations set early. It is the easiest of the three to fix after the fact and one of the most important, because it is why a reader keeps going. See `plot-kinds-of-promises`.
2. **Progress.** The signposted sense that the story is approaching what was promised. It is the bulk of the book and the least showy part. See `plot-types-of-progress`.
3. **Payoff.** The fulfilment of the promises. Sanderson's target is that it feels surprising yet inevitable: "payoffs are really just giving people a surprising yet inevitable fulfilment of your promises."

#### Using it to diagnose

When a chapter, sequence, or book is not working, ask in order:

1. What has the story promised the reader by this point?
2. In this stretch, what signals tell the reader we are moving toward any of those promises?
3. Does the payoff deliver what was promised, or something the reader was never led to want?

Most of the time a boring book or an uninvested sequence is a mismatch between promise and progress.

#### Fixing a mismatch

- When promise and progress disagree, the usual fix is to revise the promise, because the progress is what the writer actually wants to tell.
- Promises are the cheapest thing to rewrite: a few early chapters, against replacing dozens of middle ones.
- A diversion reads as dull until the reader is given a reason it matters. One worked example added a promise partway through the story (a loved character will die unless the detour is taken), and the same sequence then read as progress.
- Check whether the story has dropped a promised thread for a more interesting subplot. That is a common cause of readers feeling lost.
- Do not keep polishing early chapters until the book is finished. The promises cannot be written properly until the writer knows what they are promising.

#### Checks

- `plot.ppp.01` Name what the story has promised by this point: its tone, its central conflict, and what the main character wants.
- `plot.ppp.02` For this stretch, name what tells the reader the story is moving toward one of those promises. A stretch with no signpost reads as a stall.
- `plot.ppp.03` Does the payoff deliver what was promised, in a way that feels surprising yet inevitable, or something the reader was never led to want?
- `plot.ppp.04` Has a promised thread been dropped for a more interesting subplot? If the progress is what you want to tell, revise the promise, not the middle.

#### Caveats

- This is mostly a tool for analysis and for outliners; a writer should not feel they must master it before drafting. Discovery writers can use it as a repair lens.
- Breaking the model can work. Into the Woods drops its fairytale promises and rebuilds something different, though some readers resent it.
- Payoff differs across storytelling traditions: ambiguous endings are growing in the West while payoff endings are more common in the East.

## 25. plot-kinds-of-promises

*The kinds of promises a story makes in its opening (tone, plot, character, series) and how to check them. Load when planning or revising a story's opening, a prologue, a first chapter, or a chapter opener, or when readers say the book was not what they expected.*

A story makes several distinct promises at the start, and not all of them are obvious. Check each one against what the book actually delivers.

#### The kinds

1. **Tone promise.** What feel to expect. It comes from opening events, descriptions, names, and prose style: an early joke for a comedy, a shocking death for grimdark. A prologue can carry the tone promise when the plot starts slowly, though the device is close to a cliche and is harder to make stand out.
2. **Plot promise.** The opening offers a small picture or metaphor of the main conflict. If the real conflict arrives late, lean on tone and character first, then bring in a hard problem.
3. **Character promise.** Show what the character wants, what they need, and why they cannot have it. Attached flaws and limits signal where the character will grow or fail.
4. **Series promise.** A structural or packaging promise between books: a stated number of books, a trilogy label, a named line of adventures, a promised ending.
5. **Ongoing promises.** Each new character, plot sequence, and chapter makes its own smaller promise.

#### A chapter-level tool

Strong chapters often open with a "stealth thesis paragraph" that quietly says what the chapter is about, and close with a stinger that signals it was delivered.

#### Checks

- `plot.promises.01` Which kinds of promise does the opening make (tone, plot, character, series), and which does it skip? Not every kind is required; mark each skip as deliberate or accidental.
- `plot.promises.02` From the first pages, could a reader say what feel to expect and what the main character wants?
- `plot.promises.03` Does the character promise show what the character wants, what they need, and why they cannot have it yet?
- `plot.promises.04` If the tone will switch (for example from thriller to travelogue), is the second tone promised at the start?
- `plot.promises.05` Does each new character, sequence, or chapter make its own small promise, and does the chapter signal it was delivered?

#### Length and context

- Short stories must hit their promise harder and sooner, often in the first paragraph. There is no room for a prologue.
- Readers judge how long to give a book from its length. Established authors can trade on their name; newer writers cannot rely on that.
- First lines matter less than the first few pages and first chapter, though a good first line still helps.
- New promises can be made mid-story; the answer is "yes and yes, asterisk", depending on length and twist type.
- Promises are easier to write once the ending is known, so it is normal to revise the opening last.

## 26. plot-types-of-progress

*How to measure, signpost, and pace progress in a plot, and match the kind of progress to the climax. Load when a middle drags, a sequence feels pointless, a plot has several threads, or a climax does not feel earned.*

The writer controls progress and time completely: a time skip can take a sentence and a key moment can take fifty pages. The job is to give the reader a **satisfying sense of progression**, not to force events. Failing at this produces filler or a rushed ending that feels like a deus ex machina.

#### Match the progress to the climax

Decide what kind of progress the story measures, and make the climax resolve that kind.

1. **Information progress** (mystery). The plot rests on someone lacking information and the ending is a reveal. Progress is clues found and suspects eliminated. Signpost it with moments like the detective saying they are close, while the reader gets the information too.
2. **Relationship progress** (buddy comedy, romance). Start with people clearly not getting along, show moments of connection and occasional setbacks, then show them complementing each other. The climax is where they come together.

There are many more kinds; these two are the worked examples.

#### When there are several threads

- Identify the **soul of the story**: the progress thread that drives the ending. Usually pay all threads off together.
- The climax is won by the mechanism of the true plot, not necessarily the one the trappings suggest. In one example, the McGuffin chase is the A plot but the climax is about friendship.
- A longer story can hold more than one soul and resolve one arc before the third act.
- Do not confuse the trappings of the story with what readers actually think it is about.

#### Pacing

- External-obstacle stories (thrillers) pace differently from internal-struggle stories. Match pacing to the story and to your natural style.
- Vary the pace with breathers; constant intensity loses impact.
- To skip travel, set a small promise early that you will time jump, then jump at a structural break, such as the end of a chapter or "three weeks later".

#### Diagnosing a stall

- A page-turner understands progress and how to dole it out. A boring or confusing book usually lacks progress signposts.
- A common cause is dropping a promised line for a more interesting subplot.
- Ask: what does the reader think this sequence is moving toward, and what signals tell them so?

#### Checks

- `plot.progress.01` Name the kind of progress the story measures (information, relationship, skill, distance, or another) and check that the climax resolves that kind.
- `plot.progress.02` With several threads, name the soul of the story, the thread that drives the ending, and check that the climax turns on it and not on the trappings.
- `plot.progress.03` For this sequence, what does the reader think it is moving toward, and what signals tell them so?
- `plot.progress.04` Does the pace vary, with breathers between pushes, and are time skips set up at a structural break?

## 27. plot-cold-open-microcosm

*The cold open as a microcosm: a short opening mini-story that shows the whole story in small form. Load when a writer is designing an opening scene or prologue, wants a hook that previews the book's shape, or asks how to make an opening promise the main conflict.*

Some stories open with a short mini-story, roughly five to fifteen pages, that shows the shape of the whole book in small. The full book then returns to that shape at length, either repeating it or showing it going wrong.

#### How it works

1. A short opening, often in the middle of action, that states the story's shape.
2. The same shape returns at full length, repeated or with a twist where it breaks.

#### Examples

- An adventure film opens with the hero beating a series of traps by wit, then losing the prize, and the film does the same thing again at length.
- A heist story shows the crew pulling off a job, then the next job goes wrong.
- A space adventure puts the central threat in its opening.
- A quest story gathers its friends, reaches a safe place, then repeats the pattern across the book.

#### When to use it

- It is optional; you do not need it.
- It works only if the main conflict can be previewed. That is hard when the conflict does not appear until late in the book.
- If the conflict cannot be previewed, lean on a tone promise, introduce a character the reader likes, then hand them a hard problem. See `plot-kinds-of-promises`.

#### Checks

- `plot.coldopen.01` Can the opening be described as a small version of the book's central conflict? If not, are a tone promise and a likable character with a hard problem doing that work instead?
- `plot.coldopen.02` Does the full book echo the opening's shape, or deliberately break it?
- `plot.coldopen.03` Does the opening make a promise the book keeps?

## 28. plot-big-p-little-p

*The distinction between Big P Plot (what the book is about) and little p plot (what happens chapter to chapter). Load when a writer cannot say what their book is about, when chapters feel disconnected, or when the small-scale action and the main story pull in different directions.*

"Plot" means two things, and many problems come from mixing them up.

- **Big P Plot** is what the book is about, stated in a sentence or two.
- **little p plot** is what happens chapter by chapter: the small problems raised and resolved that hold the reader and build toward larger ones.

#### Examples

- In one epic quest, the Big P Plot is destroying the ring and the enemy's power. A little p plot beat is disguising yourself to slip past guards.
- In one ensemble adventure, the Big P Plot is chasing a McGuffin to save the world. The little p plot that the film actually runs on is whether these people can become friends.

#### Using the distinction

- Write the Big P Plot as one or two sentences. If you cannot, the book is not yet focused.
- Check that each chapter's little p plot advances a promise of the Big P Plot, or makes a promise of its own that the book keeps. See `plot-promise-progress-payoff`.
- The little p plot can give a tone promise or act as a microcosm of the larger story, so the Big P Plot can arrive at the end of act one without the opening feeling aimless. See `plot-cold-open-microcosm`.
- The soul of a story is often in the little p plot, not the headline plot. See `plot-types-of-progress`.

#### Checks

- `plot.bigp.01` State the Big P Plot in one or two sentences. If you cannot, the book is not focused yet.
- `plot.bigp.02` For each chapter, name its little p plot and whether it advances a Big P promise or makes a promise of its own that the book keeps.
- `plot.bigp.03` Where does the story's emotional core actually live, in the headline plot or in a little p thread, and do the signposts follow it?

## 29. plot-story-circle

*Use when you want a map for a transformation story, a hero's journey or monomyth feels too vague or too rigid, a character arc needs a shape, or a series needs nested arcs. Gives the eight-step Story Circle, how to bend it into tragedy, and how to avoid turning it into a checklist.*

The Story Circle is a simplified hero's journey. An ordinary person who expects life to stay the same is pushed into an adventure, is tested and changed, and comes home different, usually improving their society. Because the shape returns to its start, the change is visible: the character could not do something at the beginning and now has mastered it. That contrast is where much of the payoff comes from.

#### The steps

1. **Rest.** The character is at the top of the circle, wanting more or content. A farm boy and a comfortable homebody both fit.
2. **The call and the refusal.** A need arrives that the character feels unable to meet, and they resist it.
3. **Crossing the threshold.** An event forces them in and removes the way back, such as the family dying.
4. **The road of trials.** They are tested and shown to lack skill or knowledge. This covers roughly the first half of the middle of the story.
5. **The meeting with the darkness.** A direct confrontation with something hard, often a parental figure or the character's own shadow.
6. **Transformation and price.** The character changes and pays for the gain, the "elixir" of knowledge or skill.
7. **Master of two worlds.** They command both the inner and the outer sides of the adventure.
8. **Return.** They go home changed and bring the gain back.

#### Using it

- Run it on your own story and mark which steps are present. A missing step is a question to answer, not an error.
- Track the circle of the character's growth, which can differ from the external goal. Destroying the superweapon is the goal; the farm boy becoming a trained fighter is the arc the signposts should follow.
- Circles can nest. One book can be a full circle that is also only the first threshold of a larger arc, and a sequel can be a failure and a retry.
- The shape can turn tragic. The hero refuses the last step, refuses the elixir or the return, and fails or dies. A flaw that is never let go drives that version.

#### Checks

- `plot.circle.01` Mark which steps of the circle are present. For each missing step, decide whether it is missing on purpose.
- `plot.circle.02` Name the character's growth circle separately from the external goal, and check that the signposts track the growth.
- `plot.circle.03` If a step stopped fitting the story, was it dropped instead of forced?
- `plot.circle.04` If the story ends tragically, which step does the character refuse, and what flaw drives the refusal?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- The model is vague enough to fit almost any story, which is why it is hard to avoid and why it tells you little by itself. Dodging it on purpose usually still leaves "the character learns and gets better".
- The older monomyth version is criticized by folklorists for weak sourcing, unacknowledged borrowing, forcing every tale into one thesis, and ignoring women. The useful lesson is that a theory like this can try too hard.
- A famous film series shows the danger of a checklist: bending a character's origin to hit a monomyth beat made the story worse. If a step stops fitting what you are writing, drop the step.
- Neither following nor avoiding the model should be a goal. Tell the story you want, and accept shared ancestry with other works.
- For a first book, leaning on an established shape is fine. Characters and setting stretch it toward your own.
- See `plot-three-act-structure` for the arrow-shaped alternative.

## 30. plot-three-act-structure

*Use when a plot sags in the middle, the story lacks a clear question driving it, you are unsure where the first act ends, what the midpoint should do, or where the lowest point goes. Gives the beats of three-act structure, the major dramatic question, and how loosely to apply them in prose.*

Three-act structure treats the story as an arrow aimed at a climactic explosion. It is built on conflict and things going wrong, where a circle is built on a return home. It is the dominant form for Western audiences and tends to suit shorter novels better than long ones.

#### The beats

1. **Act I.** Introduce the character, their need, and the status quo. Pose the **major dramatic question**: what question about this character will the story answer? In an adventure about a farm boy it might be whether he becomes a warrior and avenges his family. A cold open is not necessarily Act I. Use the question to find where Act I really starts.
2. **Inciting incident.** The event that sets off a cascade of consequences. It is different from the end of Act I, and it may not be strictly required.
3. **Plot Point 1.** After an attempt and a refusal, something happens after which life can never be the same. This ends Act I.
4. **Rising action.** Rocks are thrown at the character. Everything they try makes things worse, and they still lack the skill or knowledge to win. In a mystery, theories collapse and clues are missing.
5. **Midpoint.** A big twist, which can be a surprise or can be foreseen. It is a major escalation, a reframing of the story, or a deeply personal turn. In this formula it is the most important moment.
6. **Second half of the middle.** Attempts become more frantic and failure costs more, leading to the **all is lost** moment, the darkest point.
7. **The last realization.** Something learned or accomplished gives one more thing to try.
8. **Act III.** The last chance. No more trying, only the climactic success or failure that the whole story has pointed toward.

Because the midpoint splits the long middle, the structure is really four parts under a three-part name.

#### Checks

- `plot.threeact.01` Write the major dramatic question in one sentence. Is it posed by the end of Act I, and does the climax answer it?
- `plot.threeact.02` Name the point after which the character cannot go back.
- `plot.threeact.03` Does the midpoint change what the reader believes the story is about, escalate it, or make it personal?
- `plot.threeact.04` Does each attempt in the second act make things worse, or do the attempts merely repeat?
- `plot.threeact.05` Is there a low point that the last realization climbs out of?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Prose has no page-count obligation. No reader checks for a beat at a fixed fraction of the book, and screenwriters probably follow percentages less than people think. Seven-point and nine-point variants exist; pick the one that helps.
- It is a lens for diagnosis, not a requirement. An episodic exploration story can fit the form in some parts and not in others.
- Different media need different structures. An adaptation changes things for its medium, and there is no clear line between too faithful and too liberal.
- See `plot-story-circle` for the circular model with similar threshold and low-point beats.

## 31. plot-structure-model

*Use when you want to write a story in a pattern you love, like a heist, an underdog sports team, or a revenge tragedy, or when formulas feel like recipes you follow without understanding. Gives the Structure Model method of studying a narrow pattern across many works and building your own version.*

Instead of following a broad template, study one narrow pattern across many works, find which plot points make it work, and rebuild it for your own story. The goal is to become someone who understands why a recipe works and can adapt it, rather than someone who only follows steps.

#### Steps

1. **Pick a narrow pattern.** It should be smaller than a genre: a particular type of heist, an underdog sports story, a revenge story in the shape of Hamlet.
2. **Gather many examples.** Read and watch widely. A set of films and novels about underdog teams with a coach, across very different settings, all share one skeleton.
3. **Ask what makes each work.** List the recurring points: the ragtag assembly, the coach with a past, the first humiliating loss, the team finding its own style.
4. **Keep what works.** Build out those points. They do not all need to appear.
5. **Drop what you dislike.** Cut any point you find stale or false.
6. **Adapt.** Move the result to a new medium, setting, or aesthetic, and raise the stakes if the original's were low. A sports-team skeleton can carry a squad of soldiers in an epic fantasy.

Expect the study to take time. Years of reading may precede using the shape for one subplot, and that is normal.

#### Choosing a method

- Pick whichever approach reliably gets you the work you want at your current skill. Planning and discovery are both legitimate.
- This method helps discovery writers too, because it supplies a skeleton without forcing an outline.
- A writer new to the craft should not try to hold all models in mind at once. Write consistently, add one focus at a time, and use the models later as diagnostics or outlining aids. Skills become automatic with practice.
- Tropes are unavoidable. Trying to escape all of them is a trap. Overuse of influences shows most in a first book and fades as you find your own voice.

#### Checks

None. This is a method for studying and building a structure, not a test to run on a draft.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Any structure treated as sacred does damage. If your outline stops fitting the story you are now writing, discard it and keep going.
- Knowing the points makes foreshadowing easier, but discovery writers can also add or fix structure in revision.
- Studying a pattern does not mean copying it. The study should leave you with understood principles, not a tracing.
- See `plot-three-act-structure` and `plot-story-circle` for broader templates to compare against.

## 32. plot-archetypes

*Use when choosing what kind of story to tell, a plot feels generic, you want to combine genres such as heist, romance, mystery, and revolution, a friendship or alliance needs to turn into a betrayal, or a story needs to change shape midway. Gives a way to build a plot from layered archetypes and to make an arc go wrong on purpose.*

Most stories are built from a few recognizable plot types, each of which can serve as an arc: relationship, romance, mystery or information, heist, master and apprentice, revolution, cat and mouse. A novel usually layers several. Mistborn, for example, interlocks a heist, an apprenticeship, a mystery, and a romance.

#### Choosing and combining

1. List the archetypes your story could use. Study many stories you like, distill them into types and subtypes, and note which ones excite you.
2. Decide which archetype carries the big P Plot and which ones belong to character arcs and other little p plots. See `plot-big-p-little-p`.
3. Try combinations, including swapping the genre of a familiar plot. Expect some failed tries before a combination works; the book that became Mistborn was a second attempt.
4. Choose the subtype that fits your intended feel. Two heist shapes are common. In one, the plan is thrown off by a disruption and the crew reuses what they built to recover. In the other, the plan runs brilliantly with small complications, and tension comes from one member who does not know the full plan.

#### Shifting archetypes midway

A strong move is to change the dominant archetype when things go wrong and the scope grows. A heist can widen into a revolution. A whodunit can turn into cat and mouse, as in Knives Out, and The Fugitive runs cat and mouse over an underlying whodunit. Plan the shift so that the earlier archetype's setup is reused instead of dropped. Signal the change early enough that readers who came for the first shape are not blindsided.

#### Every arc can go wrong

Each archetype has a failure version. An information arc can end with the characters reaching the wrong conclusion. A relationship arc can sour. Decide deliberately whether an arc is headed for success or failure, and promise accordingly.

#### The betrayal arc

A betrayal is a relationship plot built to fail, and it needs the same care as a good twist. It should be surprising and also feel inevitable.

1. Show early that the two characters work well together. You may skip the getting-to-know-you phase and start them as established friends.
2. Let their motivations drift apart gradually. Show friction through actions and choices while the protagonist's attention still treats the friend as a good friend.
3. Give the eventual turn a solid motivation, so a reader can think "I should have seen that."

Readers follow the viewpoint character's attention. If the protagonist trusts the friend, readers will tend to wave away the cracks, so the cracks can be planted in plain sight.

Check: list the three or four moments where the betrayer's goal visibly diverges from the protagonist's. Would each read as innocent on a first pass and damning on a second?

#### Checks

- `plot.archetypes.01` Name the archetypes the story layers, and which one carries the Big P Plot.
- `plot.archetypes.02` For each arc, has the story decided whether it heads for success or failure, and promised accordingly?
- `plot.archetypes.03` If the dominant archetype shifts midway, is the shift foreshadowed, and does it reuse the earlier archetype's setup?
- `plot.archetypes.04` For a betrayal, list the three or four moments where the betrayer's goal visibly diverges. Each should read as innocent on a first pass and damning on a second.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Archetypes are one way to build a story, not a requirement.
- A pure single-archetype plot can work, but a mid-book shift of type is what makes some stories stand out.
- Do not shift archetype without foreshadowing. A promised quest that abruptly becomes something else can lose both the readers who wanted the quest and the readers who would have liked the new shape.
- For how to schedule multiple archetype arcs across a book, use `plot-outline-interwoven-cycles`. For the relationship arc as a kind of progress, see `plot-types-of-progress`.

## 33. plot-scale-of-plot

*Use when you are unsure how big a story's plot should be, a book feels too small or too sprawling for its length, you are choosing between short story, novella, and novel, or you are planning chapter length and break points. Gives rules of thumb for matching scope to word count and reading experience.*

The scope of a story should fit its length and the way it will be read. A plot too large for the page count feels rushed, and a plot too small drags.

#### Steps

1. Pick the format: short story, novella, or novel.
2. Estimate the length, either from an outline or from your own sense of how the story grows.
3. Match the scope of the conflict to that length using the guides below.
4. Plan the chapters for the way the book will be read.

#### Rules of thumb

- **Short fiction** can follow one person through a single problem, or cover a whole life quickly in a novella. A Christmas Carol is a novella.
- **Around 80,000 to 100,000 words** suits thrillers and romances. A reader can finish one in a sitting or two, so pace the book to pull them through.
- **Above about 120,000 words**, readers expect scope to escalate. Harry Potter grows from school grades to saving the world as the books lengthen. A long book can also gain scope by spanning generations or a whole life.
- **Above about 150,000 words**, the story needs heavier weight to hold attention. Dune runs about 170,000 to 180,000 words and Lord of the Rings about 150,000 per volume.
- **Epic length** is read over days or weeks, so plot for natural break points where a reader can stop and return.

#### Chapter length

Short chapters pull readers along faster. Long chapters that work as complete units suit a book read in sessions. Shortening chapters in a climax speeds the reader through it.

#### Checks

- `plot.scale.01` Does the central conflict fit the word count for the format and genre?
- `plot.scale.02` If the book is long, where does the scope widen, and is the widening signposted?
- `plot.scale.03` Where can a reader put the book down, and what pulls them back?

If you cannot judge length yet, write the story and note how the finished count compares to what you expected. The sense improves with practice.

#### Cautions

These are figures from one author's experience and genre habits. Treat them as starting points. A long book with no escalation of scope tends to run out of momentum, which is the same problem `plot-yes-but-no-and` has when stretched. Use the outline and `plot-big-p-little-p` to see whether the large-scale and small-scale plots each have room to grow.

## 34. plot-try-fail-cycle

*Use when a character's victories feel too easy, a problem gets solved in one move, a middle section needs momentum, or you want to outline just a few big beats and discover the rest. Covers the Try-Fail Cycle and Points on the Map, plus when a run of successes can work instead.*

Before a character accomplishes something, they should generally fail at it first, and for a hard goal two or three times. Each failure shows the reader that the author considered the obvious solution and that the problem is bigger than it looked.

#### Running a cycle

1. Name the goal of this stretch of story (steal the plans, learn the Force, win the duel).
2. Have the character try the smartest thing available, and let them do it well. A foolish first attempt teaches the reader nothing.
3. Make it fail for a reason the reader can see. Either the character's flaw or missing skill sinks it, or the opposition anticipated the obvious move.
4. Let the failure change something: the character learns, the cost goes up, or the goal turns out to be larger. Then try again with what was learned.
5. Success arrives on a later attempt and should use the lesson the earlier failures taught.

Failures that teach the point-of-view character work best. Luke loses his hand fighting Vader, and the loss shows what he still lacks. A failure that happens off screen, to people the reader never sees, adds little.

#### Points on the Map

Outline only a handful of destination beats: the starting state, perhaps five big events, and the ending. Discover the road between them. Think of a road trip with a few fixed stops, where the route is open. Knowing the stops lets you foreshadow them, and a try-fail cycle is a good way to fill each stretch. A discovery writer can also add points after the first draft.

#### Checks

- `plot.tryfail.01` For each major success, what failed before it, and did the failure cost something or teach something?
- `plot.tryfail.02` Is any big problem solved on the first clever attempt?
- `plot.tryfail.03` Is it visible whether each failure comes from the character, the opposition, or both?
- `plot.tryfail.04` Do the failures differ in kind, not only in number?
- `plot.tryfail.05` If successes run in a long streak, are the stakes, the danger, and the competence strong enough to carry it?

#### When successes can carry the story

A long run of successes is possible, but it needs support:
- The stakes are so high that any failure would mean death or ruin.
- The danger and difficulty are played up, so the reader wonders whether this is the time the character falls.
- The competence is convincing.

Dune opens with Paul failing, then lets him succeed repeatedly, and the success is the tragedy. The approach is risky and stands out when done well. Competence stories often include a success that reveals a larger task, which widens the scope instead of ending the story.

#### Cautions

- A cycle repeated mechanically becomes a pattern the reader predicts. Vary what fails and why.
- Failures need to differ in kind, not only in number.
- Points on the Map is optional. Some writers do better outlining everything, and others fix structure in revision.

See `plot-yes-but-no-and` for a faster way to generate the complication that follows each failure.

## 35. plot-yes-but-no-and

*Use when you are stuck on what happens next, a scene resolves cleanly and the story goes flat, events feel disconnected, or you write by discovery and need a way to keep complications cascading. Covers the Yes-but / No-and method for chaining escalating problems.*

A story can be built as a chain of escalating incidents. State a problem, ask whether the character solves it, and answer with a complication attached. Each complication becomes the next problem.

#### The steps

1. Start with a concrete problem. Example: a farm boy needs a droid that has run away.
2. Ask whether the character solves it.
3. Answer one of two ways.
   - **Yes, but.** They succeed, and the success causes a new difficulty.
   - **No, and.** They fail, and the failure makes things worse.
4. Treat the complication as the next problem and repeat.

A plain "yes" ends the tension and a plain "no" stalls it. Keep the complication tied to what just happened, so events cascade instead of piling up.

#### A small run

A courier must deliver a letter before dawn.
- Does she get through the gate? Yes, but the guard recognizes her horse.
- Does she lose the guard? No, and he now follows her into the old quarter.
- Does she hide in the tannery? Yes, but the tanner is the man the letter accuses.

Three steps have produced a different story than the one the first line promised, and each step follows from the last.

#### Why it works

Small escalations make a big end state feel earned. A screwball plot can travel from a business trip to a room full of mobsters and absurd rocks, and the reader accepts it because no single step was a large jump. The method also applies to character: does the selfish character solve the need to be more caring? No, and she alienates the one person who was helping.

#### Where it fits

- It suits shorter fiction and is strong for getting from one point to the next.
- Over a very long book, constant escalation runs out of gas. Thrillers tend to sit at 80,000 to 100,000 words partly for this reason. In a longer book, use it inside sequences and let `plot-try-fail-cycle` and fixed destination beats shape the whole.
- It is a natural tool for discovery writers who want to see what the story does next.

#### Checks

- `plot.yesbut.01` Does each "but" or "and" grow out of the specific event before it?
- `plot.yesbut.02` Do the complications escalate in cost, or only change in kind?
- `plot.yesbut.03` Does each answer tell the reader something new about the character?
- `plot.yesbut.04` Are several plain wins in a row creating a lull?

#### Cautions

Wild complications are fine when the story has promised that tone. Without that promise, an absurd turn pulls the reader out. Use the method to generate options, then keep the ones that serve the promises the story has made.

## 36. plot-layering-conflict

*Use when a story has only outside action or only inner struggle, a character's arc feels sudden or unearned, a flaw frustrates readers, or you want a scene to carry both internal and external conflict. Covers layering internal and external conflict and signposting a character arc through the middle.*

Give the point-of-view character an outer problem and an inner one in the same scene, and let each make the other harder. A fight scene is stronger if winning requires the very thing the character cannot yet do.

#### Building the layers

Plan the inner thread so it reaches the climax with weight.

1. **Promise the change early.** Someone or something tells the reader what the character must learn, and the reader sees that they cannot do it yet. A mentor tells the farm boy to trust the Force instead of his eyes.
2. **Make the flaw legitimate.** Show why the character has it, and show a serious failure that results. The reader should believe the flaw is real and costly.
3. **Seed the beats evenly.** Return to the inner problem at intervals through the middle. Batching all character moments into a few chapters leaves long stretches where the arc seems forgotten.
4. **Acknowledge the flaw in the narrative.** Have the character, another character, or the narration notice it. Acknowledgment tells the reader the author sees the problem and is going somewhere.
5. **Finish with a one-two punch.** A bigger version of the earlier problem returns with higher stakes, and this time the character overcomes it. The trainee who flinched at a practice drone later fires on the real target with the whole fleet depending on him.

#### Pairing the layers in a scene

For each important scene, write down the outer goal and the inner struggle. Check that the outer obstacle presses on the inner flaw. If the two never touch, the scene is carrying only one layer.

#### Checks

- `plot.layering.01` For each important scene, name the outer goal and the inner struggle. Does the outer obstacle press on the inner flaw?
- `plot.layering.02` Where was the change promised, and where is the first serious failure caused by the flaw?
- `plot.layering.03` How many chapters pass between inner-conflict beats? Look for long gaps.
- `plot.layering.04` Does the climax return to the early problem in a larger form?
- `plot.layering.05` Would a reader understand why the character behaves this way, in terms they recognize from real people?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- The main risk is that readers dislike a character whose flaw frustrates them. Make the flaw understandable, tie it to something human, and let the narrative acknowledge it.
- Some characters never overcome the flaw. Readers will be frustrated, so give them other reasons to like the character. A little frustration can help.
- Heavy interior thought can turn into pages of rumination. Put inner conflict into choices and actions where possible.
- Match the arc's signposts to the arc the story is really about, which can differ from the outer goal. See `plot-big-p-little-p` and `plot-types-of-progress`.

#### Where other advice differs

Fiction craft advice often warns against narration that comments on a character's feelings or flaws, and asks writers to trust the reader to infer them. Step 4 above recommends acknowledging the flaw in the story.

Keep the acknowledgment, since readers need to know the author sees the problem. Deliver it through another character's remark, the character's own choice, or a consequence on the page. Use narration that names the flaw outright only when the reader has too little evidence to infer it. At the line level, lean toward showing and leaving the label out.

## 37. plot-twists-promise-and-wish

*Use when you want a twist that lands, a reveal feels random or cheap, or you are unsure whether the story needs a twist at all. Gives the promise-and-wish method (promise one thing, make the reader wish for another, deliver that) and the alternatives of escalation and over-delivery.*

A good twist is a swap the reader is happy to receive. Promise one thing, let the story show the reader something they would rather have, then deliver that instead. The reader should feel the new outcome is what they wanted all along, and should be able to look back and see it was signposted.

#### The three steps

1. **Promise something.** Say the toy under the tree is a toy car. State the expected outcome clearly: the hero will marry the planned match, the fairy tale will end happily, the mission will go to plan.
2. **Build the wish.** Through the middle, show the reader a better option. Make the second candidate warmer, funnier, or truer than the first, so the reader starts hoping you promised that one.
3. **Deliver the preferred thing.** The reveal hands over the toy plane. Signpost it early enough that it feels inevitable and not like an author trick.

A romantic comedy can promise a match with a fiancé while the real spark grows with his brother, until the audience wants the brother. A fairy-tale story can open on happily-ever-after, spend its second half cracking that ending until the audience no longer wants it, and then build something better in the wreckage.

#### Checks

- `plot.twist.01` What exactly did the story promise, and in which scene?
- `plot.twist.02` Where does the reader start to prefer the alternative? If nowhere, the twist is a surprise and not a swap.
- `plot.twist.03` Can you name two earlier moments that point to the real outcome?
- `plot.twist.04` Does the new outcome serve the theme and the character, or only startle?

#### Alternatives that need no twist

- **Straight delivery with complication.** A story can promise a marriage in its first line and deliver exactly that. The interest comes from obstacles and escalation, which matter as much as twists or more.
- **Over-delivery.** Promise a toy car and give a real car. Escalate the thing promised instead of replacing it.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Do not add a twist for its own sake. If the story does not need one, leave it out.
- Some readers resent the swap, especially when it breaks a beloved promise such as the fairy-tale ending. Expect a split reaction and decide whether the story gains enough.
- If the swap changes the kind of story midway, foreshadow the change early. See `plot-foreshadowing-twists`.
- For ways to make a straightforward outcome feel surprising, see `plot-payoff-deniability-forgotten-promise`. For the underlying model, see `plot-promise-progress-payoff` and `plot-kinds-of-promises`.

## 38. plot-payoff-deniability-forgotten-promise

*Use when a promised outcome is obvious and the payoff feels flat, when a rescue or victory is predictable, or when you want a last-minute arrival to get a cheer. Gives two ways to make a payoff surprising yet inevitable without a twist, plausible deniability and the forgotten promise.*

A payoff works when it is surprising yet inevitable. A twist is one way to get there (see `plot-twists-promise-and-wish`). Two techniques need no twist at all, because the outcome is exactly what was promised. They work by controlling what the reader believes at the moment of payoff.

#### Plausible deniability

Make the reader believe the promised outcome will not happen, then deliver it anyway.

1. Name the promised outcome: the hero lives, the siege is lifted, the couple gets together.
2. Stack obstacles, escalations, and red herrings until the outcome looks unlikely or lost.
3. Keep a thin, honest thread that still allows it, so the delivery feels fair and not cheated.

An example is a hero who is stung and left apparently dead while a companion must carry on alone. The reader doubts the promise but never has proof against it.

#### The forgotten promise

Set up a promise, then let the story run dark enough that reader and characters both forget it. When it pays off, the reader gets surprise and recognition together.

1. Plant a clear promise with a date, a signal, or a vow. A mentor tells the defenders to look for him at dawn on the fifth day.
2. Bury it under losses and pressure so it drops out of mind.
3. Pay it off at the lowest point. The relief lands harder because it was forgotten.
4. Optionally voice a short reminder as it arrives, so even readers who forgot understand it was set up.

A last-resort rescue that was set up this way gets cheers. A similar rescue whose rescuers were never set up beforehand lands as a convenient arrival and feels weaker.

#### Checks

- `plot.payoff.01` Which promise is being paid off, and where was it planted?
- `plot.payoff.02` At the moment of payoff, what does the reader currently believe will happen? If the answer is "exactly this", add deniability or let the promise fade first.
- `plot.payoff.03` If the promise is meant to be forgotten, is it planted clearly enough that it still reads as inevitable?
- `plot.payoff.04` Are the obstacles that create doubt honest, and not tricks that make the outcome impossible or the hero foolish?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Deniability needs honest obstacles. A red herring that makes the hero look incompetent or the outcome impossible will read as a trick.
- Forgetting needs a real setup. A rescuer with no earlier mention is a deus ex machina, not a forgotten promise.
- Some audiences prefer ambiguous endings to payoff endings, so match the technique to the tradition and readers you write for.
- See `plot-promise-progress-payoff` for the core model and `plot-foreshadowing-twists` for tuning how visible the setup is.

## 39. plot-cheer-moment-sanderlanche

*Use when a climax feels flat, several plotlines all end at once and none lands, an ending is numb or overstuffed, or you want readers to cheer. Gives the stand-up-and-cheer moment, the order of internal then external payoff, and how to stagger a cascade of payoffs.*

A stand-up-and-cheer moment is a payoff the reader has been waiting for. It lands because something promised finally happens. A Sanderlanche is a run of such payoffs near the end of a book, each one triggering the next.

#### Building the cheer

1. **Promise it early.** Show readers what the triumph could look like, such as a weak hero who will become strong. Readers will then tolerate the character being beaten down on the way.
2. **Punish before paying.** Sustained setbacks make success feel due. A heavy cost can earn a cheer even with a modest arc.
3. **Land the internal payoff first.** The character makes the emotional decision, such as trusting a friend or an ability, and that choice carries them into success on the external problem. A returning ally and a hero who trusts their instinct can both decide, and then win because both decided.
4. **Then the external payoff.** The physical victory follows the choice.

Internal first, external second is a rule of thumb. It can be reversed when the story reads better that way.

#### Merging several arcs

When overlapping arcs all converge on one moment, check that each arc has its own distinct beat in the sequence. You may not get the convergence right in the first draft, or the fifth.

#### Staggering the cascade

- Too many awesome events at once produce numbness. Space the payoffs in an order that builds.
- Rank them. Each should be larger than or different from the last.
- Give a minor character their climax earlier, around the two-thirds mark, so they can step aside and not compete with the main character at the end.
- In revision, cut payoffs and twists if too many pile up. A cascade can overreach.

#### Checks

- `plot.cheer.01` Name the promise this cheer moment pays off, and where it was made.
- `plot.cheer.02` What internal decision comes before the external win? The reverse order is fine when it reads better.
- `plot.cheer.03` If several plotlines resolve in one scene, does each get its own distinct beat?
- `plot.cheer.04` Is each payoff in a cascade larger than or different from the last, and not only louder?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A late cascade works best when the earlier chapters built the arcs it resolves. For making promises and signposting progress, see `plot-promise-progress-payoff` and `plot-types-of-progress`.
- If the cascade surprises, see `plot-payoff-deniability-forgotten-promise`. For where the biggest moment sits in the book, see `plot-climax-placement-aftermath`.

## 40. plot-climax-placement-aftermath

*Use when the best scene in the book arrives in the middle, the ending feels weaker than a midpoint climax, a second plotline drags beside a stronger one, or you are unsure how long the aftermath should run. Gives three options for a strong early climax and notes on resolution length.*

A book sometimes contains a strong climax well before the end, such as at the midpoint or three-quarters. The risk is that everything after reads as a letdown. Pick a response deliberately.

#### Three options

1. **Move the climax to the end.** Restructure so the strongest payoff sits last. Try this first if the reorder is simple.
2. **Raise the ending.** Strengthen the final climax until it matches or beats the early one. This can mean weeks of rebuilding plotlines, and it often means making the weaker viewpoint more engaging so the two storylines balance. Practice this option the most, because it also improves your writing.
3. **Accept it as a quirk of the book.** If the early climax is the best plot payoff while the emotional and narrative climax truly belongs at the end, you can keep both and let the book be lopsided on purpose.

#### Checks

- `plot.climax.01` Name each climax and what it pays off. Is an early one a plot payoff and the late one an emotional payoff?
- `plot.climax.02` If one viewpoint or plotline is far stronger than another, is strengthening the weaker one the fix?
- `plot.climax.03` Did early readers feel the real ending where you intended it?
- `plot.climax.04` Does the aftermath fit the format and deliver the last small promises?

#### Aftermath

Longer resolutions are generally fine and readers often like them, as long as they fit the format. A novel can give a full chapter or more of winding down after the climax. A flash piece cannot afford a long coda. Use the aftermath to deliver the last small promises: relationships settled, costs counted, a hint of what comes next.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Do not worry about a second book being weaker than a first. Make the first as strong as you can and treat that as a later problem.
- A strong minor-character climax placed earlier is a deliberate tool, not a flaw. See `plot-cheer-moment-sanderlanche`.
- In a series, a failed or partial goal can satisfy if readers know another installment is coming, if the failure feels deserved or fitting, or if a small win remains. Decide which feeling you want, since "they will try again" and "they had it coming" make different stories.
- Do not save the best material for later books. Put it in the first, and fulfil at least one primary character arc while leaving larger matters open.
- Match the final progress to the kind the story measures. See `plot-types-of-progress` and `plot-promise-progress-payoff`.

## 41. plot-foreshadowing-twists

*Use when beta readers guessed the twist, nobody saw it coming, a reveal feels unearned, or you are adding foreshadowing in revision. Gives a target for how many readers should guess, fixes for both extremes, and ways to hide a clue in plain sight.*

Foreshadowing is the setup that makes a payoff or twist feel earned. It is easy to adjust in revision, so a draft with a surprise at the end can be fixed later, including by writers who discover the story as they go.

#### The target

Aim for most readers to guess the twist only a page or a paragraph before the reveal. Some will guess early, which cannot be avoided. A twist that no reader could possibly have guessed is a complication, not a twist, because nothing was signposted.

#### Steps

1. List every reveal or payoff that must feel earned.
2. For each, mark where the reader first gets a clue and how many clues there are.
3. Add or cut scenes and lines to move the guess point toward the target.
4. Give early readers the draft and ask them to say when they first guessed and what gave it away.

#### Fixing the extremes

- **Nobody saw it.** Add clues. Turning foreshadowing up is easy.
- **Everyone guessed in the first act.** Dial the clues back, but note that turning foreshadowing down is harder, and a red herring is harder still to insert. Alternatively, convert the twist into suspense: let the reader know the secret and watch the characters walk into it. Make sure the characters do not look foolish for missing what the reader sees.

#### Ways to hide a clue

- Use genre and narrative expectations. Readers follow tropes and look where stories usually look.
- Mention it three times before it matters, so the third time reads as familiar.
- Follow the characters' attention. Readers look where the viewpoint looks, so a clue the viewpoint ignores slips by.
- Have a character remark on an oddity. A character who notices the strange detail and shrugs it off lets the reader shrug too.
- Show a character's quality through other people's eyes, which lets you build a reputation without the character boasting.
- Highlight an object that seems significant to use as a red herring for a different payoff.

#### Checks

- `plot.foreshadow.01` For each reveal that must feel earned, where does the reader get the first clue, and how many clues are there?
- `plot.foreshadow.02` Is the likely guess point a page or a paragraph before the reveal, and not "never" or "the first act"?
- `plot.foreshadow.03` If readers guess too early, would turning the twist into suspense serve better than hiding it harder?
- `plot.foreshadow.04` Does a shocking event serve theme, character, and plot, or only shock?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Shock for its own sake needs no skill. Shocking deaths can work when the theme supports them, but the value is in how characters respond. Treat them as complications unless they serve theme, character, and plot.
- A well-foreshadowed twist that readers spot and still enjoy makes a durable book. Do not rewrite a sequel to dodge what early readers guessed, as that tends to hurt the series.
- If the story will change kind midway, such as from classic quest to deconstruction, signal the direction in the promises at the start. Without that, you risk losing both audiences.
- Pair with `plot-twists-promise-and-wish` and `plot-payoff-deniability-forgotten-promise`. Core model is `plot-promise-progress-payoff`.

## 42. plot-outline-interwoven-cycles

*Use when you need to outline a book with several plotlines, viewpoints, a romance or a mystery thread, you lose track of promises across a long draft, or you are unsure how outlining fits your writing style. Gives a method for listing each plot cycle's progress beats and merging them into parts, for outline-first and discovery writers.*

A plot cycle is one thread with its own promise, progress, and payoff. A book is several cycles woven together. The outline is a tool for tracking all those promises and their progress in one place.

#### The method

1. **Identify the cycles.** Usually one internal arc per major character, plus the main external plot. A big book may have one main plot and two sub-main plots. Add any character-interaction plot, such as a romance, and any information plot, such as how the magic works. An apprenticeship is also a cycle, for example a character who must master a skill without a teacher.
2. **List each cycle's beats.** Start at the end, with the culminating payoff for that cycle, then work backward. Add signposts that show progress, backslides, and moments where it looks doomed.
3. **Fold the lists together.** Merge them into sections or parts. Mark the end of a part with a big complication or a major achievement.
4. **Order them as a long bullet list.** Use plot headings and keep the list unnumbered at first. Turn it into chapters while you write.

#### Checks

- `plot.cycles.01` Does every cycle have a payoff, with visible progress between its promise and its payoff?
- `plot.cycles.02` Do the big payoffs land one after another, not all at once?
- `plot.cycles.03` Is any minor character's cycle better resolved earlier, so it does not compete with the lead at the climax?
- `plot.cycles.04` Is every promised thread present in the merged outline?

#### Complexity and logistics

- Each added viewpoint multiplies the work. One viewpoint over a long book is easy. Two is hard. Two viewpoints in the same plot is manageable, while two on different worlds compounds the difficulty far more than doubling it.
- If armies or travelers move, keep a map or a distance sense so they do not teleport, unless established magic accounts for logistics.
- Use whatever tool suits you, such as a word processor with a navigation pane, a notebook, or dedicated writing software.

#### For outline-first writers

Do the full pass before drafting. Treat the list as a map that can change. Expect the first merge of cycles to be wrong, and revise it as you learn what the draft needs.

#### For discovery writers

You can still use the method. Write a draft or a section first, then list the cycles you actually wrote, work out their beats, and see which promises have no progress or payoff. This is also the stage to add setup and foreshadowing, which is easier to add later than to remove. Another option is a short, time-limited planning pass that identifies only the cycles and their endings, leaving the middle open.

New writers can try several approaches, including improvising a whole book, then settle on a mix that works for them. A writer who has a working method does not need to change it.

#### Cautions

- Getting the convergence of several arcs right may take more than one draft.
- The outline is for tracking, not for locking in. A cycle that turns out dull can be reshaped or cut.
- For how to choose the cycle types, use `plot-archetypes`. For the underlying promise structure, use `plot-promise-progress-payoff`.

## 43. plot-causal-bridges

*Use when events follow one another but do not cause each other, an outline reads as a list of interesting incidents, a turn feels arbitrary, or a character does something because the plot needs it. Tests each major turn for why this person, why now, why this method, and what it changes.*

Events in order are not automatically a story. A plot holds when each turn is caused by the one before it, through someone's choice.

#### The bridge

Between any two major turns, the chain should be readable:

new pressure or evidence, then a specific actor interprets it, then pursues a goal, then picks a method, then the choice changes someone else's options, then a visible consequence creates the next event.

At each turn, ask: **Why this person? Why now? Why this method? What was already true? What did this decision newly cause?**

#### The removal test

If taking an event out would not change the next decision, either the bridge is missing or the event is not on the story's spine. Keep it only if it earns something else (a promise, a character beat).

#### Building the spine

- **Cause before system.** Write the causal story first: the starting situation, how it begins through someone acting, the turns, the climax, and the resulting state. Explain the machinery behind it afterward.
- **Start through an actor.** The first on-page change is an assignment, an encounter, or a mistake, not a thematic statement like "the hero enters a hidden order."
- **Anchor before use.** Establish a place, group, system, or important person before asking it to carry an event. Readers should not meet the followers before the organization exists, or a victim before their situation is known.
- **No summary jumps.** "Attention grew, so the villain picked a victim" hides everything. Say what pressure the attention created, what the villain wanted, why this victim served that, and who acted.

#### Checks

- `plot.bridges.01` For each major turn, can you name the pressure, the actor who interprets it, their goal, and their method?
- `plot.bridges.02` Why this person, why now, and why this method?
- `plot.bridges.03` If this event were removed, would the next decision change? If not, it is off the spine or a bridge is missing.
- `plot.bridges.04` Does any turn rest on a summary jump (A happened, so B) with no one choosing?
- `plot.bridges.05` Is every place, group, or person established before it carries an event?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Discovery writers can find the bridges in revision. A missing one is a question to answer, not a failure.
- A coincidence can start trouble. One that solves it needs setup; see `plot-payoff-deniability-forgotten-promise`.
- For generating the next complication, see `plot-yes-but-no-and`. For what the reader is promised along the way, see `plot-promise-progress-payoff`.

## 44. plot-reveal-boundaries

*Use when a story has secrets, a mystery, or a hidden system, you are unsure when readers should learn what, or an outline explains the mechanism before the story makes anyone care. Separates what is true, what each character knows, and when the reader learns it.*

A story with secrets runs on three different things that are easy to blur: what is actually true, what each character knows, and what the reader has been told so far.

#### The layers

- **Starting state.** The minimum situation that makes the story possible.
- **The story as the reader meets it.** The causal run of events, in the order readers learn them.
- **Author truth.** What is actually true, including things the story never shows.
- **Reveal boundaries.** At each point, what the reader knows and what each character knows.

Tell the causal story first. Explain the hidden system only once the spine is clear and a reader has reason to want the explanation.

#### Planning each secret

For every secret, write down:

1. the true fact
2. who knows it
3. who is wrong about it, and how
4. where the reader first gets a clue
5. where the reader learns it
6. what changes when they do

A reveal that arrives before its setup, or a setup that nothing ever rewards, is a defect. Keep the author-truth notes separate from the chapter text.

#### Keeping the boundary

Do not let author truth leak into narration the viewpoint character could not know. When the reader must know something a character does not, show it deliberately (dramatic irony) instead of letting it slip.

#### Checks

- `plot.reveals.01` For each secret, is the true fact written down apart from what the reader is told?
- `plot.reveals.02` For each secret, can you name who knows it, who is wrong about it, where the reader gets the first clue, and where they learn it?
- `plot.reveals.03` Does any reveal come before its setup, or any setup go unrewarded?
- `plot.reveals.04` Does narration leak author truth the viewpoint character could not know?
- `plot.reveals.05` Does the outline tell the causal story before explaining the hidden system?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A mystery left deliberately unresolved is allowed. Mark it as deliberate so it is not read as a gap.
- For tuning how visible a clue is, see `plot-foreshadowing-twists`. For swapping a promised outcome for a better one, see `plot-twists-promise-and-wish`.

## 46. character-proactive-relatable-capable

*Use when a character feels boring, wooden, or hard to root for and you can't say why, when planning a character arc, or when deciding what a character should be strong or weak at. Gives three sliding scales (proactive, relatable, capable) for diagnosing a character and for describing an arc as movement along them.*

Compelling characters are built from a mix of three attributes. Each is a sliding scale, and a character sits at some point on each one.

- **Proactive**: the character makes the plot move. This is the biggest single factor in whether a character is readable, because readers bond with whoever makes things happen.
- **Relatable**: the reader can step into the character's shoes and cares what happens to them. Without it the stakes drop, because nobody minds if the character dies.
- **Capable**: readers enjoy watching competent people do things competently.

Few characters max out all three, though some do. Details live in `character-proactivity-toolkit`, `character-relatability-toolkit`, and `character-capability-design`.

#### Using the scales

1. Place your character on each scale at the start of the story, roughly low, middle, or high.
2. Use a rough rule of thumb, never applied rigidly: one scale high, one moderately low, one growing as the story goes. A character high on everything has nowhere to go.
3. Decide on purpose which scale is the place the character learns, which is already a strength, and which stays weak as a defining trait.
4. Remember that each scale has sub-areas. A person can be proactive about teaching and organizing a team and passive about saving the world in a video game. Place the character per domain when the answer matters.

A character can be strong on two scales and still work. The lead of a survival drama can be proactive and capable and among the least relatable people on the cast, and the story can still be built around him. It then needs the other scales or the supporting cast to carry the reader's warmth.

#### Arcs as movement along the scales

An internal arc is a character ending at a different point on at least one scale.

- **Relatability rises** as the character works on a flaw and the reader cheers when it is overcome.
- **Capability rises** in apprentice stories, such as a farm boy learning the Force.
- **Proactivity rises** when the first act is about learning to act, or when the midpoint marks the turn from reacting to driving.
- A scale can also fall. A trusted helper who slides downward over a long series makes a painful, effective arc.

Purely internal arcs usually map onto these scales. External problems and relationship arcs are separate kinds and need their own handling.

#### Checks

- `character.scales.01` Place the character low, middle, or high on proactive, relatable, and capable, per domain where it matters.
- `character.scales.02` Which scale is lowest? A passive character needs a want and a first action, a cold one a reason to be liked, an unimpressive one something they are visibly good at.
- `character.scales.03` Which scale does the character's arc move along, and is the movement shown on the page?
- `character.scales.04` Is any character high on all three scales with nowhere left to go?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Slow-progress stories can work. A quiet, drawing-room fantasy of manners does not need the drive of a spy thriller.
- Overpowered or divine characters are still read through these scales. Give them relatable wants and a clear limit, and use them sparingly as viewpoints. See `character-capability-design` and `character-flaws-restrictions-limitations`.
- No trait relates to every reader, so judge relatability against the readers you are writing for.
- Villains usually sit high on proactive and capable and low on relatable. See `character-villain-and-antihero-patterns`.
- For a first look at what makes a character memorable on the page, see `character-iconic-characters` and `character-presenting-a-character`.

## 47. character-proactivity-toolkit

*Use when a protagonist is passive, reluctant, imprisoned, depressed, or stalling the plot, when the villain is more interesting than the hero, or when a character feels wooden. Gives techniques for building and restarting proactivity, from a first want to small acts of initiative.*

Readers bond with the character who makes the story happen and get frustrated with one who stalls it. Opening on a character taking action is one of the quickest ways to make them readable.

#### Start with a want

- Give the character a want from the first scene, even a small one such as a cup of coffee. The want is what turns events into actions.
- A wooden or lifeless character is more often a motivation problem than a dialogue problem. Check whether the reader has been shown what the character wants and how their actions follow from it before rewriting lines.
- Give each character several wants, some of them in tension. Let those wants drive choices, so that when a desire shifts the shift makes sense.
- When a motivation changes, establish the new one on the page. A character who flips goals without setup breaks.
- Small wants can stand in for large ones. A hero who does not yet want to save the world can love their family, and that love makes the later choice believable.

#### The reluctant hero

Heroes often have to be pushed into action. Plan where the push lands: commonly the end of the first act or the midpoint. Until then, plant a seed of initiative. The character solves a minor problem cleverly, or turns back to help one stranger. That is enough to mark them as someone who acts.

#### When the villain is more active than the hero

Readers will bond with the villain if the villain drives events while the hero reacts. Raise the hero's proactivity with the techniques in this skill, give them their own plan, and let their choices cause the villain's setbacks. See `character-villain-and-antihero-patterns`.

#### Restricted characters

- **External limits** such as prison: show them trying something, planning, or scheming. They can work up to bigger action over time.
- **Internal limits** such as depression are the hardest. Stasis is what fails, so give the state motion, even downward motion. Or accept that proactivity is unavailable and lean on the other two scales. Or give the character control over a small sphere where their choices matter.

#### Restarting a stalled character

- Cut to another viewpoint who carries the action or drags the stalled one along.
- Save the last bit of growth for the end of the second act. Something the character has learned returns and prompts a final push into the third.

#### Pairing proactive characters

Two characters with competing agendas make tension, especially when one is more capable and the audience suspects the other is right. In a cat-and-mouse chase both leads act, and the reader follows both.

#### Checks

- `character.proactive.01` What does the character want in their first scene, and does an action follow from it?
- `character.proactive.02` If the character reads as wooden, has the reader been shown what they want before the dialogue is blamed?
- `character.proactive.03` For a reluctant hero, where does the push land, and what small act of initiative comes before it?
- `character.proactive.04` Is the villain driving events while the hero only reacts?
- `character.proactive.05` When a motivation changes, is the new one established on the page?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A deliberately cruel choice still counts as proactive unless fear has frozen the character. Letting someone drown is an action. It lowers relatability instead, so check `character-relatability-toolkit`.
- A character can be proactive in one domain and passive in another. See `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
- To find what a character wants, see `character-motivation-discovery-questions`.
- Not every story needs a driving lead. Slow-progress stories can run on quieter engines.

## 48. character-relatability-toolkit

*Use when readers don't care about a character, a lead is unlikeable, stakes feel low because nobody minds if the protagonist dies, or a godlike character is hard to connect with. Gives techniques for making a character easy to step into and care about.*

A relatable character is one the reader can step into and wants to see succeed. Relatability keeps the stakes real: if the reader is not bothered when the character is hurt, danger carries no weight.

#### Quick techniques

1. **Show kindness or generosity early.** A character who uses their ability to help someone, with nothing to gain, becomes relatable almost at once. The reverse marks a villain: cruelty to the helpless tells the reader who to fear.
2. **Show the character through another character's eyes.** A friend, rival, or stranger who notices good traits does the work without the character praising themselves, which reads as arrogance. Use it lightly.
3. **Give self-awareness.** A character who knows their flaw and works on it is forgiven much. Do not overdo it, or the character seems to be lecturing the reader.
4. **Use ordinary, widely shared traits and wants.** A hatred of Mondays or a love of a favorite food gives readers something to hold. No trait works for every reader, so aim for the readers you have.
5. **Give a moral compass that matches the reader's.** It works best combined with self-awareness.
6. **Pull back competence.** Show an expert hurt, afraid, or out of their depth, and the reader steps closer. A reboot of a suave spy who bleeds and doubts is more approachable than the polished original.
7. **Draw on real people.** Passions and quirks of people you know are good seeds for traits, arcs, and settings.

#### Archetypes and quirks

Tropes and archetypes let readers sort a character quickly. Start with the familiar box, then subvert it with a reversal or widen the box as the story goes. Quirks work best when they expand or break the role, such as a hardened mercenary who knits, rather than random wackiness. See `character-overused-trait-trap` for what happens when a quirk becomes everything.

#### Godlike and divine characters

Motivation that crosses alien biology or divine nature carries empathy regardless of power. Care, being blocked from a goal, and wanting what someone else has are all relatable. Give gods relatable wants and clear limits, and use them sparingly as viewpoint characters, because bonding with someone who can do anything is hard. Distinctive prose can help. So can making the reader curious about what the figure cannot do. See `character-flaws-restrictions-limitations`.

#### Checks

- `character.relatable.01` Within the first chapters, what does the reader learn that makes them like or pity this character?
- `character.relatable.02` Does a trait carry the reader's sympathy, or is the narration telling them to feel it?
- `character.relatable.03` Is the likeable trait shown by action?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A flaw earns sympathy at first and wears it out if unresolved. See `character-flaws-restrictions-limitations`.
- Some characters work with low relatability when another scale is very high. See `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
- Likeability is not the same as virtue. Villains and antiheroes can be compelling at low relatability. See `character-villain-and-antihero-patterns`.
- To introduce a character on the page, see `character-presenting-a-character`.

## 49. character-capability-design

*Use when deciding what a character is good at, when a hero is either helpless or too perfect, when a competent character reads as dull or arrogant, or when strength is not about fighting. Gives rules for designing skills, strengths, and weaknesses that drive the plot.*

Readers enjoy watching competent people do things competently. A heist crew, a master detective, and a veteran spy all draw on that pleasure. Capability is a scale, and the right setting depends on the story.

#### Design rules

1. **Choose one or two areas of excellence.** Make the character truly excellent there and weak in others. A character good at everything has no texture and no need for allies.
2. **Let early strengths advance the plot.** Show the skill solving a problem in the first act, so the reader trusts it.
3. **Exploit the weaknesses.** Build scenes in which what the character lacks decides what happens. A brilliant strategist who panics in a crowd forces the plot into crowds.
4. **Let capability grow when the arc calls for it.** Apprentice stories move the character up this scale as the main arc.

#### Capability beyond combat

A skill is anything that matters to the story's problem. A loyal gardener whose steadiness saves the quest, or a medic whose patience holds a team together, carries as much weight as a swordsman. If your story is about loyalty, loyalty can be the character's superpower and the thing that wins the ending.

#### The cost of too much

High capability tends to lower relatability, since readers cannot see themselves in someone who never struggles. Two ways to handle it.

- Pair the strength with vulnerability, a relatable want, or a visible limit. A spy who can do anything but must keep a promise to a dying friend is easier to follow.
- Write a story that is about being proactive and superb. Readers of such stories are there for the competence, and they will accept a high setting.

#### The overpowered or divine figure

What makes a nearly invincible character interesting is what they cannot do. Choose a restriction or limitation that matters to the story's central problem, and give them a relatable want. See `character-flaws-restrictions-limitations`. Consider using such a figure sparingly as viewpoint, as discussed in `character-relatability-toolkit`.

#### Checks

- `character.capability.01` Name the one or two things this character does better than anyone else in the story. Where does the plot use them?
- `character.capability.02` Name two things they do badly. Where does the plot punish that?
- `character.capability.03` If the character fails nothing, where would a reader worry?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Capability is one of three scales. A character can be capable and passive or capable and unlikeable. See `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
- A strength repeated in every scene can turn into a single-note trait. See `character-overused-trait-trap`.
- Villains are often highly capable. See `character-villain-and-antihero-patterns`.
- Magic abilities have their own design rules, covered in the magic worldbuilding skills.

## 50. character-flaws-restrictions-limitations

*Use when giving a character weaknesses, when a hero is too perfect, when a flaw is going stale, or when you are unsure whether a handicap should be fixed or worked around. Sorts weaknesses into flaws, restrictions, and limitations, each handled differently.*

Strong characters have expertise and weaknesses in roughly equal measure. "Weakness" covers three different things, and mixing them up produces arcs that go nowhere or stories that cheat.

#### The three kinds

1. **Flaw**: something the character needs to overcome. The arc is the overcoming. Examples are cowardice, pride, or an inability to trust.
2. **Restriction**: a constraint, imposed by the story or by the character, that they could get past but the audience does not want them to. A hero's moral code against killing is a restriction. Breaking it would be possible and would cost the reader something.
3. **Limitation**: something forced on the character against their will, often physical. A one-armed person who wants to play football has a limitation. The story is about working around it or living well despite it, and a cure would miss the point.

#### Sorting a weakness

Ask three questions about each weakness you plan to give a character.

- Could the character change this by choosing to? If yes, it is a flaw or a restriction. If no, it is a limitation.
- Does the reader want it to change? If yes, it is a flaw and the arc should address it. If the reader wants it to hold, it is a restriction.
- Is the story about fixing it, bearing it, or testing it? Fixing points to a flaw, bearing points to a limitation, and testing points to a restriction.

Then match the handling. Flaws get an arc. Limitations get ingenuity and workarounds. Restrictions get pressure scenes where the easy way out is available and the character declines, or occasionally takes it. Some stories explore what happens once a restriction finally breaks.

#### Using weakness to build relatability

Showing a flaw makes a character relatable early, because readers see something familiar in them. The effect decays. If the flaw blocks progress for too long without being addressed, it turns from an asset into a liability and readers lose patience. Plan when the flaw starts to move, and let the character show awareness of it or effort against it.

#### Weakness for the very strong

An overpowered character becomes interesting through what they cannot do. Where raw capability would flatten the story, give such a figure a restriction or limitation that bears on what the plot demands. Pair it with a relatable want, as discussed in `character-capability-design`.

#### Checks

- `character.weakness.01` For each weakness, is it a flaw, a restriction, or a limitation, and is it handled that way: an arc for a flaw, pressure scenes for a restriction, ingenuity for a limitation?
- `character.weakness.02` Is a limitation ever treated as a flaw the character should simply get over?
- `character.weakness.03` Does an unaddressed flaw block progress so long that readers lose patience?
- `character.weakness.04` For a very strong character, which restriction or limitation bears on what the plot demands?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Do not label a limitation a flaw. A character who "should just get over" a disability or an outside constraint reads as a lesson in blame.
- A trait that dominates the character and is repeated until it grates is a different problem. See `character-overused-trait-trap`.
- To see how flaws, restrictions, and limitations raise or lower each of the three scales, see `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
- Self-awareness helps with flaws. See `character-relatability-toolkit`.

## 51. character-villain-and-antihero-patterns

*Use when the villain is more compelling than the hero, a villain feels flat or cartoonish, you are writing an antihero, or you want a villain's fall to feel earned. Gives patterns for where villains and antiheroes sit on the three scales and how to build them.*

Villains and antiheroes are best understood by their positions on the scales in `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.

#### The classic villain position

A typical villain is high on proactive, low on relatable, and often high on capable. The villain has a plan and the means, so the villain drives the plot.

**The villain problem.** When the villain acts and the hero reacts, readers bond with the villain. The fix is to raise the hero's proactivity so they pursue goals and cause setbacks. See `character-proactivity-toolkit`.

#### Making the villain stronger

- Put the villain slightly higher on relatability than the classic. A reader who can follow the logic of a villain's wants feels the conflict more.
- Mark villains with cruelty to the helpless early, where you want readers to fear and reject them. A moment of kindness to someone small makes a character relatable. The inverse marks a villain.
- Use the trajectory pattern. The villain begins relatable, makes a morally forbidden choice driven by a tragic flaw, and relatability falls from there. The fall works because the reader remembers the start. Plan the choice, the flaw behind it, and the point of no return.

#### Hero versus protagonist

A **hero** does virtuous things. A **protagonist** is the one we follow and want to succeed. They need not be the same person. Decide which each of your leads is.

#### Two antihero shapes

1. **Modern pop-culture antihero.** High proactive and capable, low relatable, set against people who are worse. Readers root for them because the opposition is worse. The sliders keep them watchable, but check that the people against them are clearly worse.
2. **Classical literary antihero.** The opposite: a protagonist who refuses to act. Their passivity is the point, and the story explores what that costs them.

#### The true antihero

A true antihero also does villainous things, and the story takes the moral weight of those acts seriously. Grimdark stories do this. A character who is merely rude and wears a scowl does not qualify. Ask what the character does that costs someone else, and whether the narrative treats the cost as real.

#### Checks

- `character.villain.01` Where does the villain sit on each scale? If low on proactive, why would they threaten anyone?
- `character.villain.02` Does the reader understand any of the villain's want?
- `character.villain.03` For an antihero, who is worse than them, and does the story show it?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A moral line that the character will not cross reads as a restriction. See `character-flaws-restrictions-limitations`.
- Decide early whether a lead is meant to be a hero, a protagonist, or both, because the story promises different things for each.
- Some readers will find a given sympathetic trait unrelatable. See `character-relatability-toolkit`.
- For the pull of a memorable antagonist, see `character-iconic-characters`.

## 52. character-overused-trait-trap

*Use when a character's one defining quality keeps repeating, readers say a character is annoying or one-note, a quirk feels like random wackiness, or a relatable trait has turned into the whole personality. Gives a check for spotting and fixing an overplayed trait.*

A relatable trait is a strength until it becomes the only thing the character is. Then readers stop being charmed and begin to be irritated. Think of a character whose single relatable motivation, say worry about his child, is invoked in every scene until the audience dreads his appearances. A comic exaggeration: a character who responds to every situation with a lunch order.

#### How it happens

1. A trait works in an early scene, so the writer reuses it.
2. The trait becomes the character's answer to everything.
3. Other wants, skills, and contradictions never appear, so there is nothing else to enjoy.
4. The reader starts anticipating the trait and resenting it.

#### Checks

- `character.trait.01` List the traits the reader has seen for each recurring character. How many scenes in a row has one trait been the driver?
- `character.trait.02` What else does this character want, and has it ever competed with the trait?
- `character.trait.03` Could the trait be absent from a scene without breaking the character? If not, the trait is carrying too much.

#### Fixes

- **Give competing wants.** A parent who is also ambitious and sometimes resents the child is a person, not a slogan. Wants that conflict create choices.
- **Rotate the spotlight.** Let a different trait or skill carry some scenes. Rest the main one so it lands when it returns.
- **Let the trait cost something and change.** An unresolved flaw that blocks the plot too long becomes a liability. See `character-flaws-restrictions-limitations`.
- **Move quirks from decoration to function.** Quirks work best when they expand or break the character's role, and they grate when they are random. A hardened soldier who restores clocks tells us about patience. A character who is just loud and strange tells us nothing.
- **Vary the domain.** Check which area of the character's life each scene touches. See the sub-areas idea in `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.

#### Archetypes

Archetypes help readers sort a character quickly, and that is the right starting point. The trap is staying in the box. Once the reader has sorted the character, widen the box over time or subvert it with a reversal.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Repetition is useful for a comic side character who appears briefly. The danger rises with screen time and with viewpoint characters.
- Use the same check on strengths. A trademark skill used in every scene turns into a one-note capability. See `character-capability-design`.
- Relatability traits in `character-relatability-toolkit` should be varied and shown by action, not repeated.
- Giving a character a distinct first impression is a separate task. See `character-presenting-a-character`.

## 53. character-iconic-characters

*Use when a series hero or detective never seems to change, an editor says the protagonist has no arc, or you are writing episodic adventures, mysteries, or a long-running villain. It explains how to build a character whose appeal is competence and cost instead of growth.*

An iconic character has no internal arc that defines who they are, and stays much the same from story to story. James Bond, Dirk Pitt, the Hardy Boys, and Hercule Poirot work this way. Their stories can often be read in any order, and the tension comes from outside pressure instead of from a lesson the character must learn.

Arcs remain the usual recommendation. Iconic is a valid alternative for stories where it fits better, not an excuse for an undeveloped lead.

#### What carries the character

1. **Competence and proactivity.** The audience trusts this person to act well, so they do not need to watch them learn. See `character-proactive-relatable-capable` for how to make those qualities land.
2. **A changed story question.** Replace "will they grow in time?" with "is this the time the villain is too capable for them?" and "what does saving the day cost them?"
3. **Cost.** The hero is strong enough to save most things but not everything, so endings are often bittersweet. Decide in advance what each story takes from them.
4. **Emotional depth anyway.** A Poirot novel can be moving without the detective overcoming a flaw. Put the depth in the victims, suspects, and the price of the solution.

#### Checks

- `character.iconic.01` Can a new reader start with this installment and follow the character? If yes, the traits are stable and clear enough.
- `character.iconic.02` Is the tension coming from a capable opponent or a real cost? If neither, the story has no engine, since the arc was removed.
- `character.iconic.03` Is any small arc inside this one story kept from becoming central to who the character is?
- `character.iconic.04` Is the lead memorable through a clear trait, voice, or signature skill?

#### Iconic villains and ensembles

A villain can be iconic too: a huge force with no explained motive, like Sauron. Pair that kind of villain with a relatable figure whose motive is simple and small, as Gollum is. The motive only needs internal logic, not outside justification.

In an episodic ensemble, such as the crew of a long-running TV series, making each lead iconic lets a viewer enter any episode without prior knowledge.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A flat lead with no competence, no cost, and no arc is just a weak character. Pick one of the supports above.
- If the story promises a transformation early, readers will expect it. Choose iconic before you make that promise.
- For arcs that do shift a character, see `character-presenting-a-character` for how motivation and values move.

#### Where other advice differs

General structural advice says every arc or installment should permanently change something, such as relationships, power, stakes, or the lead's understanding, and calls an installment that changes nothing filler. This skill allows installments that reset, because the lead stays fixed and the appeal is competence and cost. Default to this skill for episodic series and for detective or adventure leads that readers can enter in any order. Choose the other side when installments are meant to be read in sequence, when readers expect escalation across the series, or when the story has promised a transformation. Then let each installment leave a lasting mark, even if the lead's core stays the same.

## 54. character-first-entry

*Use when a new important character walks in and readers cannot picture or place them, a villain or love interest arrives without an impression, or the author knows who someone is but the page has not shown it. Gives the first-entry anchors: role, link to the scene, first impression, and one recognizable trait.*

The reader knows only what the page has shown. If the author knows who a person is, how they are related to someone, what a place looks like, or why a behavior matters, and the prose has not shown it, it is missing. A character who matters should not walk in bare.

#### The anchors

When an important character first enters in a way that counts, give the reader enough to place them:

1. **Identity or role.** Who or what they are in this world, in a form the reader can hold.
2. **Link to the moment.** How they connect to the current event or to the viewpoint character.
3. **First impression.** What the viewpoint character would actually register on meeting them, not a catalog.
4. **One recognizable trait**, for a villain, love interest, rival, or core ally. Pick from appearance, clothing, posture, voice, a habitual gesture, or how other people react to them.

Minor and walk-on characters need less: a role and one detail is enough. A deliberately mysterious figure can withhold who they are, but the reader still needs an impression and a reason to care.

#### Where to put it

Put the anchors in the moment of entry, not three pages later, and deliver them through what the viewpoint character notices and how they feel about it. Do not stop the scene for a description block. A reaction from someone else in the room is often the fastest anchor.

#### Checks

- `character.entry.01` At first entry, has the prose shown the character's role or identity, or has only the author known it?
- `character.entry.02` Is the character's link to the current event or to the viewpoint character clear?
- `character.entry.03` Does the reader get a first impression the viewpoint character would actually form?
- `character.entry.04` For a villain, love interest, rival, or core ally: is there at least one recognizable trait (appearance, clothing, posture, voice, a habitual gesture, or how others react)?
- `character.entry.05` Is anything about this character, their place, or their relationships assumed because the author knows it, and not shown?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Do not front-load a full description. One trait that sticks beats five that blur.
- A character introduced by name only, in passing, is a different case from an entrance. Judge by how much the story is about to ask the reader to care.
- For what to show about a character over time, see `character-presenting-a-character`. For delivering description through the viewpoint, see `worldbuilding-viewpoint-filtered-description`.

## 55. character-presenting-a-character

*Use when a character feels generic or interchangeable, characters announce their own motives in dialogue, you cannot tell how a character should act or speak, or you are introducing a new protagonist. It sorts what to show about someone into motivation, personality, and values.*

Three linked things tell a reader who a character is. Motivation is what they want. Personality is how they go about getting it. Values are what they will do or give up to get it. Convey all three, and keep them connected so each explains the others.

#### The three parts

1. **Motivation first.** It is the most important and usually grows out of what the story needs. Find it with `character-motivation-discovery-questions`.
2. **Personality as the surface over the motivation.** Ask how this person pursues the goal. Do they push, wait, hint, or never mention it? How do they show care: by looking after people, or by writing a check? Two characters with the same want should go after it differently.
3. **Values as the price.** Ask what they will sacrifice. A change in values is also what drives an arc, up or down, since a character who acquires a new value replaces the old motive.

#### Showing it on the page

- Personality comes through dialogue and the first actions a character takes. Early choices set expectations.
- Do not let characters state their own motives to people who already know them. A butler telling the maid what both already know is the warning sign.
- Dialect and verbal habits work in small doses, and they should hint at something deeper about the person.
- To show intelligence, skip the stereotype of no contractions and rare words. Use precise word choice, references, interrupting, a clear structure of claim and evidence, and sentences that build.
- A quirk works best when it reinforces the core trait. A quirk that just sits on top feels tacked on.
- Use early scenes as promises about what the text treats as a flaw to overcome and what it treats as a trait to keep.

#### Checks

- `character.presenting.01` Can you name the character's motivation, how their personality goes after it, and what they will give up for it?
- `character.presenting.02` Does any character state their own motive to someone who already knows it?
- `character.presenting.03` With dialogue tags and setting removed, could a reader tell who is speaking from the lines alone?
- `character.presenting.04` Does each quirk reinforce a core trait, instead of sitting on top of it?

A practice exercise: write a scene with three people whose wants conflict, remove the tags and setting, and rework wants and personalities until each speaker can be identified from their lines alone.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Every character begins as a stereotype or a mix of archetypes. Nuance arrives over the story, so do not demand full depth in the first scene.
- A main character can begin with a built-in contradiction. For side characters it is riskier, though it can be their defining trait.
- Outline plot and world heavily and characters lightly. Draft to find the personality and values, then rebuild the outline once you know them. Early versions that fail are normal.
- Background characters can be told apart by a visual signifier, a quirk that comes up naturally in conversation, or both. Give a quirk-defined character a viewpoint or a candid scene when you want them to grow past the type.
- In an ensemble, show each member reacting to the same event, and let the cast break from type over time.
- For characters who do not change, see `character-iconic-characters`.

## 56. character-motivation-discovery-questions

*Use when you do not know why your character does what they do, their choices feel arbitrary, or you want a quick interview to run on a new protagonist, villain, or side character. It gives a question set that yields a few core facts you can extrapolate from.*

The goal is a few fundamental facts about the character, so you can predict what they would choose in a situation you have not written yet. Treat it like deriving a magic system's consequences from a few rules. Not every character needs every question, but most need something like them. Many writers answer by instinct, and this list is for when instinct runs dry.

Run the questions on your own character and write short answers. Always follow an answer with "why?" until you reach something specific.

#### Backstory

1. What in this person's history explains the decisions they make today? It does not have to be tragic.
2. Which key decisions, theirs or other people's, shaped them?

#### The hard questions

3. What do they not want anyone to know?
4. What failure will they never repeat?
5. How do they treat blame? Do they take it on themselves, deflect it, or look for it in others?

#### Limits

6. What are their flaws?
7. What hinders them, and what limits their skills or position?

#### The four wants

8. What do they want?
9. Why can they not have it?
10. What do they need, which may differ from the want?
11. What must they sacrifice to get it?

#### Using the answers

- Pick the answers that conflict with each other. That tension is usually where scenes live.
- Check a scene against the answers. If the character acts against the pattern, the text must explain why, because readers sense the pattern and notice breaks.
- A villain's answers can make a sensible motive taken past the point a hero would stop, a tragic flaw at an extreme. A clear, understandable "why" carries a lot of weight for a villain.
- Fold the answers into `character-presenting-a-character` to decide what to show about motivation, personality, and values.

#### Checks

- `character.motivation.01` Can you name what the character wants, why they cannot have it, what they need, and what they must sacrifice?
- `character.motivation.02` Does each choice on the page follow the pattern of those answers, and where it breaks, does the text show why?
- `character.motivation.03` Do at least two of the character's answers conflict, giving scenes something to turn on?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Dossiers and pictures of a real-looking person are other ways to the same facts. Use whichever gets you answers.
- Do not paste the answers into the book as backstory. They are for your decisions.
- If answers come easier while drafting, draft first and ask the questions afterward. Several rewrites of a character before the right version is common.
- A large force without a human motive can skip these questions. See `character-iconic-characters`.

## 57. character-writing-unfamiliar-backgrounds

*Use when you are writing a character whose culture, disability, profession, or experience you have not lived, worry about stereotyping, or wonder who should read your draft. It gives a three-part practice of reading, individuality, and sensitivity readers.*

Writers often need a character shaped by an experience they lack. The main risk is harm from repeating a stereotype, and that risk grows with the size of your readership. Three practices reduce it.

#### 1. Read primary sources

- Look for first-person accounts from people with the experience or job.
- Seek out pieces on what media gets wrong about the group or profession. They show the shortcuts you are most likely to copy from fiction.
- Check small physical and practical facts. A reader on a draft might point out that lip reading is far less capable than films show, which changes a scene.

#### 2. Write an individual

- No person fits their category perfectly. Give the character some experiences common to the group and some traits that are theirs alone.
- Make the individual traits visible as individual, so the book does not suggest that every member of the group is like this one.
- Ask of each trait whether it comes from the background or from the person. Aim for a mix.

#### 3. Have people with the experience read it

- Use several readers, since individuals differ and one reader's view is only one view.
- Pay them if you can. New writers can ask on forums about a specific scene rather than a whole manuscript.
- Ask pointed questions: what rings false, what is missing, what would you never see in a real day?
- Expect rewrites. Pilots reading a space-fighter draft, or readers who share a diagnosis, can change large parts of a plot.

#### Checks

- `character.background.01` Does the character have a goal and personality apart from the background?
- `character.background.02` Could any trait be swapped for a cliche from media? If so, replace it.
- `character.background.03` Are other members of the group shown with variety, if they appear?
- `character.background.04` Is the thing that makes the character different treated as a source of skill and difficulty, not just a label?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Stretching for authenticity tends to improve the story for all readers, because more specific detail is better writing. Design changes made for access often help everyone, as curb cuts do.
- Readers will not all agree. Weigh responses and decide, then note the cost of choices you keep.
- For how to make an individual vivid in the first place, see `character-presenting-a-character`.

## 58. character-arc-types

*Use when you are planning how a character changes, an arc feels flat or predictable, a flaw does not seem to cost the character anything, or you cannot tell why a change-of-heart moment is unsatisfying. Gives four recurring arc shapes (apprentice, flaw-to-fix upward and downward, ignorance to knowledge) and a way to plan each.*

An arc is a shape that a character's change follows. Knowing a handful of common shapes lets you pick one on purpose, see which one a draft is drifting into, and check that it has the parts that make it work. Keep a personal list of arcs you notice in books and films: what defines each, what makes it work, and which ones you like. The four below are a starting set.

#### The four shapes

**1. Apprentice arc.** One character follows a master. The interest is in where the apprentice diverges: what each can do that the other cannot, and how the same skill is approached differently. It is also a way to separate two characters who have similar skills.
- Example: two sword-trained siblings, one taught to hold a line and one taught to break it. The apprentice's first real fight shows the master's method failing, and the reader sees the difference in how they fight.
- Plan it: list the shared skill, then write down one place the apprentice will choose differently and one thing the master cannot do.

**2. Flaw-to-fix arc.** The classic. Show early that the character is bad at something, such as pride, and show how it harms their life. Then they learn.
- A flaw usually has an upside, since hubris can look like self-confidence. Before the lesson, establish what the flaw costs them in opportunities, growth, or relationships, so the reader wants it fixed.
- Plan it: write the flaw, its upside, three concrete costs shown on the page, and the scene where the cost is largest.

**3. Upward and downward versions.** These share the flaw-to-fix setup and differ at the decisive moment.
- Upward: the character learns the lesson, reaches a decision point, and we see why they choose differently this time.
- Downward: the character learns the lesson and still refuses to apply it at the decisive moment. They know the flaw and hold on anyway.
- Plan it: write the decision point as a single choice, then write the reason the character gives themselves. That reason is the heart of the arc.

**4. Ignorance to knowledge arc.** A mystery arc told through the character, who learns about the world and about themselves. They need to change in order to use what they learn.
- Example: a detective finally learns there are guilty people they can know are guilty and never prove it. The fact is easy to state; the arc is what they must become to live with it.
- Plan it: list the pieces of information, and for each one name the change in the character that it demands.

#### Checks

- `character.arc.01` Does the reader see the flaw hurting the character before the lesson arrives?
- `character.arc.02` Is the decisive choice explained by who the character has become, not by how many times they have tried?
- `character.arc.03` In a downward arc, does the character know the flaw at the key moment? Constant arrogance that simply causes failure is a different, weaker shape.
- `character.arc.04` Can you name which of the four shapes each major character is on, or say that they have none?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Avoid the pattern of failing three times and then succeeding. The satisfying part is the why: who the character has become and why this decision is believable.
- A character who does not change much, or who changes only a little each book, follows different rules. See `character-iconic-characters`.
- Arcs are easier to believe when the character is also active and likable. See `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.
- How a flaw is shown to the reader on first meeting is covered in `character-presenting-a-character`.
- An arc can be the progress thread of a whole story. See `plot-types-of-progress`.

#### Where other advice differs

General craft advice treats character change as gradual. It wants small shifts visible across many scenes and welcomes backsliding and partial breakthroughs that do not stick. This skill puts the weight on one decisive choice and warns against a run of failed tries followed by success. Default to this skill: build the arc around the decision point and the reason behind it, with the flaw's costs shown beforehand. Choose the other side in a long novel or series where a single decision would feel sudden, or where realism about relapse is the point. Then show the incremental movement and the slides back, and keep the final choice explained by who the character has become.

## 59. character-floating-outline-for-dialogue

*Use when a dialogue scene is about to be written and you are unsure what it is for, conversations come out as flat exchanges of information, characters sound alike, or you want to show motivation without narration. Gives a short pre-scene list of goals, then an improvised draft and a note-to-self pass.*

A floating outline is a short list of scene goals kept separate from the main outline. You write it just before the scene, then improvise the scene itself. All writing involves some discovery; the list gives that discovery a direction so the scene does not wander or only pass along facts.

Dialogue is usually a stronger place than narration to show what characters want, because people with different motives push against each other. Use both, but let the conversation carry the friction.

#### Steps

1. **Write the purpose as one bullet.** Say what must happen and what gets in the way. Example: the general tries to recruit a neighboring king and is turned down because of who that king is.
2. **Ask what makes it interesting.** Add bullets for what the scene reveals about each speaker, any foreshadowing to plant, and what side characters present can show or react to.
3. **Freeform the scene.** Write it without checking the list line by line. The bullets are there to be remembered, and the dialogue can take surprising turns.
4. **Leave notes about small problems.** Put short bullets at the top of the scene for a later reread, or fix the problems immediately if they are quick.

#### Checks

- `character.dialogue.01` Can you say in one sentence what each speaker wants from the other?
- `character.dialogue.02` Does the refusal, or the compromise, come from the character's own nature, so a different character would have answered differently?
- `character.dialogue.03` Does each reveal about a character come through what they say and how they push, not through the narrator explaining?
- `character.dialogue.04` Is there something here for a side character to do beyond listening?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Keep the list short. Its job is to point, and a long list turns improvisation back into a script.
- The list sits outside the main outline, so changing it does not mean the plot has changed.
- To choose which flaws and quirks to bring out, see `character-presenting-a-character`. For characters who stay fixed across a story and push against others, see `character-iconic-characters`.
- When the scene belongs to a character who drives events, see `character-proactive-relatable-capable`.

## 61. magic-hard-and-soft

*Use when you are deciding how mysterious or how rule-bound your magic should be, readers say the magic feels random or too much like a puzzle, or you are unsure whether a system is hard magic or soft magic. It gives a spectrum and a way to place your system on it.*

Magic sits on a spectrum. At the hard end, readers can learn the rules and predict what the magic will do, and characters win by mastering and exploiting those rules, much as scientists or engineers would. At the soft end, readers do not know the rules and cannot guess the outcome, so the magic produces wonder or dread. Neither end is better. They do different jobs.

#### Place your system

1. **Ask what the reader can anticipate.** Describe a scene where the magic is used. Could a reader guess what it will do next, and what it cannot do? If yes, that part is hard. If the honest answer is "anything", that part is soft.
2. **Place each element separately.** One story can hold both. A ring whose effects are clearly stated (invisibility, being seen by a villain, corruption) is hard, while the wizard beside the ring hobbit is soft because nobody on the page knows his limits.
3. **Check across the whole work.** A series can be hard inside each book and soft across the series if later books forget the consequences of earlier inventions. That gives whimsy and costs consistency. Choose it on purpose.
4. **Pick the job you want.** Hard magic suits stories about clever problem-solving, heists, and characters who train. Soft magic suits horror, wonder, and urban fantasy where the protagonist should lack control. Hybrids exist: a wish-granting object where the reader knows wishes come but not their price leans soft.

#### Moving along the spectrum

- A story can start soft and become hard as characters work out the rules.
- A story can present rules and later reveal they were wrong. This is harder to pull off and works best when the whole plot is built around it.
- What the author knows can differ from what the text shows. Knowing the rules of a mysterious power helps you keep it consistent even if the reader never sees them.

#### Checks

- `magic.hardsoft.01` For each magical element, could a reader predict what it will do and what it cannot do? Place each element separately.
- `magic.hardsoft.02` Does each element's place on the spectrum fit its job: problem-solving and training for hard magic, wonder and lack of control for soft?
- `magic.hardsoft.03` Across a series, are earlier inventions' consequences remembered, or is the drift toward softness chosen on purpose?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Hardness comes from what the reader can predict, which is separate from how scientific the system sounds. See `magic-internal-and-external-logic`.
- The place on the spectrum limits how you can resolve conflicts. See `magic-first-law`.
- Stories where nature is the magic, such as a recurring deadly phenomenon, tend to lean hard, because characters race to understand and stop it.
- If you need a hint that the rules might be wrong, flag it early: state how the magic is believed to work, add a few historical cases nobody can explain, and let a mentor deflect certain questions.

## 62. magic-internal-and-external-logic

*Use when you are unsure whether your magic system counts as hard because it borrows real science, when you want your magic to sound believable, or when readers cannot predict what it does despite a pseudo-scientific explanation. It separates the logic readers use from the logic that only lends credibility.*

Two different things get called "logic" in a magic system, and they do different jobs.

- **Internal logic** is the set of rules that let a reader anticipate what the magic does inside the story. It decides how hard or soft the magic is.
- **External logic** is the borrowed layer: real physics, biology, or history used to make the magic feel respectable, easy to grasp, or fun. It is a veneer.

#### Separate them for your system

1. **Write the internal rules in plain story terms.** For example: "Burning metal gives a push or pull on nearby metal, and heavier things move less." A reader can use this without any science.
2. **Write the external explanation separately.** For example: "The push comes from a fundamental force." Check whether the story would work if this sentence vanished. If yes, it is flavor.
3. **Test the label.** A mutation gene and a power drawn from the sun are equally hard if each is applied consistently, since readers can predict both. A system that sounds technical but has erratic effects is soft.
4. **Decide where each one earns its place.** Use external logic for credibility, for the pleasure of real-world detail, and for making the magic easier to picture. Use internal logic for plot, since the plot runs on what readers can predict.

#### Using external logic well

- Keep it consistent with what your characters could know. If the reader holds information through real-world science that the characters lack (germs, say), that gap can read as characters being foolish. Create information gaps through different viewpoints instead.
- When a system is complex and not important to the plot, a technical-sounding exchange works if the audience trusts the experts understand it and the decision-maker only needs the timeline. Avoid the stock line asking someone to say it in plain English.
- A reason of "magic did it" is acceptable when the internal logic is solid. If the reason must drive a satisfying climax, the reader needs to see the why.

#### Checks

- `magic.logic.01` Are the internal rules written in plain story terms a reader could use without any science?
- `magic.logic.02` Would the story still work if the external explanation vanished? If so, it is flavor, not rules.
- `magic.logic.03` Is any system called hard because of its vocabulary, not because of what a reader can predict?
- `magic.logic.04` Does the reader know something from real-world science that the characters cannot, making them look foolish?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Do not call a system hard on the strength of its vocabulary. Ask what the reader can predict.
- Starting from internal rules and adding external logic afterward is common. See `magic-zeroth-law` for working backward from a cool element.
- For the spectrum itself, see `magic-hard-and-soft`.

## 63. magic-zeroth-law

*Use when you are stuck choosing between a plausible worldbuilding choice and an exciting one, when you want a striking element such as magical armor or a strange creature and cannot justify it, or when your world feels dutiful and dull. It gives the Zeroth Law (also spelled Zeroeth) method of picking the cool thing first and justifying it backward.*

When a choice is open, lean toward what is awesome. The defining line is "err on the side of what's awesome." Treat it as a method for building worlds, and it sits above the other magic laws as a tie-breaker.

#### Steps

1. **Name the cool thing you want in the book.** Be concrete, such as knights in magical power armor, a city of glowing fog, or a duel fought with borrowed memories.
2. **Work backward to justify it.** Ask what would have to be true of the world for this to exist, and what forces it exists to answer. In one example, the armor exists in the story because of the powerful forces it fights, and those forces exist because the author wanted armored knights.
3. **Choose the kind of justification.** Internal logic (rules the reader can follow) suits plots that depend on the element. External logic (borrowed real-world grounding) suits stories where credibility matters. Many need both. See `magic-internal-and-external-logic`.
4. **If the justification does not hold, change the element.** Alter the cool thing until its reasons are good enough, and keep it as awesome as the story can support.

#### Checks

- `magic.zeroth.01` For the elements of the world you are most excited about, what is the one-sentence in-world reason each one exists?
- `magic.zeroth.02` For the elements that exist only because they are realistic, would something more striking serve the story as well?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- This contrasts with science-fiction extrapolation, which starts from the present and projects forward. Fantasy can start from the destination.
- The law is a rule of thumb for choices that have no stronger constraint. It does not excuse breaking promises the story has already made, or contradicting rules already shown. See `magic-first-law`.
- If the magic is meant to resolve a climax, the reader must be able to see the why, so the backward justification has to reach the page. "Magic did it" works only when the internal logic is solid.
- Awesome includes tonal fit. An element that clashes with the tone you promised fails the law.

## 64. magic-first-law

*Use when your magic solves the climax and it feels like a cheat, readers call an ending deus ex machina, a hero finds a new power at the last moment, or you cannot tell how much of the magic to reveal before the final conflict. It gives Sanderson's First Law and a check for whether the magic may resolve the plot.*

The author's ability to solve problems with magic in a satisfying way is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that magic. This is a plotting rule. The more the reader knows, the more the magic can be asked to do at a key moment.

#### Checks

- `magic.first.01` Name the emotion the resolution should give, and ask whether the reader's understanding of the magic can support it.
- `magic.first.02` At the climax, could a reader have predicted the move from what they were shown? If they learn the rule only as it is used, expect a cheat reaction.
- `magic.first.03` If the magic is soft, does the resolution rest on what the characters do and want, not on the magic saving the day?
- `magic.first.04` If the magic is hard, were its pieces seeded earlier as tools that the characters now combine?

Soft magic can still frame a satisfying ending when the payoff is a character achievement unrelated to its rules. With hard magic, you may leave one step soft to leave room for later exploration.

#### Making soft magic pay off

A soft power can be given a hard-structured payoff by promising a specific event. A mysterious wizard who announces he will return at dawn on a stated day gives the reader a firm expectation, and his arrival then lands as earned. A rescue by a similar power with no setup reads as a near deus ex machina.

#### Seeding

- Authors usually under-seed. Add more clues than feels necessary, then trim if beta readers guess too much.
- Avoid info dumps. Give one small aspect of the magic per scene, keep other things happening in it, and build on it.
- Foreshadow without making characters look stupid by letting them hold the theory you want the reader to hold, then let that theory fail while a deeper layer remains.
- A red herring can help. Let readers settle on an expected explanation, then break it near the climax so they scramble with the characters.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Early in a story, a rescue by a newly arrived character is acceptable because its job is introduction. The same device in the final third reads as deus ex machina.
- Deliberately unsatisfying endings can serve a different emotion, provided you choose them on purpose.
- The reverse failure is arbitrary trouble from the author, where things get worse for no reason the reader could trace. Readers accept some, but too much shows the hand of the author. Prefer complications that come from choices, consequences, or clues that were present all along.
- A big event with no explanation can still satisfy if the real emotional payoff is elsewhere, such as a romance carrying a story that also has a vampire plot.
- See `magic-hard-and-soft` for placing your system, and `plot-promise-progress-payoff` for the broader setup and payoff structure.

## 65. magic-second-law

*Use when your magic or hero is too powerful and tension collapses, your powers feel like generic superpowers, you cannot decide what your magic should cost, or you want weaknesses that matter. It gives Sanderson's Second Law with a list-powers-then-costs-then-limits check.*

Limitations are more interesting than powers. Instead of hunting for ever more original abilities, invest in the constraints. Constraints force strategy, and characters are most interesting at the places where they are weak.

#### Checks

- `magic.second.01` List what the magic can do, what it costs the user, and what stops it, including who or what is stronger.
- `magic.second.02` If the first list is long and the others short, does the magic solve problems too easily?
- `magic.second.03` Do the most interesting scenes come from the costs and the limits?
- `magic.second.04` Is each power described with rules specific enough to make a tactical puzzle, not a generic effect?

A power described as "pushes metal" is generic. The same power with rules (it pushes directly away, pulls directly toward, and heavier things move less) becomes a tactical puzzle.

#### Four kinds of constraint

- **Cost.** What using it takes: money, physical harm, years of study, a mystical price. The size of the cost shapes how often and how freely it is used.
- **Limitation.** What the magic can and cannot do. Characters work inside it and are not expected to get around it.
- **Flaw** (in the strict sense). Something the reader expects characters to fix or overcome, such as magic that used to work and now fails.
- **Hindrance.** A line characters could cross but would prefer not to, leaving open whether they should.

A flaw can turn into a hindrance when characters find that fixing it would make things worse.

#### Match constraints to people and theme

- Choose limits that press on your protagonist's personal weakness. Someone who cannot fly well is more interesting than someone who can.
- Connect the constraint to the theme. A desert hero's relationship to water, tied to a magic that runs on water, carries the theme into the system.
- The richest stories sit where a flawed character meets a flawed magic.
- The strongest heroes face problems bigger than themselves. Give the powerful figure opponents who outclass them, and give the burden to someone who is not the most powerful.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Resource costs behave like bullets in a gun: readers sense the moments when supplies run low. Let characters voice how low things are, or give an exact number when you want countdown tension.
- Too many flaws smother a system. See `magic-third-law`.
- Constraints still need to be visible to the reader before the climax. See `magic-first-law`.

## 66. magic-third-law

*Use when your world keeps growing with new systems, peoples, and rules, readers say it feels shallow or overwhelming, you feel pressure to add another magic system, or you are drafting a long setting and fear a mile-wide inch-deep result. It gives Sanderson's Third Law and a check to run before adding anything new.*

Before adding something new, expand what you already have. Depth beats breadth, and a few elegant rules that interact are worth more than a pile of finicky ones. The value of a world comes from how its ideas affect each other and how far each is explored, and density of ideas matters far less.

#### Checks

- `magic.third.01` Before adding a new element, could something already in the story do this job: a changed rule, a second use of an existing magic, a branch of an existing group?
- `magic.third.02` For each core rule, have its consequences for economy, war, family, religion, or crime been followed?
- `magic.third.03` If a new element is added, is it linked to something already on the page?

#### Examples

- Two religions can be written as branches of a single faith that split over doctrine before inventing an unrelated religion. The shared roots give conflict and texture for free.
- A single premise, such as a world where the dark lord already won, can support several books of exploration by asking what that does to prophecy, rulers, heroes, and everyday life.
- A game with ten thousand near-identical dungeons loses to one with fifty handcrafted ones.
- A draft that follows ten character arcs thins each of them. Cutting to a few focused arcs lets each develop.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- The law does not forbid adding new things. Longer series can sustain more.
- Pressure from fans or publishers to multiply systems is a reason to apply the check, because a new system added for count alone rarely earns its place.
- Depth in the world is separate from depth shown on the page. Keep most of what you build as hidden structure, and show only what the scene needs.
- Limits stack: many flaws and costs on one system also break this law. See `magic-second-law`.
- When contrast helps, build from what differs and what stays the same between your world and ours rather than from a long list of new inventions.

## 67. worldbuilding-idea-intersection

*Use when you have a pile of story ideas and no book yet, a premise feels thin, a setting has no plot, or you are not sure whether you have enough ideas for a novel. It gives a way to sort ideas into setting, character, and plot, find the holes, and combine ideas so each makes the others stronger.*

A short story can rest on one good idea. A novel usually needs a couple of strong ideas in each of three areas: setting, character, and plot. Ideas get better when they collide, so the work is to gather them, sort them, and force them against each other.

#### Steps

1. **Make three headings** (setting, character, plot) and list every idea you have, however rough. Pull in old ideas from your notes file as well. Keep that file somewhere you can find it, because an idea that is dull alone can come alive next to a new one.
2. **Look for holes.** Which column is thin? A rich world with no hook for a protagonist, or a vivid lead with nothing for them to do, tells you what to develop next.
3. **Convert between columns.** A plot hook can become a setting hook. For example, the plot question "what if the hero failed?" turns into a setting: the dark lord won and the world has lived under him for a thousand years.
4. **Collide pairs.** Take one idea from two columns and ask what each does to the other. A heist plot adds a thieving crew to that conquered world. The crew needs powers suited to roles such as scout, talker, and heavy, so a magic idea gets rebuilt around those roles.
5. **Redesign borrowed pieces.** An old idea that does not fit as written can be broken into parts and rebuilt to serve this story. Keep the spark, not the original shape.
6. **Follow the consequences.** A single premise explored for its ramifications can fill several books. The conquered-world idea can support a deconstruction of prophecy, chosen ones, and dark lords, and it shapes each major character. Depth from asking what an idea implies is worth more than a larger number of unrelated ideas.
7. **Brainstorm aloud** with a writing group or a friend when the combinations stall.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.ideas.01` Can you name at least two ideas in each column that you are excited about?
- `worldbuilding.ideas.02` For each setting idea, is there a character or plot idea that presses on it?
- `worldbuilding.ideas.03` Is any idea just sitting there, affecting nothing else? Either connect it or save it for another book.
- `worldbuilding.ideas.04` Do your own fascinations and expertise show up somewhere?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- There is no objective measure of a better idea. Choose the combinations that excite you and make the story more interesting.
- Do not collect ideas forever. When the columns are filled and connected, start outlining or drafting; see `worldbuilding-worldbuilders-disease`.
- Setting ideas still need to be sorted into physical, cultural, and magical material when you develop them; see `worldbuilding-three-part-split`.

## 68. worldbuilding-three-part-split

*Use when you are starting to build a fantasy or science fiction world and do not know what to work out, your worldbuilding notes are a jumble, or you suspect you have missed whole areas such as food, taboos, or weather. It splits world notes into physical setting, cultural setting, and magic, with prompts for each.*

Organize world notes into three documents. Each answers a different question, and sorting ideas this way shows quickly what you have not yet thought about.

#### The three parts

1. **Physical setting.** What would exist if no thinking beings had ever lived here? Prompts: geography and topography, geology, weather and climate, flora and fauna (food chains, biomes, adaptations), disease and viruses, natural disasters, astronomy and cosmology, rotation, tides, or a tidally locked planet. Domestication of plants and animals sits on the border with culture.
2. **Cultural setting.** Everything thinking beings create, and how they think and interact. Prompts: technology level and gaps between cultures, religion, food and food customs, social manners and taboos, law and government, languages and dialects, social roles such as gender and class, relationship to the natural world, traditions, education, fashion, building methods and materials, festivals.
3. **Magic.** Its own section because it touches both of the others. It acts on the physical world and shapes culture. The rules for designing it belong to a separate set of skills.

#### How to use it

- Brainstorm a list under each heading and mark the items that your characters will run into.
- Follow chains of consequence between parts. Food customs shape social manners, available materials shape architecture, and architecture reflects technology level. One change in the physical setting often ripples through several cultural items.
- Look for the surprising item. A small custom, such as a gesture that is polite in one culture and a grave insult in another, can open a whole set of taboos you would not have invented from the top down. Real cultures are a good source for this.
- Check how living things interact. A believable ecosystem where creatures eat, avoid, or depend on each other makes a setting feel real.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.split.01` Does each part (physical setting, cultural setting, magic) hold the items your characters will actually run into?
- `worldbuilding.split.02` Does a change in one part ripple through the others, such as food customs into manners or materials into architecture?
- `worldbuilding.split.03` Do the chosen items make each other more interesting, with everything else left vague on purpose?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- The lists are long on purpose. Their size shows how overwhelming the possibilities are, which is the cause of the trap described in `worldbuilding-worldbuilders-disease`. Do not try to fill every prompt.
- Choose the items that influence your characters' decisions and the problems they face, and let the rest stay vague; see `worldbuilding-hollow-iceberg`.
- Pick items that make each other more interesting. A river that floods yearly matters more once a festival, a law, and a trade route depend on it.
- To keep the build tied to the story, begin from the ideas you combined with `worldbuilding-idea-intersection`.

## 69. worldbuilding-worldbuilders-disease

*Use when you have been building a world for months or years and have not started the book, every new detail opens ten more questions, you are polishing languages or maps instead of writing, or you cannot tell how much worldbuilding is enough before drafting. It gives a way to cap the work and aim it.*

Every worldbuilding element is a rabbit hole. Food leads to social customs, which lead to building materials, then architecture, then technology level. Because there is always more to do, the writer stays in preparation and the book never gets written. That is worldbuilder's disease.

#### Remedies

1. **Set a deadline for worldbuilding** and keep it, then start the book. The deadline is the cure; without one, the work expands to fill any time.
2. **Choose a few elements, not all of them.** If you are newer, pick some where you already have expertise and a few you want to learn. A manageable set can still be deep.
3. **Spend time where the story leans.** Ask what influences the decisions your characters make and the challenges they face. A murder mystery needs a worked-out justice system far more than it needs a constructed language. A first book with heavy language work and no legal system shows the mistake.
4. **Decide gaps as they appear.** When drafting reveals something you did not build, either do the work now or leave a placeholder, depending on how vital it is to that scene.
5. **Return to the notes while drafting.** Worldbuilding can continue after you begin writing. The rule is to keep going back to the story.
6. **Measure by confidence.** Count worldbuilding as done when you feel sure of the setting, not when you hit a word count. As a rough pattern, planning notes may run to about a tenth of the final book length, starting as exploratory paragraphs and turning into bullet points once the world clicks. You cannot anticipate every need, so fill gaps without stopping the writing for long.
7. **Alternate if useful.** Some writers swap weeks of writing with weeks of worldbuilding.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.disease.01` How many months since you began building, and what have you written in the story itself?
- `worldbuilding.disease.02` For the last five things you built, can you name a scene or decision they affect?
- `worldbuilding.disease.03` Is the next worldbuilding task something the draft needs, or something you enjoy more than drafting?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- The advice assumes you want to produce finished books at a steady pace, roughly one a year. If building a world for twenty years is the goal, that is a valid goal and the deadline does not apply.
- A new writer often cannot yet tell where preparation time pays off. That judgment improves with each book, so expect to build more usefully over time.
- For deciding what to build and what to merely suggest, see `worldbuilding-hollow-iceberg`. For choosing areas to work through, see `worldbuilding-three-part-split`.

## 70. worldbuilding-hollow-iceberg

*Use when a draft reads like an encyclopedia, you feel you must put all your worldbuilding on the page, readers say the opening is dull or overloaded, or you wonder whether you have too much or too little worldbuilding. It covers what to build in depth, what to only evoke, and a two-part test for excess.*

The common advice says the reader sees the tip and the great bulk of worldbuilding lies below the surface. The risk is that a writer with a huge submerged mass wants to show it, and the result is info dumps. The alternative is a hollow iceberg. Build in depth only what you truly know and what informs your characters and their decisions. For everything else, suggest that something is there.

#### Steps

1. **Research the part that matters.** Find what shapes character choices and plot, and do real work on it. This can happen before drafting or after, as long as you keep returning to the story.
2. **Evoke the rest.** Give one or two lines with a light hook, such as a passing reference to a distant war or a regional dish. Readers sense depth, and the hook can seed later books.
3. **Use one vivid detail.** Describe a single detail of a place in sharp terms and let readers fill in the rest. A room with bullet holes in the wall, a flickering light, and a dead ceiling fan implies a poor neighborhood without any explanation.
4. **Lean on what readers know.** Jump gates, faster-than-light travel, and dragons need no mechanics. Explaining how a dragon's wings could lift its mass invites boredom or arguments.
5. **Make long description earn its place.** If a paragraph is heavy on world detail, write it well enough that readers enjoy it.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.iceberg.01` Is worldbuilding keeping you from drafting, measured against your own deadline?
- `worldbuilding.iceberg.02` Is each piece of world detail on the page there because it supports stakes, character, or plot, or because you invested time in it?
- `worldbuilding.iceberg.03` Is the world built deeply only where it shapes choices and plot, with a passing hook for everything else?

For drafting pace, about one normal-length book a year is a good target and one every two years is acceptable; past that, move time from building to writing. The symptom of detail that does not serve the story is paragraphs of explanation that do not match the storytelling and make readers ask why this matters.

#### Too little worldbuilding

The sign is reader confusion. Early readers will tell you where they could not tell what questions to ask. Some confusion at the start is acceptable if dynamic storytelling carries readers until later realizations connect it.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- There is no wrong way to write a book. A dense, worldbuilding-first opening can work, and it costs some readers while winning others. Step two of the test is not a failure if that density is the story you are telling.
- Extras can be valid. Songs and histories that delight you and your readers have a place when they match your goals.
- If explaining something does not move the plot, half the audience will want more and half will be bored, so decide on purpose.
- For the cost of heavy openings, see `worldbuilding-learning-curve-and-exposition`. For concrete grounding of scenes, see `worldbuilding-pyramid-of-abstraction`. For deadlines, see `worldbuilding-worldbuilders-disease`.

## 71. worldbuilding-pyramid-of-abstraction

*Use when a scene feels like talking heads in a void, a worldbuilding or magic explanation reads as an info dump, readers lose track of where a scene is, or a character's reflection drifts too far into abstraction. Gives a way to anchor scenes in concrete detail so abstract material is earned.*

Words sit on a pyramid. At the bottom are concrete images readers all picture the same way. At the top are abstractions such as love, destiny, or the nature of a magic system. Each descriptive word pulls a concept down toward the concrete. Even "a dog" is fairly abstract, because every reader pictures a different dog. "A small, wet poodle huddled in an alley, whimpering" puts everyone in the same place.

The practical rule is that readers will follow you up the pyramid into explanation, philosophy, or a character's navel-gazing only if you have first anchored them in a concrete scene.

#### Using it in a scene

1. **Anchor first.** Open with concrete sensory detail. Use more than sight: smell, texture, temperature, sound, taste.
2. **Climb while staying anchored.** Move into the character's thoughts, the history of a place, or how the magic works, but keep the reader in the room while you do it.
3. **Re-anchor.** Drop back to a sensory detail or a physical action every so often. How often is a judgment call. If a reader could not say where the characters are standing, you waited too long.
4. **Close concretely.** A useful pattern for dialogue is an anchor at the start, a few beats in the middle, and an anchor at the end. Too many beats clutter the talk.

#### Example

A scene where two characters argue about a prophecy. Unanchored, it is two voices exchanging lore. Anchored, one of them has sand in her teeth and the spice shimmers in the lamplight while they talk, so the prophecy is discussed on a specific planet at a specific hour.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.pyramid.01` Find the most abstract paragraph (an explanation, a reflection, a cosmology). What concrete detail sits in the paragraphs just before and after it?
- `worldbuilding.pyramid.02` Does the scene use only sight? Add one or two other senses.
- `worldbuilding.pyramid.03` Is any concrete detail doing no work? When the image is irrelevant, a plain noun is enough.
- `worldbuilding.pyramid.04` Is a figure of speech colliding with the world, such as "he flew across the room" where people really fly?
- `worldbuilding.pyramid.05` Does a prologue about gods or cosmology give the reader no character and no scene?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- This is a tool for managing attention, not a quota of adjectives. With practice anchoring becomes automatic, and a writing group can flag the places where they lost the scene or the viewpoint.
- Pair it with `worldbuilding-viewpoint-filtered-description` so the anchors reflect what this character would notice, and with `worldbuilding-hollow-iceberg` to decide how much of the hidden world to surface at all.

## 72. worldbuilding-naming

*Use when you need names for characters, places, or things in an invented world, names feel random or inconsistent across a culture, or you worry about accidentally borrowing a real word or insulting a real culture. Gives three naming approaches and the checks to run on any name.*

A culture's names should feel like they belong together. There are three ways to get there, from easiest to most hands-on. Pick one per culture, so that its names share a sound.

#### The tools

1. **Atlas method.** Choose a real region that resembles the culture you are building and adapt words, names, and sounds from it. This is the fastest approach and works best with a region you know something about. A desert empire seeded from one part of the map, or a noble house with French-flavored names, gives readers an instant sense of place.
2. **Linguistics method.** Study how languages work and borrow one structural feature. If a language has few distinct sounds, its words run long and repeat syllables, so a culture built on that pattern will sound distinct from one with many consonant clusters. You are borrowing a rule, not a vocabulary.
3. **Linguistic trick.** Apply a simple, deliberate pattern. Examples are names that are palindromic, names that repeat an initial consonant, or a family where every name ends in the same sound. Judge the result by feel and read the names aloud.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.naming.01` Search the name. Does it accidentally make a real word, in the source language or another one?
- `worldbuilding.naming.02` Is it, or does it contain, a slur or swear word in a language your readers are likely to know?
- `worldbuilding.naming.03` Has someone who knows the source region or language looked at it?
- `worldbuilding.naming.04` Does it trigger a different mythology or brand than you meant, for people who have not seen the project?
- `worldbuilding.naming.05` Does it collide with a huge, famous work? A small overlap with an obscure one rarely matters.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Borrowing from real cultures is an ongoing conversation with no settled answer. Be specific, such as a particular dynasty or period, rather than treating a whole culture as one block. Think about your own relationship to the source and the history behind it, and about how you would want to be depicted if you were on the other side.
- Do not treat a living culture's beliefs as raw material for a magic system.
- Keep the number of names low in an opening chapter. See `worldbuilding-learning-curve-and-exposition`.
- Make the choice on purpose. A name pattern or cultural source you chose deliberately holds up better than one you drifted into.

## 73. worldbuilding-learning-curve-and-exposition

*Use when an opening is crammed with names and jargon, beta readers say they are confused or bored by the setup, you do not know how much of your world to explain, or you are deciding whether to set a story in the real world. Gives the learning curve model and ways to deliver exposition through a character.*

Every book asks the reader to learn something: who the people are, how the world works, what the rules are. That demand is the learning curve. You choose where it is steep and where it is shallow, and you pay for it in the reader's attention.

#### Choosing the curve

- **Shallow curve.** Familiar settings and genre staples need little explanation. A wormhole in science fiction or a football game in a real-world story arrives pre-learned. Readers can go straight to character and plot.
- **Steep curve.** Heavy names, invented terms, and unfamiliar rules promise a demanding read. This is a legitimate choice when it is deliberate, and it suits readers who want to be dropped in.
- **Real-world settings** shift the cost. You skip explaining the world, but expert readers will notice errors, and a few wrong small details can make them distrust everything else. Cover gaps with a viewpoint character who does not know the domain, so the mistakes read as characterization, and use knowledgeable early readers to catch them.

#### Signs the curve is unmanaged

- Many proper names or jargon terms in the first pages. A couple can set tone. Many make eyes glaze.
- The world is shown off rather than used. A prologue that begins at the dawn of time with a magic is the standard example.
- Early readers cannot say what questions they have, which suggests the world is too thin or too opaque.

#### Delivering exposition

1. **Put a character with a problem in front of the mechanism.** A student who is dyslexic and writes magical spells in a system where mistakes in the written word matter shows the rules and earns sympathy at once.
2. **Convey the world through the character's eyes.** The reader should feel they are getting character and plot while the world arrives as a side effect.
3. **Make history matter to someone.** Where the character's attention goes, the reader's follows. If a piece of lore is not relevant to the character yet, hint at it with a brush-off and return later, or cut it.
4. **Make exposition visual or active** when you can, and pay it off soon so readers learn that attending is rewarded.
5. **Do not hide essentials in epigraphs or prologues.** Some readers skip them, and material not tied to a moment is forgotten easily.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.curve.01` Count the new proper names and terms in the first chapter. Which could wait?
- `worldbuilding.curve.02` For each paragraph of explanation, who is thinking it, and why now?
- `worldbuilding.curve.03` Is the learning curve steep on purpose, and does the opening promise that?

See `worldbuilding-viewpoint-filtered-description` and `worldbuilding-pyramid-of-abstraction` for the mechanics, and `worldbuilding-hollow-iceberg` for how much to build before writing.

## 74. worldbuilding-gorilla-in-the-phone-booth

*Use when a late reveal, new character, or new worldbuilding element steals attention from the main thread, readers say a subplot was more interesting than the main plot, or you are deciding when to introduce a big new idea. Gives a test for elements that are too interesting for where they sit.*

A gorilla in a phone booth is an element so interesting that readers stop caring about whatever the story was doing. If the main thread is a woman trying to place a call and a gorilla is in the booth, nobody wants the call. The danger is introducing something more exciting than the thread you are on, because the reader resents the lesser material until they get back to the exciting element.

#### How it happens

- **Promise, then cut away.** You promise something compelling and then detour into a thread the reader cannot bond with. The audience wants to get back and resents the detour. A character is told to find a friend, then spends a long stretch on an unrelated side adventure.
- **Introduced too late.** A new problem arriving three-quarters of the way through, even if foreshadowed, feels like a new problem from nowhere. In one first draft, a king's son arrived late and caused tens of thousands of words of chaos. Cutting him made the ending stronger.
- **Introduced too early.** A story about a teenager's problems at school gets a monster in chapter one. Nobody cares about the school problems anymore. Wait too long, and the readers who came for the monster are annoyed at the reveal. Both complaints are real, so decide which readers you are promising to.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.gorilla.01` List the most exciting elements in the book (a creature, a magic, a faction). Where does each first appear?
- `worldbuilding.gorilla.02` At that point, what thread is the reader following, and is the new element stronger than that thread? If so, raise the main thread, move the element, or cut it.
- `worldbuilding.gorilla.03` Does the new element serve the promise the reader is already holding, or create a rival one?
- `worldbuilding.gorilla.04` In the last quarter, does anything arrive that nobody has mentioned? A long-foreshadowed threat is fine; an unforeshadowed new problem is not.

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A structural signal can defuse the gorilla. A prologue that shows powerful magic, then moves to a farm boy, does not trigger the problem, because the format tells the reader the cool material is done for now and will be explained later.
- There is no firm cutoff for when to stop introducing new elements. Use the checks above, and prefer fewer.
- Seeding material for a sequel can start around the three-quarter mark, kept light so it does not distract, and be brought to a head in the denouement or epilogue. Do this after the first book works.
- This is the reverse side of showing off the world. See `worldbuilding-learning-curve-and-exposition`.

## 75. worldbuilding-viewpoint-filtered-description

*Use when scene-setting reads like a travel brochure, you are unsure how to open a chapter or how often to describe a place, every chapter in the same location feels repeated, or readers cannot tell whose head they are in. Gives a method for describing settings through the viewpoint character so description also builds character.*

Describe a place the way this character, in this mood, would see it. A neutral author's inventory of the room does no work. A filtered description is characterization and foreshadowing at the same time, so scene-setting is never wasted effort.

#### Method

1. **Pull the reader in first.** Start with the characters launching into a problem, or with dialogue, then set the scene around it.
2. **Anchor the viewpoint early.** Make the viewpoint character's name the first one in the scene or chapter, since readers scan for it. If another name must come first, give the action verb to the viewpoint character or have them watch that person. A beginner's rule of thumb that experienced writers can break.
3. **Describe through perception.** The same mist, or the same ticking clock, is playful to one character and ominous to another. A reader should be able to tell whose head they are in from the description alone.
4. **Use a single line in the character's voice.** One sharp line, such as a woman so tough she could chew steel and spit nails, does more than three paragraphs.
5. **Re-set the scene in every chapter,** even in the same place, with the character noticing something different.
6. **Remind through interaction.** When characters are talking at length, have them lean on a wall, slam a table, or look across the setting. A single line about a recurring feature is enough, and it pulls the dialogue back to the concrete.

#### Checks

- `worldbuilding.filtered.01` Cover the name tags. Can you tell from the description who is looking and how they feel?
- `worldbuilding.filtered.02` Is each description a catalog of what exists, or a record of what this person notices?
- `worldbuilding.filtered.03` Across chapters in one location, does each opening notice something new?
- `worldbuilding.filtered.04` Do recurring features get a full description each time, where a line would do?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- There is no rule for how often to describe. Add a reminder when the reader might lose the setting.
- A story set almost entirely in one room can work very well, as long as the room changes through the character's changing state. Making repeated description fresh is a skill to practice, for example by describing a two-person scene in an empty room through shifting mood.
- If the worldbuilding matters to the plot but not to this character, signal its weight through the narrative, cut to a viewpoint that cares, or tie it to something the character does care about.
- Use `worldbuilding-pyramid-of-abstraction` to keep the description concrete, and `worldbuilding-learning-curve-and-exposition` for how much to reveal.

## 77. scene-and-sequel

*Use when pacing feels relentless or sluggish, when a chapter of nonstop action leaves readers numb, when you wonder how long characters should react after a big event, or when you need to slow a thriller without killing momentum. Gives a scene then sequel rhythm and a way to tune it by book length.*

Every scene that ends with a change is followed by a sequel: a beat where the characters respond to the new situation. Books alternate between the two. The alternation does not have to be rigid, and one chapter can hold both.

#### The three steps

1. **Scene.** Rising action that reaches a conclusion. Something is won, lost, learned, or broken.
2. **Sequel.** Time for the characters to take in the result: grieve, argue, regroup, decide. The new status quo becomes real to them and to the reader.
3. **Spin up.** The decision or the pressure made in the sequel launches the next scene.

#### Using it

- **Control pacing with response time.** A long, solid sequel slows the book and deepens character. A short sequel keeps the pace high. The amount of room characters get to respond is the dial.
- **Check a dragging stretch.** Are there several sequels in a row with no scene between them? Nothing is changing, so the response has nothing to respond to.
- **Check a numbing stretch.** Are there several scenes in a row with no sequel? The reader never gets to feel the consequences, so each event weighs less than the last.
- **Make the sequel carry something.** A sequel can hold a quiet revelation, a joke, a relationship beat, or a hook into the next scene. It is a place for the character arc, so it should not be filler.
- **Mix the sizes.** A fast adventure can run bursts of action separated by brief relaxation. A big epic can afford long sequels after major events.

#### Checks

- `scene.sequel.01` After each scene that ends with a change, is there a sequel where the characters take in the result, sized to the pace you want?
- `scene.sequel.02` Is a dragging stretch a run of sequels with no scene between them?
- `scene.sequel.03` Is a numbing stretch a run of scenes with no sequel to feel the consequences?
- `scene.sequel.04` Does each sequel carry something (a revelation, a joke, a relationship beat), and does its decision launch the next scene?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Book length changes the right ratio. A short thriller that readers finish in one or two sittings can run very short sequels that end on the next explosion; this is much of what people mean by a page-turner.
- Tension has diminishing returns. Even a high-octane story usually benefits from one real slowdown near the middle or just before the end.
- Scene and sequel is a diagnostic. It does not require every scene to be followed by a labeled reaction section. Sometimes a single paragraph of response is enough.
- Use `plot-promise-progress-payoff` to decide what the scene itself should deliver.

## 78. show-dont-tell

*Use when you are unsure whether to dramatize a trait or just state it, when a critique says tell not show, when a scene spends too many words proving something minor, or when a summary feels like a cheat. Gives an apprentice rule and a journeyman judgment for choosing between showing and telling.*

Showing a quality through action is usually more memorable than stating it. Showing also costs more words. The rule has two levels.

#### Apprentice level: show

If you are learning, default to showing. Instead of "he was a nervous type," have him tap his toes through the whole meeting. Instead of telling readers a frog is dangerous, let it explode when someone pokes it in the opening scene. This builds the habit of finding the concrete action behind a label, and it lets readers draw the conclusion themselves.

#### Journeyman level: choose

An experienced writer decides case by case. Ask three questions.

1. **How much does this matter to the story?** A trait that shapes the plot or the character arc earns a dramatized moment. A passing detail does not.
2. **What kind of scene is this?** In a tense scene, spend words on the action. In a transition, a sentence of summary is fine.
3. **How important is the character?** A one-time character does not get paragraphs of demonstration. A line of telling is enough.

#### When telling is the better tool

- Summary for long spans. A school term can pass in a sentence.
- A fact that is awkward to dramatize. Having a character notice out loud that she looks different from everyone around her is often clumsier than stating it plainly.
- Minor traits where the cost of a scene outweighs the gain.

#### When showing earns its words

- Traits that matter over time and could be misread if stated once, such as a way of thinking that readers should come to understand through repeated behavior.
- The first appearance of a character or element you want readers to remember.

#### Checks

- `scene.show.01` For each stated trait or emotion, does it matter enough to the story to be shown? If yes, find one specific action or detail; if no, leave the plain statement.
- `scene.show.02` Is a minor point being proved at length, such as a passing trait or a one-time character given a dramatized scene?
- `scene.show.03` Is the first appearance of anything the reader must remember shown, not only stated?

#### Where other advice differs

Many craft guides treat a stated emotion or trait as a label to be replaced by behavior, almost without exception, with summary allowed mainly for logistics and transitions. This approach goes further toward telling: stating a trait is fine whenever a dramatized version costs more than the trait is worth, including for a minor trait of a minor character.

Default: use this approach to decide how much of the story's word budget a trait deserves, since that is a question of structure and character importance. When you have decided a moment deserves to be shown, or when you are editing a sentence that reads as a flat label, follow the stricter line-level advice: replace the label with a specific action, reaction, or thought. If a draft is short on words or the trait is minor, keep the plain statement.

Related: `prose-ai-tells` for prose that tells in a machine-sounding way.

## 79. viewpoint-and-tense

*Use when choosing first, second, or third person and past or present tense, when you wonder whether to use omniscient narration, how many first-person narrators a book can bear, whether a narrator can be unreliable, or whether to switch viewpoint or tense mid-book. Gives a choice matrix with the strengths and costs of each mode.*

A story is presented in a person (first, second, third) and a tense (past, present, future). Each combination has its own strengths. Second person and future tense are rare and experimental, though second person can work well when done with skill.

#### The modes

1. **Third limited.** One head per scene. Break for another viewpoint, then stay for the chapter. Some writers ease in from a brief omniscient opening and ease out again.
2. **True omniscient.** An impartial observer who can enter any head, even paragraph by paragraph.
3. **Storyteller omniscient.** A hybrid of first and third: a storyteller who holds the whole story and talks to you about it.
4. **Epistolary first person.** Told through documents, like found footage.
5. **Close first person.** Written as if from inside the character's head, with no sense of a teller. It works like third limited with "I." Most current YA uses it.
6. **Storyteller first person.** Someone telling the story after the fact, often with a frame.

#### Choosing

- **Cast size.** Third suits a large cast. A clear omniscient narrator, or naming the viewpoint character up front each time, tells readers whose head they are in. First suits one or two viewpoints. With more than two first-person narrators every voice says "I" and the book gets jarring.
- **Voice.** First person makes exposition into characterization, so info-heavy or comic narration is easier. Third limited colors the voice but cannot fully sound like a very young or very odd narrator.
- **Survival.** Any first person implies the narrator lived long enough to tell it. Close first person offsets this by feeling immediate.
- **Mystery versus suspense.** In true omniscient, withholding information feels like a cheat, so lean on suspense, where the reader knows more than the characters. Limited modes let you withhold. Storyteller omniscient can get away with more.
- **Unreliable narrators.** They work in every mode except true omniscient. Storyteller first person suits them best. Third limited feels most like cheating unless you signal it, for instance with a character whose hyperbole is obvious.
- **Tense.** Adult fantasy and science fiction is mostly past tense and YA often present. Both read as happening now, so the difference is mostly reader preference.

#### Checks

- `scene.pov.01` Does the viewpoint mode fit the cast size and the information the story needs to withhold?
- `scene.pov.02` Within a scene, does the narration stay in one head, and is any switch deliberate and clear to the reader?
- `scene.pov.03` In true omniscient, is information withheld in a way that will feel like a cheat? Lean on suspense instead.
- `scene.pov.04` Does any change of tense or mode mid-book have a reason the reader can see?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Conventions are traditions, not laws. Published books slip viewpoint or change tense; what matters is whether readers understand why.
- Contrast on purpose: one character in present tense and the rest in past is a legitimate design.
- Switching mode or tense mid-book can work if the reason is clear to the reader, such as a framing story.

#### Where other advice differs

Common line-level guidance tells writers to establish one point of view and stay in it, and to show other characters only through what can be observed. This approach treats that as one convention among several. It counts true omniscient and storyteller modes as full options, with a different contract with the reader, and treats a viewpoint slip as a matter of whether the reader is confused.

Default: for the choice of mode, cast fit, and what information to withhold, which are structure and character questions, follow the approach above. For executing a limited viewpoint on the page, including how close the narration sits to the character's thoughts, how the character's own words color the narration, and catching accidental slips while editing, follow the stricter line-level advice. If you have chosen omniscient on purpose, relax the single-head rule but keep each paragraph clear about whose perception it is.

## 80. scene-access-limits

*Use when characters seem to know things the scene has not given them, a viewpoint character notices or understands too much, an expert's knowledge is not kept apart from everyone else's, or someone makes a choice that belongs to someone else. Separates what a character can see, hear, reach, and infer, and who owns each decision.*

A character knows only what the scene lets them know. Check each thing a character acts on against how they got it.

#### What a character can know here

Four separate limits: what they can **see**, **hear**, physically **reach**, and correctly **infer**. Seeing something is not understanding it. Getting close once is not having time to inspect it. If a character acts on something none of the four gave them, the scene owes the reader how they got it.

#### How knowledge is held

Sort what is known into three layers, relative to this character, this moment, and this audience:

- **Shared**: the common understanding of the setting. It constrains the scene without explanation.
- **Specialized**: what an expert or insider knows. It changes risk and the methods available.
- **Private**: what one person alone knows, wants, or has decided. It drives what they choose, hide, distort, test, or say.

Then name the status of each statement: an observation, a report, a judgment, an intention, or a mistake. A character who repeats a rumor, states a guess as fact, or misreads what they saw is working from a different status than one who watched it happen.

#### Who owns what

Keep five roles apart, even when one person fills several:

- the **experiential center**: whose perception the reader gets
- the **problem owner**: whose problem it is
- the **decision owner**: who actually chooses
- the **domain actor**: the one with the relevant skill
- the **execution owner**: who carries it out

Viewpoint decides what pressure reaches the reader first. It does not automatically give that character authority, expertise, or control. A consequential scene reads best as: perception or misreading, narrowed options, a decision, an action, a consequence, a reassessment. That is a diagnostic, not a formula, and not a ban on omniscient or multi-viewpoint fiction.

#### Checks

- `scene.access.01` For each thing a character acts on, which of seeing, hearing, reaching, or inferring gave it to them in this scene?
- `scene.access.02` Does any character understand something they only saw, or inspect something they only passed once?
- `scene.access.03` Are shared, specialized, and private knowledge kept apart (an expert's judgment does not leak to a layperson, and a private motive is not known to others)?
- `scene.access.04` Is each consequential choice made by the character who owns that decision, and does it follow from what they perceived or misread?
- `scene.access.05` When a character states a fact, is it clear whether it is an observation, a report, a judgment, an intention, or a mistake?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- An omniscient narrator sets its own limits. Apply these checks to what the narrator has established, not to a single head.
- Mysteries depend on withholding. Check that the withheld thing is withheld from the character, not just left out by the author.
- Do not narrate the analysis. Let the scene carry it. For the scene's response structure, see `scene-and-sequel`; for dialogue under pressure, see `scene-dialogue-behavior`.

## 81. scene-dialogue-behavior

*Use when a dialogue scene reads like a transcript, characters take orderly turns, people nod and sip between every line, or a meeting, interview, or lab scene is all talk and props. Separates action that changes the next beat from decorative and procedural motion, and says where a beat belongs.*

Dialogue is people under pressure, not information sorted by speaker. A scene is not alive just because hands move or cups and files are handled. The useful question is whether a character's action or reaction reveals what they want, shows the last line landing, changes their next move, or changes the situation.

#### Three kinds of action

1. **Functional action** comes from what the character wants right now and changes how the exchange goes. It can expose concealment, hesitation, status, or expertise; claim or give up space; cut off access to an object or person; make a lie harder to keep up; force the other person to change their next question; or have a physical or social consequence. The test: **the reaction changes the next beat.**
2. **Decorative action** is motion with no meaning specific to this scene: a smile, frown, nod, sip, or look away that any character could do without affecting anything. It is not forbidden. It is weak as a repeated speaker tag or as proof that a static scene is moving.
3. **Procedural action** performs the task: opening a file, reading a result, moving equipment. It can be necessary and accurate, and a scene made only of procedure can still be socially dead. Tie it to pressure: who rushes, hesitates, protects something, hides a result, challenges an assumption, or notices a mistake.

#### Reading an exchange

Read it as a chain, at the level of the exchange and not line by line:

- the speaker has a local goal
- they choose what to say, leave out, soften, or distort for this listener
- the listener hears it through a different goal and a different amount of knowledge
- they control the reply, or fail to
- the reply changes the next line, action, or tactic

The effect may come late, or stay unresolved. A believable reply may evade, test, bargain, redirect, misunderstand, delay, or answer only the safest part.

#### Where a beat belongs

Put an action beat where something changes: new information lands, a private fact is nearly exposed, someone lies or changes their story, status or control shifts, someone decides whether to comply, evidence contradicts a favored explanation, composure weakens, or the relationship turns warmer, colder, or more dangerous. The beat can be small. Make it specific to this person, this pressure, and this place.

#### Do not overcorrect

- Action density is not a goal. Do not spread beats evenly, and do not give every line its own gesture, tone label, and inner reaction.
- Rapid dialogue can stay untagged when the voices, positions, and pressure are clear.
- An emotion label or a gesture on every line is not a repair. "Angrily" can clarify delivery, but it does not show what the anger makes the character do.
- A plain "He said nothing" is a legitimate sentence. It needs no gesture, motive, or consequence added to be allowed.
- When the dialogue or action already carries the response, do not add a label saying it answered.
- If a scene is meant to leave something unresolved, leave it. Do not invent an explanation to close the exchange.
- Keep the viewpoint selective. Do not cut to every participant for a reaction; let the viewpoint character notice what they would under this pressure, misread some of it, and miss the rest.

#### Checks

- `scene.dialogue.01` For each action beat in a sustained exchange, does the reaction change the next line, tactic, or situation? If not, it is decorative or procedural.
- `scene.dialogue.02` Are beats placed where something changes (information lands, a lie, a shift in power, a decision) and not spread evenly or attached to every line?
- `scene.dialogue.03` Is any procedural action (opening, reading, moving things) connected to human pressure, or is the scene static with props?
- `scene.dialogue.04` Was a flat exchange "fixed" by adding emotion labels or a gesture to every line? Untagged rapid dialogue and plain stated silence are fine when voices and pressure are clear.
- `scene.dialogue.05` Does the viewpoint character notice only what they would under this pressure, instead of the scene cutting to every participant's reaction?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- Comic, cozy, and ritual exchanges can run in deliberately tidy turns. Keep that when the scene is built on the rhythm.
- This is a judgment about what a beat does, not a list of banned gestures. Replacing every "did not answer" with a smile or a turn of the head repeats the same mechanical habit.
- For stiff or staged lines, see `prose-ai-tells` (dialogue patterns). For planning what each speaker wants before drafting, see `character-floating-outline-for-dialogue`.

## 82. prose-craft

*Use when prose is clean but flat, a scene reads like a screenplay or a summary, the viewpoint character has no inner life on the page, details feel generic, every moment gets the same weight, or a draft sounds competent and written by nobody. Gives the choices that make narration alive: a mind on the page, the detail that could only be here, exact sentences, proportion, and range.*

Taking the faults out of a passage leaves it clean, not alive. Prose reads as written by someone when its sentences show choices made for this scene and this character: what to notice, what to think, what to skip, where to slow down. A default is the choice that would do for any scene. This skill is about making the specific one.

A worked before-and-after for each section is in `examples.md` next to this file. Load it when you need to see the difference.

#### Put a mind on the page

In first person and close third, the narration is the viewpoint character's experience. A camera records what happens. A character notices some things and misses others, judges what they see, wants something from it, and gets part of it wrong.

- **Perception carries judgment.** What the character notices arrives with what they make of it, in their own vocabulary, with no "she thought" in front. Filmed: the clerk's desk was clean. Lived: the clerk's desk was clean, so somebody else was doing his work.
- **Let them think.** Working something out, rationalizing, rehearsing the next sentence, remembering what the thing in front of them calls up, changing their mind. Keep it specific and partial.
- **A thought is not an explanation.** An explanation belongs to the author and tells the reader what the scene means. A thought belongs to the character: it can be mistaken, and it leads to what they do next. If a reflection could not be wrong and changes nothing, it is a caption.
- **Want and dread drive the scene.** The character wants something now and is afraid of something else. The reader should feel both before the scene turns, through what the character attends to.
- **Carry feeling through attention and action.** A label ("she was afraid") and a report from the body (tight chest, held breath, racing pulse) are the two defaults. Stronger: what the feeling makes this character notice, avoid, do badly, or think about instead. When the character would know what they feel, they can say it plainly in their own words; the fault is the narrator's label laid over a feeling already shown.
- **Move closer and further.** Stand back to summarize routine. Come in close where it matters. A chapter held at one distance, all outside or all inside, goes flat.

Putting a mind on the page does not mean a row of rhetorical questions, a thought in italics in every paragraph, "she realized that" followed by the theme, or the character briefing the reader on what they already know.

#### Choose the detail

- **Go past the first one.** The first detail to arrive is the one any writer would use for any such place. Ask what is here that would be nowhere else, and what this person notices because of their trade, their history, and what they want right now.
- **Make it do a job.** A detail earns its place when it shows who is looking, changes what someone does, carries history, or sets up something later. One that works beats three that decorate. Not every object needs a habit or a backstory attached.

#### Say the exact thing

- **The literal sentence first.** The right noun and verb do more than an adjective and a simile. Use a comparison only when it shows something the literal sentence cannot, and take it from the character's world.
- **Describe what is there and what happens.** Description built from denials ("it did not rush", "it was not loud, exactly") gives the reader nothing to see.
- **Every sentence states something.** For any line that sounds good, say what it literally claims or what a reader would picture. If you cannot, or the picture is impossible, it is decoration.
- **Put the weight at the end.** The last words of a sentence and the last sentence of a paragraph are where the stress falls. Put the new or important thing there.
- **Let length follow the moment.** A long sentence can carry a chain of perceptions or a thought that will not stop. A short one stops. Vary them because the content varies, not on a pattern.

#### Proportion

- **Three speeds.** A scene moves moment by moment. A summary covers an hour or a year in a few lines, and can carry as much voice as a scene. A skip leaves it out. A chapter that uses only the first has no shape.
- **Spend the words where the scene turns.** The approach is short or gone. Start where the pressure starts. Stop once the change has happened; do not walk the character out of the room.
- **Uneven on purpose.** Scenes and paragraphs differ in length because what they hold differs.
- **Vary how scenes open and close.** A scene can open on a thought, a line of speech, or the middle of an action as well as on the setting. It can end on a line of dialogue, a decision, an interruption, or an action begun. Ending every scene on a quiet image is a habit, and so is opening every scene with the weather or the light.

#### Range

- **Not everything is restrained.** Somebody says the blunt thing. Something is funny, or ugly, or goes on too long. A loud scene is loud. When every exchange is oblique and every feeling understated, the restraint is the default.
- **Change the temperature** from one scene to the next.
- **One thing the plan did not predict.** A remark, an object, a reaction that surprises and is still right for these people.

#### Across chapters

A device that works once wears out by the third use. Compare the chapter with the ones before it: how each opens, how each closes, the comparisons and sentence shapes it leans on, what the viewpoint character does when upset. Change at least the opening and the closing.

#### Reworking a flat passage

Polishing words does not fix a passage that was never imagined. Go back into the moment: what does the character want here, what do they see, what do they think it means, what are they wrong about? Then write the passage again whole, with the same events and facts, at about the same length. Do not decorate the old sentences.

#### Checks

- `prose.craft.01` For each scene, where on the page does the viewpoint character want, judge, or think something through? A scene of only actions, objects, and speech reads as filmed.
- `prose.craft.02` For each reflection, is it the character's (partial, possibly wrong, leading to what they do next), or the author's caption on the scene?
- `prose.craft.03` How is each strong feeling carried? If mostly by labels or body reports (chest, breath, pulse, stomach), find what the feeling makes this character notice, avoid, or do.
- `prose.craft.04` Take the most prominent details. Could each sit unchanged in another story, and does each do a job (show who is looking, change an action, carry history, set something up)?
- `prose.craft.05` Does any sentence or image sound meaningful without stating anything exact? Say what it literally claims or what a reader would picture.
- `prose.craft.06` Where does the chapter spend its words? Does the moment that turns each scene get the most room, with routine summarized or skipped?
- `prose.craft.07` Is description built from what is present and what happens, or from denials and reflex comparisons?
- `prose.craft.08` Do the scenes differ in temperature, and does anyone say or do something blunt, funny, ugly, or unplanned?
- `prose.craft.09` Against the earlier chapters, does this one open and close in a different way, and avoid leaning on the same devices?
- `prose.craft.10` Which passage would a reader skim, and which is most alive? Quote both. Is the chapter's key moment the alive one?

#### Cautions and exceptions

- A deliberately external style is legitimate: hard-boiled narration, fable, a storyteller who stays outside, a minimalism the writer chose. Its life then comes from selection and voice, so the checks on detail, exactness, and proportion still apply and `prose.craft.01` does not.
- None of this is a quota. One thought where it counts, one detail that works. A thought in every paragraph and a striking detail in every sentence is a new default.
- The writer's voice file and their own pages come before anything here.
- This is line-level craft from common editing practice, not from the lecture series behind the plot and character frameworks.
- Related: `prose-ai-tells` for what to take out, `show-dont-tell` for how much room a trait deserves, `worldbuilding-viewpoint-filtered-description` for setting, `scene-dialogue-behavior` for speech.

### prose-craft: examples.md

Every passage here is invented for this library. Each pair uses the same events. The examples show the kind of choice to make; never reuse their images, details, or sentence shapes in a story.

#### 1. A mind on the page

Dara, a locksmith, has come to open her dead sister's flat. Close third.

**Filmed**
> Dara set her bag down in the hallway and looked at the door. The lock was a five-lever mortice, old but solid. She knelt, took out her tools, and began to work. From inside the flat came the hum of a refrigerator. After a few minutes the lock turned. She stood for a moment before pushing the door open.

**Lived**
> A five-lever mortice, the good kind, fitted by somebody who had cared. Dara had told Lena for years to change it, and Lena had said every time that nothing in the flat was worth stealing. Which was true, and was also why she would have been easy to rob. Dara knelt and got the picks out. Four minutes, on a lock like this, if she was awake. She took eleven, and noticed she was taking eleven, and went on taking them. Inside, the fridge was still running. Somebody would have to deal with that. She knew who.

**What changed:** the second has her trade's judgment of the lock, an argument with the dead that she is still having, a job she does badly and notices herself doing badly, and a dread that arrives as a practical thought. No feeling is named and no body part reports in. "She stood for a moment" keeps the feeling outside her; the eleven minutes puts it in what she does.

#### 2. A thought, not a caption

Okafor, a ship's engineer, has just lied to the captain about the fuel reserve.

**Caption**
> He watched her go. He had crossed a line, and he knew that nothing between them would be the same again.

**Thought**
> He watched her go. Six days of margin, he had said, and she had written it down. She wrote everything down. If the feed line held, he had four days to turn the lie into the truth. If it went, the log would show her name under his number.

**What changed:** the caption could close any scene about any lie. The thought is his arithmetic: it adds facts, he may be wrong about the feed line, and it sets what he does next.

#### 3. Feeling without a label or a body report

First person. Maren hears her former husband on the car radio, being interviewed about his book.

**Body report**
> My chest tightened. I gripped the steering wheel until my knuckles went white, and a wave of anger and grief rose in my throat.

**Attention and action**
> I missed the turn for the school. He was telling the interviewer about the year he spent learning to listen, and I drove another mile and a half down the bypass with the indicator going, working out which year that would have been.

**What changed:** the feeling is in a mistake she makes and in what her attention sticks on. The reader supplies the word for it.

#### 4. The detail that could only be here

A new orderly, Teodor, arrives at a field hospital.

**The first details to arrive**
> The tent smelled of blood and antiseptic. Wounded men lay in rows on narrow cots, and somewhere a man was moaning. Lanterns swung from the poles, casting long shadows.

**Chosen**
> Somebody had chalked a number on the sole of each boot that stuck out past the end of a cot. Teodor worked it out before anyone told him: the numbers were the order for the surgeon. Down at the far end, by the stove, where it was warmest, the soles were bare.

**What changed:** the first is the tent every reader has already seen. The second is one thing this hospital does, noticed by someone trying to learn its rules, and it tells him what the warm end is for.

#### 5. The exact thing

A fisherman's daughter watches the ice go out on the river.

**Decorative**
> The ice did not break all at once. It was not loud, exactly; it was more like a long sigh, as if the river were waking from a dream it had not wanted to leave. Something ancient moved beneath the surface.

**Exact**
> The ice went out at the bend first, where the current was. A grey seam opened along the far bank and widened to the width of a boat, and the slab on the near side tilted, slowly, until the fish-house her father had left out too long was standing at an angle on its runners.

**What changed:** the first is two denials, a comparison, and a sentence that claims nothing. A reader cannot picture any of it. The second says where, in what order, and what it does to something her family owns.

#### 6. Proportion

Yusra crosses a city under curfew to reach a pharmacy. The scene turns when the pharmacist recognizes her.

**Even:** three paragraphs of streets, patrols, and weather, each as detailed as the last; then the pharmacy; then the recognition in two lines.

**Weighted**
> It took two hours to cover eleven streets, and she spent most of one of them behind the bins of a bakery, listening to a patrol argue about football. The pharmacy had its shutter half down. She went under it.
>
> The man at the counter was counting blister packs into a paper bag and finished the row before he looked up. Then he kept looking. "You're Halim's girl," he said. "You were at the wedding. You sang."
>
> She had sung. She had been nine and furious about the dress.

**What changed:** the crossing gets two sentences and one live detail. The words go to the counter, where the scene turns.

#### 7. Range

Two brothers divide their mother's things.

**Restraint in every line**
> "Take the clock," Paul said.
>
> "You always liked it."
>
> "Did I." He turned it over in his hands. Outside, a car passed.

**Somebody says it**
> "Take the clock," Paul said.
>
> "I don't want the clock. I want to know why she paid your rent for nine years and I got a card at Christmas."
>
> Paul put the clock down on the stack of towels. "Eleven years," he said. "And you could have asked her."

**What changed:** the first is tasteful and could run for pages with nothing risked. In the second one brother says the blunt thing and the other answers with something worse. Obliqueness is one way people behave; when it is every line, it is a default.

## 83. prose-ai-tells

*Use when drafting fiction or asked to humanize, de-slop, or check machine-sounding prose. Detects and avoids over-explanation, stock rhythms, stilted dialogue, and narration that states the subtext.*

A model writes the choice that fits the widest range of readers and scenes. A human writer chooses for one reader and one scene, so their choices are uneven and specific. Every pattern here is a default choice, a way of sounding finished without adding anything.

Two rules follow. Every sentence you keep must give the reader something new. A tell counts in proportion to how rarely a careful writer would do it on purpose, so a few patterns justify an edit on one sighting and the rest need company from other tells in the same passage.

Taking the tells out makes prose clean, not good. A passage with none of these patterns can still have no mind in it, no detail that belongs to it, and one temperature throughout. For what to put in, load `prose-craft`.

The full list, with fiction examples, is in `patterns.md` next to this file. Load it for a detection pass or when you need an example.

#### Avoid mode (while drafting)

Keep these out of new prose from the start:

1. **Not X but Y.** Do not deny something nobody claimed so the real thing sounds bigger. Say the real thing.
2. **Interpretive closers.** Do not end a beat or a scene with a line that explains what it meant.
3. **Aphorisms in the narration.** Do not hand the reader a wise saying in place of the specific moment.
4. **Narrated significance.** Do not announce that a moment mattered ("little did she know"). Show the consequence.
5. **Forced triads.** Do not list in threes unless there are three.
6. **Same-subject runs.** Do not start four sentences in a row with the same word out of habit.
7. **Reaction riders.** Do not bolt a phrase onto an action to explain it ("..., his silence speaking volumes").
8. **Stacked hedges.** Do not soften every perception with seemed, almost, somehow, and a kind of.
9. **Named emotion after a shown one.** If the gesture shows the feeling, do not add a sentence that names it. Avoid "a flicker of" and "a mix of".
10. **Dialogue that states the subtext.** Let the line carry the feeling without naming it.
11. **Wrapper text.** Deliver the prose. No greeting, no framing line, no offer to continue.
12. **Description by denial.** Do not describe a thing by what it did not do or was not. Say what happened.
13. **Hollow depth.** Do not write a line that sounds meaningful and states nothing ("something shifted between them"). Say what changed.
14. **Reflex comparisons.** Do not reach for "like a", "as if", or "the way a" where the literal sentence would do.

Avoiding one habit often produces its nearest cousin: a named emotion becomes a tight chest, a cut explanation becomes a comparison, a banned phrase becomes its synonym. Replace a default with the specific thing, not with the next default.

#### Detect mode (review and revise)

1. Read the whole passage once before marking anything.
2. Mark each tell. Look at paragraph shape, not only sentences: three parallel beats, or the same closer after every scene, is the same tell at a larger scale.
3. Report each finding with the quoted text, where it is, the pattern, and a suggested fix. Report only what you would act on. If you looked at something and decided it is fine, leave it out of the list; do not report a device you are keeping.
4. In review, report. In revise, apply the fix: state the point directly, merge or vary sentences, or cut the line. Keep the writer's events, characters, and facts. Do not add story facts to repair a sentence.
5. Check the result: read it aloud, then search again for the tells that survive a rewrite (not X but Y, closers, triads, repeated openings, dashes) and for the cousins a rewrite introduces (body reports, comparisons, denials). The habit counter `habits.py` in the `story-check` skill folder lists several of these with line numbers.

If the writer gives a sample of their own prose, match its sentence length, word choice, and punctuation. The sample overrides the patterns here, including the dash rule.

#### When not to flag

- **Dialogue and in-world text.** A character may say anything. Flag a pattern in speech only when every character speaks in the same polished rhythm, or when the speech explains what the scene already showed.
- **Deliberate repetition.** Repeated openings used for rhythm or emphasis, and refrains.
- **A short paragraph that lands.** A one-line paragraph is a normal device. Flag it only when it restates or explains the beat before it.
- **Dashes for interruption** in dialogue, and in a writer's own style.
- **A real contrast.** Keep a "not X" when a character or the narrator corrects a belief that was actually stated or held.
- **Real uncertainty.** A hedge is fine when the point-of-view character really cannot tell.
- **Text before late 2022** and anything the writer wrote and can explain.

One tell is a hint. Several together, in one passage, are the signal. Do not rewrite a passage to remove a single weak tell.

### prose-ai-tells: patterns.md

Numbered strongest first within each family. Patterns 1 to 5, 13 to 15, and 24 justify an edit on one sighting. A pattern marked *weak alone* needs company from other tells in the same passage before you act. Every example below is invented for this library.

#### A. Staging instead of stating

The strongest tells. They sound significant and add nothing.

##### 1. Not X but Y

**Watch for:** it wasn't X, it was Y; not X but Y; not just X, but Y; the same contrast split over two sentences ("It wasn't fear. It was something older."); a tail like "..., not out of anger, but out of tiredness".
**Problem:** The negative half names something nobody claimed, so the positive half sounds larger. State the point directly.
**Before:**
> It wasn't anger she felt, but something quieter. It wasn't the storm that frightened her; it was the stillness after.
**After:**
> The stillness after the storm frightened her more than the storm had.
**Keep when:** a character corrects a belief that was actually stated ("I didn't come for the money." "Then what?"), or both halves carry information.

##### 2. Closers that explain the beat

**Watch for:** a final line that says what the moment meant ("It was the end of something, and the beginning of something else."); "In that moment, she understood"; "And that was enough"; a row of fragments that repeat a point ("No warning. No sound. No mercy.").
**Problem:** The line asks the reader to pause on a meaning instead of adding a fact. A short line can land a beat when it carries something new.
**Before:**
> She folded the letter and put it in the drawer. It was the end of something, and the beginning of something else.
**After:**
> She folded the letter and put it in the drawer.
**Keep when:** the short line adds a fact or turns the scene ("The door was already open.").

##### 3. Aphorisms in the narration

**Watch for:** "Silence, he understood, was the loudest answer"; "Hope is a dangerous thing"; X is the Y of Z; a general truth in place of this moment.
**Problem:** A saying dresses an ordinary point as wisdom. Replace it with what happens.
**Before:**
> Silence, he understood, was the loudest kind of answer.
**After:**
> He waited. She gave him nothing, and the clock went on ticking.
**Keep when:** a character says it, in voice, or the book's narrator uses sayings sparingly as part of an established style.

##### 4. Arguing with no one

**Watch for:** "She was not the kind of woman who..."; "It wasn't that she didn't care"; a narrator rejecting a reading nobody offered.
**Problem:** The text defends against an objection that appears nowhere else.
**Before:**
> Ines did not run. She was not the kind of woman who ran. She walked, deliberately, toward the pier.
**After:**
> Ines walked toward the pier.

##### 5. Forced triads

**Watch for:** three adjectives, three nouns, three parallel actions or beats, repeated throughout a passage.
**Problem:** Ideas arrive in threes to sound complete, whether the scene has three parts or not. Check that each item adds something. Keep three when there are three.
**Before:**
> The market was loud, crowded, and alive. She smelled fish, smoke, and rain.
**After:**
> The market was loud. She smelled fish and rain.
**Keep when:** three is a real count, or the rhythm is deliberate and rare.

#### B. Rhythm by rule

##### 6. Repeated sentence openings

**Watch for:** several sentences in a row starting with the same subject, usually *she* or *he*.
**Problem:** Repetition handled by habit instead of by ear. Merge sentences, change the subject, or begin with the action. Do not ban the word. Repeating an opening on purpose for rhythm is a device.
**Before:**
> She opened the door. She stepped inside. She looked at the table. She saw the key.
**After:**
> She opened the door and stepped inside. The key lay on the table.

##### 7. Dashes as the universal connector (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** em dashes or spaced hyphens joining clauses throughout a passage.
**Problem:** A dash lets the writer skip choosing how two clauses relate. One dash is ordinary; a page full of them is not. Replace most with a comma, period, colon, or parentheses.
**Before:**
> The tide, which had been rising all night, finally turned. The guild — or what remained of it — gathered at the quay — silent, watching.
**After:**
> The tide, which had been rising all night, finally turned. The guild, or what remained of it, gathered silently at the quay.
**Keep when:** it marks an interruption in dialogue ("But I—"), or the writer's own sample uses dashes.

##### 8. Stacked hedges (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** seemed, almost, somehow, a kind of, something like, as though, a sort of, piled on one perception.
**Problem:** Hedges are added until nothing in the scene is stated. Keep one when the point-of-view character truly cannot tell.
**Before:**
> It seemed almost as though the water was somehow moving of its own accord, in a way that felt vaguely wrong.
**After:**
> The water was moving by itself.

#### C. Inflation

##### 9. Narrated significance

**Watch for:** "little did she know"; "a moment that would change everything"; "she would remember this for the rest of her days"; "everything was about to change".
**Problem:** The narration announces importance instead of earning it. Show the consequence, or cut.
**Before:**
> Little did she know, this single decision would change the course of her life forever.
**After:**
> (Cut. Let the next scene show what the decision cost.)

##### 10. Reaction riders

**Watch for:** an -ing phrase or appended clause that explains an action: "..., his silence speaking volumes"; "..., a smile that carried the weight of everything unsaid".
**Problem:** The rider tells the reader what to feel about a simple action. Keep the action.
**Before:**
> He shrugged, his indifference speaking volumes about how little he cared.
**After:**
> He shrugged.

##### 11. Stock AI words (*weak alone, strong in groups*)

**Watch for:** tapestry, testament, symphony (of), dance (of), palpable, kaleidoscope, vibrant, nestled, delve, meticulous, quietly or softly used as an emotion marker, a sense of, a mix of X and Y.
**Problem:** Models use these far more than people do, especially together. A single use in the right place is fine.
**Before:**
> The village was a tapestry of colors, a testament to generations of craft.
**After:**
> The houses were painted blue and ochre, each one patched by the family before it.

#### D. Leftovers

##### 12. Chatbot wrapper

**Watch for:** "Here's the scene you asked for:"; "Certainly!"; "I hope this captures..."; "Would you like me to continue?"; a note after the prose about what was changed.
**Problem:** A drafting assistant's wrapper remains around delivered prose. Remove it and keep the prose.
**Before:**
> Here's a revised version of the scene! I've tightened the dialogue. Would you like me to continue?
**After:**
> (The scene alone.)

#### E. Fiction habits

Habits that show up in machine-written scenes in particular. Patterns 13 to 15 act on one sighting. Patterns 16 to 20 need company.

##### 13. Emotion named right after it is shown

**Watch for:** a gesture or reaction followed by a sentence that names the feeling ("Her hands shook. She was terrified.").
**Problem:** The second sentence repeats what the first already showed.
**Before:**
> Dev's hands shook as he opened the letter. He was terrified.
**After:**
> Dev's hands shook as he opened the letter.
**Keep when:** the narration adds a cause or a contradiction the gesture does not show ("His hands shook, though not from fear.").

##### 14. "A flicker of" and "a mix of" emotions

**Watch for:** a flicker, flash, ghost, hint, or trace of an emotion or a smile; a mix, mixture, or cocktail of two feelings; "something like" a feeling.
**Problem:** The feeling arrives hedged and pre-packaged. Pick the specific thing the character does or notices.
**Before:**
> A flicker of hope crossed his face, mixed with a hint of fear.
**After:**
> He glanced at the door and did not ask her to stay.

##### 15. Dialogue that states the subtext

**Watch for:** a character says outright what they feel or what the scene means; characters tell each other what both already know ("As you know, our father died").
**Problem:** The speech does the reader's work for them. Let the line carry the feeling without naming it.
**Before:**
> "I feel betrayed," his sister said, "because you promised you'd stay and you didn't."
**After:**
> "You said you'd stay."
**Keep when:** the character is the sort who says such things, or a plain statement is the event of the scene.

##### 16. Stock body reactions (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** a breath she didn't know she was holding; a heart pounding or hammering; a shiver down the spine; white knuckles; a clenched jaw; every feeling in a chapter reported from the chest, throat, stomach, or breath.
**Problem:** Each is common in published fiction too. A passage that leans on several is running on defaults, and a body report in place of every named emotion is the same label in a different place. Find what the feeling makes this character notice, avoid, or do.
**Before:**
> He let out a breath he didn't know he was holding, a shiver running down his spine.
**After:**
> He kept his eyes on the stairs and stopped breathing until the footsteps passed.

##### 17. Stock atmosphere lines (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** the air thick with tension; silence stretching; "somewhere, a clock ticked"; the smell of ozone or petrichor; an offstage sound dropped into a pause in the dialogue (a chain dragged, a dog barked, a stove ticked).
**Problem:** Generic mood laid over the scene instead of a detail that belongs to this place. Swapping the clock for another ticking object keeps the habit.
**Before:**
> The air was thick with tension, and somewhere, a clock ticked.
**After:**
> The kettle had been boiling for a minute. Nobody got up.

##### 18. Tag and gesture filler (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** "said softly", "whispered", "voice barely above a whisper" repeated across a page; nodded, smiled, sighed, sipped, or looked away between every pair of lines; a scene whose only motion is handling props or doing a task.
**Problem:** Decorative action (any character could do it without changing anything) and procedural action with no human pressure in it fill pauses without adding character. Cut them, or replace them with an action that changes the next beat: it reveals an agenda, forces a new tactic, or changes the situation.
**Keep when:** the beat sits where something changes (information lands, a lie, a shift in power, a decision), or an untagged exchange is already clear without it. A plain "He said nothing" is a sentence, not a defect, and a flat exchange is not repaired by a gesture on every line.

##### 19. Reflex comparisons (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** two or more "like a..." or "as if..." comparisons in one paragraph, or one in most paragraphs of a page; the shapes "like a man who...", "the way you would...", "as if it had always..."; objects given human intentions for charm ("a pen laid down like a man who had not told his chair").
**Problem:** Comparisons crowd each other and none lands, and a comparison in place of the literal sentence leaves the reader with the vehicle and no picture. Say the literal thing, and keep the one comparison that shows what it cannot.
**Before:**
> The marker sat in the stone as though the stone had grown around it, cold like a held breath, the way old iron is.
**After:**
> The marker would not turn. She put her weight on it and the stone step shifted before the iron did.

##### 20. One register for every speaker (*weak alone*; whole-chapter pass only)

**Watch for:** every character speaks in the same polished, complete sentences of similar length and vocabulary.
**Problem:** Voices are interchangeable. Only judge this across a full chapter, never from a short passage.

##### 21. Lines that answer each other in tidy pairs

**Watch for:** each line of an argument answers the one before it exactly, often reusing its wording ("The ledger is the guild's." / "The guild signed the ledger."); every question gets a direct answer; nobody interrupts, dodges, or leaves a line unanswered.
**Problem:** Real arguments slide past each other. People answer the question they wish they had been asked, change the subject, or go quiet. Tidy call and response reads as staged.
**Before:**
> "You moved the marker," Ines said.
> "The marker was already moving," Tomas said.
> "Markers do not move on their own."
> "This one did."
**After:**
> "You moved the marker."
> Tomas went on coiling the rope. "Tide's turning. You want a ride back or not?"
> "Tomas."
> "It's a long swim."
**Keep when:** the characters are trading lines on purpose, as banter, ritual, or a duel of wits, and the scene is built on that rhythm.

##### 22. A cornered character explains their position in a tidy speech

**Watch for:** a character caught out, accused, or losing an argument responds with a complete, reasoned paragraph that lays out how things work and why they were right; the explanation reads as a briefing for the reader.
**Problem:** People under pressure deflect, appeal to loyalty or status, ask who told you, offer food, or attack. A clean explanation from the losing side is exposition wearing a character.
**Before:**
> "Every survey crew rounds the counts," the clerk said. "The guild knows it, the harbor knows it, and the ships expect it. A rounded count is a courtesy, not a lie, and no inspector has ever objected."
**After:**
> The clerk laughed, short. "Who's been showing you the counts?"
> "Nobody."
> "Your father know you're in the office this late?"
**Keep when:** the character is the kind who lectures when cornered, and the speech itself is the characterization.

#### F. Second-generation habits

What appears once the patterns above have been avoided: clean, restrained prose that still reads as written by nobody.

##### 23. Description by denial (*weak alone*)

**Watch for:** narration that says what did not happen or what a thing was not: "It did not rush." "It was not loud, exactly." "She did not look at him." Three or more in a passage; "not quite"; "not exactly".
**Problem:** A denial gives the reader nothing to see, and a run of them sounds precise without choosing anything. Say what happened.
**Before:**
> The water did not rush. It was not stuck, exactly. She did not move.
**After:**
> The water slid past her shins at a walking pace. She kept still.
**Keep when:** the reader expected the thing denied (asked a question, he did not answer), or the absence is the event.

##### 24. Hollow depth

**Watch for:** a line that gestures at meaning and states none: "Something shifted between them." "It was the kind of quiet that had weight." "...and a great many things about it would be true." An image that cannot be pictured (a gull standing on the water).
**Problem:** It sounds like insight and claims nothing. Ask what the sentence literally states. If there is an answer, write that; if there is none, cut the line.
**Before:**
> Something had changed in the room, though she could not have said what.
**After:**
> Nobody had sat back down.
**Keep when:** the character truly cannot identify the thing, and the story is going to.

##### 25. One temperature (*whole-chapter pass only*)

**Watch for:** every scene quiet and controlled; every speaker dry and oblique; every feeling understated; every scene ending on a held image or a short flat line.
**Problem:** Restraint applied to everything is a default, as melodrama applied to everything would be. Nobody risks anything and no scene differs from the last.
**Keep when:** the writer's voice file asks for it, or the story is built on that reserve and breaks it once on purpose.
