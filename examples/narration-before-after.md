# Narration, before and after

Both drafts come from the same beats (`evals/prose-pairs/pair-2/outline.md`), the same voice file, and the same model. One was written with `story-draft` before the narration guidance existed; the other with it. The full drafts are in `evals/prose-pairs/pair-2/`. In a blind read, one reader preferred the second.

The scene: alone on the quay, Ines places a marker she has no permission to place, to see what it does. It fixes the channel for good.

Read this as a single illustrative sample, not a benchmark.

## The moment before she does it

**Before**

> She knelt at the edge of the quay and wedged the bar into the gap between two stones where the water came up green and slow. Then she waited, because a thing placed was a thing worth watching.
>
> For a while nothing happened.

**After**

> It was a very small channel. It led nowhere she was allowed to go.
>
> She took the marker out and held it up and looked at it. It looked like a tent peg, which she found a relief.
>
> "Just to see," she said aloud, to nobody. It sounded exactly like something a person says before being sorry.

The first reports what her hands do and closes on a saying. The second has her talking herself into it: she makes the channel small, makes the marker ordinary, and then hears her own excuse. Nothing names what she feels.

## What the water does

**Before**

> Then the water at the bend drew in on itself. It did not rush. It was more like a held breath being let go, a smooth sliding pull that took the green with it, and a line opened out across the harbor mouth, straight as a ruled edge, from the quay to the far channel markers and past them.

**After**

> The water stopped leaning.
>
> It kept moving, the way a river moves, but the thread of the channel lay along the pier as straight as a rule, and the foam ran down its length in a clean line, and the shoal beyond it, which for six years had sat in the wrong place at the wrong time, was simply and plainly where a shoal ought to be.
>
> It was the best route she had ever seen. She stood in the cold and looked at it and could not find a single thing to correct.

The first describes by denial and comparison. The second says what the water did and then gives the surveyor's verdict on it, which is where the reader learns why she will do this again.

## The last image

**Before**

> Behind her a gull came back and landed on the new straight water, and stood on it as if it had always been there.

**After**

> Up on the pier, a ferry was coming in along the old route, wide and slow, round the shoal as it always had. The pilot raised a hand to her. She raised one back, and kept it wet in her pocket.

The first sounds like an ending and cannot be pictured: a gull does not stand on water. The second shows the secret being kept.

## What changed in the guidance

- Before writing a scene, the drafter settles what the viewpoint character wants and dreads, what she has wrong, and what only she would notice.
- Narration carries her judgments and her thinking. A thought that could be mistaken and leads to what she does next is kept; a sentence that explains the scene to the reader is not.
- The literal sentence comes first. Description says what is there and what happens.
- The drafter reads the chapter back, rewrites the passage a reader would skim, and asks of each line that sounds good what it actually states.

The second draft still leans on comparisons more than it should ("the way a river moves", "as straight as a rule"). The guidance moved the scene; it did not make the draft finished.
