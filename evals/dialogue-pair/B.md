The tally office kept its lamp lit after the harbor bells stopped, because Teo Varga did not trust the dark to leave his books alone. Ines found him at the long desk with his sleeves pushed up and a heel of bread beside the inkwell.

She set the three ledgers down one at a time. The last one she dropped, a little, so it made a noise.

"You're late for supper," Teo said.

"I wasn't invited."

"You're always invited." He tore the bread in half and held out the bigger piece. She did not take it. He put it down on the blotter, on top of a column of figures, and she watched the grease spread into the paper.

"Spring crossings," she said. "The guild count is four thousand and eleven. I stood on the south landing for the whole of spring, Uncle. I counted what went over. You know what I got?"

"You were on the landing for the whole of spring."

"Two thousand six hundred and some."

"Landings aren't the ferry." He wiped his fingers on his trousers. "Boats cross at the Weir, too. At the mill steps. You'd have to be standing in three places."

"Then I'd have counted the mill steps. I did the mill steps in summer." She opened the second ledger to a page she had marked with a bit of twine. "Same thing. Same gap. And the gap is the same size, give or take, every season back to when I was eleven." She turned the book to face him. "That's your hand. That's the hand that showed me how to carry the tens."

Teo looked at the page for a while. He did not lean in to read it. He had never needed to lean in.

"You were quick at the tens," he said. "Your mother thought it was a trick. She thought somebody had been whispering the answers."

"Did you add these?"

"I add everything that comes through that door."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No." He picked up the bread again and put it down again. "Who knows you're here?"

"Nobody."

"Good girl."

"Don't." She heard her voice go up and brought it down. "Don't say that like I did something right. Say whether the numbers are wrong. You can say yes. I'll know what you mean by it."

"I'd say," Teo said, "that you've got your father's walk. I noticed it at the funeral. Heavy on the left heel."

"Uncle."

"The guild pays by the count. Always has. You'd know that, if you ever read the charter instead of the ledgers." He closed the second book gently, over her twine. "Go home. Eat something. Come to me in the morning and we'll go through the landing sheets together, line by line, and you can show me where I've gone astray."

"You'd let me."

"I'd let you sit in the chair."

It was not a no. She stood there trying to find the place in it where a no should have been, and the door opened behind her.

Aunt Bel came in shaking rain from her shawl, which was odd, because it had not rained since Tuesday. She had a covered dish in both hands. She looked at the ledgers, then at Ines, then at the dish, and set the dish on the corner of the desk as if the dish were the reason for the visit.

"Lamb," Bel said. "Cold, but it's lamb. Who's got the lamp turned so low? Teo, you'll ruin your eyes."

"Ines has been showing me some arithmetic."

"Has she." Bel found a chair and sat in it. "She always did like sums. When she was small she'd count the stairs out loud, every time, and then tell you how many there'd been."

"Forty-one," Ines said.

"Forty-one." Bel smiled at her. It was a lovely smile and it stayed exactly where it was. "Sit down, love. You look like you've walked here from the Weir."

"I did."

"There you are, then."

Ines did not sit. She said, "Did you know?" and Bel said, "Know what, love," and then neither of them said anything, and in the quiet the lamp ticked.

The door opened a third time.

Her mother did not shake out a shawl or bring a dish. She came in with her hat still on and shut the door and stood against it, the way she stood at the back of the hall at the guild dinners. She was looking at the three ledgers in a row on the desk. She counted them. Ines saw her do it, saw her lips move once.

"Who told you I was here?" Ines said.

"Nobody has to tell me where you are." Her mother took off the hat. "You've got ink on your cheek."

Ines put a hand up. It was not there. She felt the heat come into her face anyway.

"I can show you," she said. "All three of you. It's not hard. It's a column of subtraction." She had the first ledger open again, and she was talking fast now, finding the pages by thumb. "Here. Autumn, last year. Here, the year before. The guild logs a crossing that nobody made and the guild pays the ferrymasters on it and the ferrymasters pay the dues on it and the dues come back down to —" She stopped. "Come back down to who?"

Nobody looked at the ledger. Bel looked at the lamb. Teo looked at the ceiling beam, where the damp had made a stain shaped roughly like a hand.

"The roof was done in the spring," her mother said. "The new slates."

"I know about the roof."

"Your brother's boots."

"Mama."

"The stair rail. You like the stair rail."

"I'm not —" Ines pushed the heel of her hand against her eye and found the ink, and smeared it. "I'm not asking about the stair rail."

"No," her mother said. "You're asking about everything else, in front of your uncle, in his own office, with the lamp on. At night. And the window not shut."

Bel rose and shut the window.

There was a long time then. Ines could hear the harbor, the small knock of hulls against the wall. She had been right for eleven days, ever since the twine had gone into the second ledger, and she had thought being right would feel like something. It felt like standing in a room where somebody had just stopped a clock.

"Teo," her mother said. "You'll want to lock up."

"I will."

"Ines will carry those home. Tonight. All of them." She was already looking at the ledgers, not at her daughter. "They'll be safer in the kitchen. Under the bench, where the good knives are. I'll make room."

"Safer from what?" said Ines.

Her mother put her hat back on. She did it carefully, with both hands, and tucked a grey strand under the brim.

"Take the big one first," she said. "It's heavy."

Ines picked up the oldest ledger. It was heavy. Teo held the door for her, and as she went through, he laid the bread on the top of the stack, the half she had not wanted.
