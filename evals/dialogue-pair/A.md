The tally office smelled of lamp oil and wet rope, the way it had when Ines was eleven and tall enough to see over the counter only if she stood on the ledger stool. Teo had left one lamp lit. He was at the long desk with his sleeves rolled, counting something with his lips, and he did not look up when she shut the door.

"You're late," he said. "Supper was an hour ago."

"I wasn't asked to supper."

"You were always asked. You just never came." He turned a page. "What have you got there?"

Ines set the three crossing ledgers on the desk, one on top of the other, squared at the corners because he had taught her to square them. Then she set down the paper she had made, a single sheet, her own hand, columns ruled with the edge of a ruler.

Teo read the heading. He read down the first column. His thumb, which was stained blue at the nail from forty years of the guild's ink, moved along the margin and stopped.

"Ferry tallies, six years," she said. "I counted the tickets in the harbor store, the ones nobody burns. Then I counted what the ledgers say. The ledgers say more. Every season, more. By about a fifth."

"Tickets get lost."

"Lost tickets make the ledger lower than the count. Not higher."

"Hm." He picked up the sheet and held it at arm's length, as he did with everything now. "Your sevens have gotten sloppy."

"Uncle."

"I'm only saying. If you're going to bring a thing to a man at night, bring it clean." He put the sheet down. "Who else has seen this?"

"Nobody."

"Good."

She had expected that word to feel like a door opening. It felt like a hand on the back of her neck.

"Is it true?" she said.

Teo took off his spectacles and pressed the bridge of his nose. The lamp threw his shadow up the wall behind him, over the pigeonholes where the season books stood in their rows. "The harbor guild has a way of counting that is older than you," he said. "It has been argued over by better heads than yours or mine."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No. It isn't."

He waited, and she waited, and the lamp ticked. She had seen him do this to captains who came in swearing about their fees. He would let the quiet go on until they filled it with something they hadn't meant to say. She knew the trick, and she still felt the pull to speak first.

She held out. It cost her.

"You taught me that a ledger is a promise," she said at last. "That if the number's wrong, somebody pays for it."

"I taught you a great deal." Teo smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the stool. "You were a good student. You are a good surveyor. The guild is lucky to have you, and you ought to be careful about what you spend that on."

"That isn't a no."

"Is it a yes?"

She opened her mouth and found she had nothing to put in it. He had not denied it. He had not said it was so. He had handed her the sheet back with the sevens corrected in his head, and she was standing in the lamplight with a fact she could not make him hold.

The door opened behind her. Cold came in off the quay, and with it Aunt Bel, in her good wool coat, which she did not wear to the office.

"Teo said you might be here," Bel said, though Ines had not heard anyone say it. She looked at the ledgers, then at the sheet, and did not ask what it was. That was how Ines knew she already knew.

"Bel," Teo said.

"I brought bread." She set a cloth bundle on the corner of the desk, on top of the sheet, and smoothed it flat. "You'll want to eat."

"I want to know why you're not surprised," said Ines.

"Because I've known you twenty-four years." Bel pulled out the visitor's chair and sat as though this were a visit. "You find a loose thread and you pull till the sleeve comes off. You did it with the Ostrand boy's kite string. You did it with the pantry latch."

"This isn't a latch."

"No," Bel said. "It's the roof."

Nobody spoke. Down on the water a ferry horn sounded once, low, the nine o'clock boat going out with its tickets counted.

The door opened again. Ines's mother stood in it with her hood still up and her hands inside her sleeves. She looked at the three of them and at the lamp and at the ledgers, and she did not come in any farther.

"Mama."

"I walked down to see if you had eaten."

"Did Bel send for you?"

"Nobody sent for me." Her mother pushed the hood back. Her hair was coming loose from its pins, which it never did. "I thought you might be here. That's all."

Ines turned the sheet around on the desk, under Bel's bread, and tapped the bottom line. "It's here in my hand. Six years, a fifth over. The harbor store has the tickets to prove it. I'm not guessing."

"I know you're not," her mother said.

It was quiet again. Ines understood that this was the answer, all of it, and that it had been given in four words, by the one person she had not thought to prepare for.

"You knew," she said.

"This house," her mother said, and stopped, and began again. "The house on Sallow Street has a new roof because of that line. Your cousins have shoes. Your father's debt at the chandler's is a thing we do not speak of because it is gone." She said it flatly, like a list read back at a counter. "I'm not saying it's right."

"Then say it's wrong."

"Ines." Bel's voice was gentle. "Not here."

"Where, then?"

"Nowhere," said Teo, and for the first time all evening he sounded tired. "That's rather the point of it."

Ines looked from face to face. Not one of them looked away from her. She would have preferred it if they had. She had walked in with a number and she was going to walk out with it, the same number, still true, and nobody in the room would ever say it back to her.

She picked up her sheet from under the bread and folded it in thirds.

"Leave that," said Teo.

"It's mine."

"It's a piece of paper." He rose, and he was not as tall as she remembered. "The ledgers are the guild's. I'll lock them up tonight."

"Not tonight." Her mother's hand came out of her sleeve and rested on the topmost ledger. "Let her take them home. Let her have them for a few days. She'll want to look them over again, and she should do it where she's comfortable."

Teo looked at his sister-in-law for a long moment, and then at Bel, and whatever passed between them was too fast and too old for Ines to follow.

"A few days," he said.

"Bring them home, Ines," her mother said. "Put them somewhere safe. Somewhere no one will come looking."

Ines took the first ledger up in both arms. It was heavier than it had any right to be. Her mother held the door for her, the way she did for a child with her hands full, and the cold came in around them.

Nobody had asked her to bring them home to read. She walked out into the night with the whole of her family's roof held against her chest, and she knew exactly what she was being asked to do with it.
