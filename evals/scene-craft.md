# Evals: scene-craft hub

Each entry is a realistic writer prompt, the skill expected to trigger, and what a good answer must show. Run each prompt with the skills installed and without, and compare.

---

## scene-01 - The numbing climax
**Prompt:** My space-heist novella has the crew escape a collapsing station, fight off guards in the vault, and then fight again on the shuttle, all in four chapters. Beta readers say it's exciting but exhausting, and the big death in chapter four did not land. Do I just cut action?
**Should trigger:** `scene-and-sequel`
**Must show:**
- Diagnoses a run of scenes with no sequel, so consequences never register
- Proposes a response beat after the death, sized to the book's length and pace
- Notes that a short, fast book can keep sequels brief, so the fix is not to cut action wholesale
**Must not rely on:** generic advice such as "vary your pacing" or "add quiet moments" with no scene and response structure

## scene-02 - Prove it or state it
**Prompt:** A critique partner marked every line like "Tomas was a stubborn man" with "show, don't tell". My cozy fantasy has a tavern keeper who appears once and a hero whose stubbornness drives the plot. Do I really have to dramatize all of them?
**Should trigger:** `show-dont-tell`
**Must show:**
- Separates the apprentice habit of showing from the journeyman judgment of choosing
- Says to dramatize the hero's plot-driving trait and state the one-time character's trait in a line
- Uses story importance, scene type, and character importance as the test
**Must not rely on:** "always show, never tell" or "balance showing and telling" with no way to decide

## scene-03 - Where the two sources disagree on telling
**Prompt:** I'm editing a draft. A line says "Mira was terrified of heights" about a minor guide who appears in one scene. A style guide I follow says any named emotion or trait should be turned into behavior. Should I rewrite it as her gripping the rail and refusing to look down?
**Should trigger:** `show-dont-tell`
**Must show:**
- States plainly that the two positions differ, rather than silently picking one
- Gives a default: decide the word budget by story importance, so a minor character's trait can stay as a plain statement
- Says when to follow the stricter line-level advice instead, such as when the moment is chosen for showing or the sentence reads as a flat label
**Must not rely on:** a one-sided "yes, show it" or "no, telling is fine" answer

## scene-04 - Narration for a big cast
**Prompt:** My epic has seven viewpoint characters and a long history to convey. I was drafting in first person present because it feels immediate, but it keeps getting confusing. What should I use, and can I dip into other characters' heads inside a scene?
**Should trigger:** `viewpoint-and-tense`
**Must show:**
- Says first person strains beyond one or two narrators and that third suits large casts
- Names omniscient narration as a legitimate option, with the cost that withholding information feels like a cheat
- Treats the rule against entering other heads as a convention, and gives when to follow the stricter single-head advice (executing a limited scene, catching slips)
- Notes tense is largely reader preference
**Must not rely on:** "pick whichever feels natural" or a flat "never head-hop"

## scene-05 - Clean but flat
**Prompt:** My editor says chapter six is "clean but dead". I took out every adverb, every named emotion, every cliché. It's a woman breaking into her late brother's workshop and it reads like stage directions. I don't know what's left to fix.
**Should trigger:** `prose-craft`
**Must show:**
- Says the problem is what is missing, not what is wrong: nothing on the page is the character's judgment, thought, or want
- Separates a character's thought (partial, possibly wrong, leading to what she does next) from the author explaining the scene, so the fix is not to put the named emotions back
- Gives a way to carry feeling other than a label or a body report: what she notices, avoids, or does badly
- Says to write the passage again from inside the moment, not to decorate its sentences
**Must not rely on:** "add more emotion", "add sensory detail", or "show, don't tell" with no way to tell a thought from an explanation

## scene-06 - Every detail is the expected one
**Prompt:** Here's my tavern: "The room was warm and smoky. A fire crackled in the hearth, and the smell of roasting meat hung in the air. Rough men hunched over tankards." A beta reader said she'd read this tavern forty times. What do I change?
**Should trigger:** `prose-craft`
**Must show:**
- Names these as the first details to arrive, the ones any such place would get
- Asks what is in this tavern and nowhere else, and what this viewpoint character notices because of their trade, history, and what they want right now
- Says a detail earns its place by doing a job, and one that works beats three that decorate
- Offers the questions or one short illustration, and does not rewrite the writer's passage for them unless asked
**Must not rely on:** "use all five senses" or "be more specific" with no test for which detail to keep

## scene-07 - The style is external on purpose
**Prompt:** I write hard-boiled crime. My narrator reports what people do and say and almost never tells you what he thinks. A workshop leader told me to "get inside his head more". Is she right?
**Should trigger:** `prose-craft`
**Must show:**
- Treats a deliberately external style as legitimate and does not push interior thought into it
- Says where the life comes from in that style: what the narrator selects, how exactly he says it, and his attitude in the telling
- Says which questions still apply (detail, exactness, proportion, range) and which does not
**Must not rely on:** a flat "yes, add interiority" or "no, ignore her"
