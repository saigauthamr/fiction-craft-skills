Teo kept the tally office warmer than the rest of the guildhall, and after hours he kept it with one lamp. Ines found him where she knew she would, at the high desk, with his pen cleaned and laid across the inkwell like a man who had finished for the night and had not told his chair.

She set the ledgers down in front of him. Seven years of crossings, the guild's copies, bound in the green cloth he had taught her to open by the spine so it didn't crack. Her own pages sat on top: the ferrymen's slate counts for the same days, copied out in her small square hand.

The office smelled of lamp oil and wet wool and the pear he ate every evening at this hour, down to the stem. Along the back wall ran the pigeonholes where each ferry's monthly sheet went, three hundred and some slots, every one labeled in his handwriting. She had spent her first summer as a girl copying those labels fresh, and he had paid her in sugared almonds and never once corrected a letter.

"You're late for supper," Teo said. "Your aunt will have words."

"Look at the second of Harvest. Yours says four hundred and twelve crossings." She turned her pages to him. "The slates say three hundred and thirty."

"Slates get wiped."

"Not on the second of Harvest. Tam Oyelaran kept his for the inquiry about the sunk barge. He kept all of them." She had walked to the Low Stairs to ask, and Tam had given her a cup of tea and the slates in a flour sack. "I did the whole year. Then I did the other six."

Teo picked up the top ledger and held it the way he had held it when she was eleven, with one thumb in the gutter. He turned two pages. He did not look at her numbers.

"You were always quick with a column," he said. "I told your mother. Ines will go further than any of us."

"It's a quarter over. Every month. Every year, and it never dips, Teo. Nothing real runs that smooth."

"Rain dips it. Ice dips it."

"Then show me the rain."

He turned another page. The lamp ticked. Down in the yard somebody dragged a chain across the stones and stopped, and Ines noticed she had been counting the links.

"Who've you shown this to?" he said.

"Nobody."

"Good girl."

"That isn't what I asked you."

"You asked me about rain." He closed the ledger, gently, and set it square on the others. "Have you eaten? There's bread in the drawer. Bel does the heel for me, I can't chew it."

"The levy is charged per crossing. If the count is a quarter high, someone is paying for crossings that never happened, and it isn't the guild. It's the ferrymen. It's Tam."

"Tam has a flour sack of slates, apparently. He seems well organized."

"Say it was a mistake. Say it started as one, and nobody caught it. I'll take that."

Teo smiled at her. He pushed the drawer open with his knee and took out the bread and broke it, and offered her half, and she did not take it, and he ate his own piece standing.

"I never taught you to be sure of things," he said. "I taught you to check."

"I checked."

The door opened without a knock.

Aunt Bel came in with her shawl already off, which meant she had meant to stay. She looked at the ledgers, at the slate copies, at Teo's hands, and at none of them for long. "There you are," she said, to the room.

"Ines has been doing sums," Teo said.

"I can see that. In the dark."

"There's a lamp."

"Is there." Bel took the second stool and sat on it as though it were hers, which it was. "Your mother's on the stairs. She walked fast. She'll want to sit."

"Did you tell her to come?" Ines said.

"Nobody tells your mother anything. Teo, your collar."

He fixed it. Ines watched him fix it. Her mother came in a moment later with her hand on the doorframe and her breath short, and did not say hello to anyone. She looked at the green ledgers for a long time, the way she looked at a pot she suspected of being cracked.

"How much of this have you told people," her mother said.

"Why does everyone ask me that?"

"Everyone?"

"Teo asked it first."

Her mother's eyes went to Teo and came back. "Then you've got your answer about what people want."

"I don't want an answer about what people want. I want him to say the counts are wrong."

"Nobody's saying anything is wrong." Bel's voice was mild. "Ines. Who paid for your boots?"

"That isn't."

"I only ask."

"The guild paid for my boots. I'm a surveyor. It's in the contract."

"The guild paid for your apprenticeship," Bel said. "Your boots were Teo."

Teo looked at the lamp. Ines's mother had found a loose thread on her sleeve and was winding it round one finger, tighter, until the fingertip went white.

"Four years of boots," Ines said. "That's not a house."

"Who said anything about the house?" Bel said, quickly, and then, less quickly, "Nobody did."

Teo coughed into his fist. It was a small cough, the kind he used in guild meetings when a motion was about to be seconded that he didn't care for. The motion had always died of it.

"The roof on Cooper Lane was done in the spring," her mother said at last, to the thread. "Before it was done, the rain came into your room. You remember."

"I remember."

"Then you remember it was dry all winter."

"Mama."

"I'm not saying anything. I'm saying it was dry."

Ines looked at her uncle. He had finished his bread and was brushing the crumbs from his waistcoat into his palm, and then into the bin, one careful tap at a time. He didn't glance up. He had not said the counts were true. He had not said they were false.

"Say it," she said. "One word. Say it and I'll go home and eat."

"It's late," Teo said.

"Teo," said Bel.

"It's late, that's all I said."

Her mother stood. She did it slowly, using the desk, and when she was up she put her hand on the top ledger, flat, the way you would steady a child's head. "Take them home," she said.

"They're the guild's copies."

"Then the guild will not miss them tonight." She slid the second and third from under Ines's own pages, and the fourth, and stacked them in the crook of her arm like loaves. "Bring them to the house. All seven. Your father's cabinet has a lock, and the key is in the tea tin where it has always been."
