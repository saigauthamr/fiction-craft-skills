# Evals: magic and worldbuilding hub

Each entry is a realistic writer prompt, the skill expected to trigger, and what a good answer must show. Run each prompt with the skills installed and without, and compare.

---

## magic-first-law-cheat-climax - Magic wins the climax and feels like a cheat
**Prompt:** My beta readers say the ending of my fantasy novel feels like a cheat. In the last chapter my heroine, who has only ever done minor weather-reading, suddenly calls down a storm that wipes out the invading fleet. I wanted it to feel triumphant. What went wrong and how do I fix it?
**Should trigger:** `magic-first-law`
**Must show:**
- Ties the cheat feeling to how little the reader understood the magic before it solved the problem
- Asks which emotion the ending should deliver and checks the magic against it
- Gives a choice by magic type: seed the storm power as earlier tools for hard magic, or move the resolution onto character choices if the magic stays soft
**Must not rely on:** Generic advice to add foreshadowing and make the ending more earned without a rule linking reader understanding to what the magic may solve

## magic-second-law-overpowered - Powers too strong, no tension
**Prompt:** My protagonist can teleport anywhere she has seen and carry anyone with her. I keep writing myself out of tension because she can just leave. How do I fix the magic without taking the power away?
**Should trigger:** `magic-second-law`
**Must show:**
- Runs the check of listing what the power does, what it costs, and what stops it
- Separates costs, limitations, flaws, and hindrances and offers at least one concrete option for each
- Suggests tying a constraint to the protagonist's personal weakness or the theme
**Must not rely on:** A generic "add a weakness" or "make the villain stronger" with no distinction between kinds of constraint

## magic-third-law-sprawl - World keeps growing and feels shallow
**Prompt:** I am building a world with three magic systems, five nations, and four religions, and my critique group says it feels like an encyclopedia and they cannot tell what matters. I want to add a fourth magic system for the desert people. Should I?
**Should trigger:** `magic-third-law`
**Must show:**
- Applies the check of asking what existing material could do the job before adding the new system
- Suggests exploring ramifications of existing rules and merging or branching existing religions
- Does not forbid additions outright and notes that length of the work changes how much can be sustained
**Must not rely on:** General advice to cut the setting down or show less exposition

## magic-hard-soft-science-sounding - Is my system hard magic?
**Prompt:** My magic uses "quantum resonance" and I describe the physics in detail, so I think it is hard magic. But early readers say they cannot predict what a mage will manage in any given fight. Who is right?
**Should trigger:** `magic-internal-and-external-logic`
**Must show:**
- Separates internal logic (what the reader can anticipate) from external logic (borrowed science as veneer) and says hardness comes from the former
- Concludes the system currently reads soft despite sounding scientific
- Gives a concrete step such as stating the in-story rules separately and testing whether the physics sentences could be removed
**Must not rely on:** A generic definition of hard magic as having clear rules, with no distinction between scientific vocabulary and predictability

## process-01 - Pile of ideas, no book
**Prompt:** I have a notes file with about forty ideas: a city built inside a dead god's ribcage, a smuggler who can't lie, a heist, a cult that worships weather, a rivalry between two siblings. I want to write a novel but none of it feels like a book yet. Where do I start?
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-idea-intersection`
**Must show:**
- Sorts ideas under setting, character, and plot and looks for the thin column
- Converts or collides ideas across columns (for example the heist plot with the ribcage city and the smuggler)
- Asks what each idea implies, aiming for depth over more ideas
- Notes that a novel needs a couple of strong ideas per column
**Must not rely on:** generic advice to pick a theme, outline, or just start writing

## process-02 - What should I build
**Prompt:** I'm creating a desert world for my fantasy trilogy and my notes are a mess of random things like sandstorms, a salt-trading guild, and two religions. I feel like I'm missing whole areas but don't know what. How should I organize this?
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-three-part-split`
**Must show:**
- Divides notes into physical setting, cultural setting, and magic as separate documents
- Gives prompts per part (ecology, weather, food customs, taboos, law, building materials)
- Follows a chain of consequence from physical to cultural items
- Says to pick the items that affect characters rather than fill every prompt
**Must not rely on:** a generic list of worldbuilding topics with no split or prioritizing

## process-03 - Three years and no chapter one
**Prompt:** I've spent three years on my world. I have a conlang with 4,000 words, a full calendar, and a map of every river. I haven't written a single chapter because every time I finish one thing I find three more I need to figure out. My book is a political murder mystery. Help.
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-worldbuilders-disease`
**Must show:**
- Names the pattern as each element opening further rabbit holes
- Recommends a worldbuilding deadline and then moving to the draft
- Points out the mystery needs a legal and investigative system more than the language
- Says to fill gaps during drafting with work now or a placeholder
**Must not rely on:** reassurance that worldbuilding is valuable, or "just write"

## process-04 - Encyclopedia opening
**Prompt:** My beta readers say chapter one feels like a textbook. I spent years on my magic guilds and history and I put the best parts in the first chapter. I don't want to waste it. How do I know whether I have too much worldbuilding on the page?
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-hollow-iceberg`
**Must show:**
- Applies the two-part test (is it keeping you from writing; is the detail serving stakes, character, and story)
- Advises building in depth only what informs characters and decisions and evoking the rest in a line or two
- Suggests a single vivid detail or relying on familiar tropes without explanation
- Allows that dense openings can be valid if that is the intended story
**Must not rely on:** the generic advice to "show, don't tell" or "cut info dumps" with no test

## page-01 - The abstract pyramid
**Prompt:** In my epic fantasy, chapter three has two priests debating how the soul-magic works for about four pages. My beta readers say they skimmed it and can't picture where it happens. How do I keep the explanation but make it land?
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-pyramid-of-abstraction`
**Must show:**
- Explains that abstract material is earned by anchoring the scene concretely first, with more senses than sight
- Gives a pattern of anchor, climb into explanation, re-anchor periodically, and close concretely
- Has the writer check where the most abstract paragraph sits and what concrete detail surrounds it
**Must not rely on:** generic advice to "show don't tell" or "cut the info dump"

## page-02 - Names for a new culture
**Prompt:** I'm building a mountain culture for my fantasy and I can't come up with names that feel like they belong together. I also don't want to accidentally copy a real word or offend anyone. Where do I start?
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-naming`
**Must show:**
- Offers the three approaches: adapting a real region, borrowing a structural feature of a language, and a simple deliberate pattern
- Lists checks such as searching the name, looking for slurs, and asking someone who knows the source language
- Advises being specific about the source culture rather than treating it as a block
**Must not rely on:** a generic list of invented names or "mash syllables together"

## page-03 - Glazed eyes in chapter one
**Prompt:** My first chapter introduces eleven names, three factions, and the history of the empire. Test readers say it's confusing and they didn't care. Should I simplify or is that just how epic fantasy is?
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-learning-curve-and-exposition`
**Must show:**
- Frames the issue as the learning curve and says a steep curve is a valid choice only when deliberate
- Suggests counting names and terms in the opening and deferring those that can wait
- Recommends delivering the world through a character with a problem whose experience shows the rules
**Must not rely on:** the generic "show don't tell" or "trim exposition" advice with no model of reader demand

---

## page-04 - The thing that outshines the book
**Prompt:** At the three-quarter mark of my thriller I introduce a mysterious ancient machine. Readers say it's the best part, but they lost interest in the heist plot afterward. What went wrong?
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-gorilla-in-the-phone-booth`
**Must show:**
- Identifies the machine as an element more interesting than the thread the reader was following
- Notes that introducing it this late reads as a new problem from nowhere, and offers moving, cutting, or raising the main thread
- Asks whether it serves the promise readers already hold
**Must not rely on:** "foreshadow it more" as the only fix

## page-05 - Describing the same room
**Prompt:** Most of my chapters take place in the same tavern and I keep either skipping description or repeating it. How often should I describe it, and how do I open a chapter there?
**Should trigger:** `worldbuilding-viewpoint-filtered-description`
**Must show:**
- Describes the tavern through the viewpoint character's mood and what they notice, so description is also characterization
- Advises re-setting the scene each chapter with a different noticing, plus brief interaction reminders during dialogue
- Says there is no fixed frequency, and that a single-setting story can work if the setting evolves
**Must not rely on:** a generic "use sensory details" tip
