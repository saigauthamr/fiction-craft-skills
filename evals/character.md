# Evals: character hub

Each entry is a realistic writer prompt, the skill expected to trigger, and what a good answer must show. Run each prompt with the skills installed and without, and compare.

---

## cpr-01 - The lead nobody roots for
**Prompt:** My thriller's protagonist is a disgraced surgeon who is brilliant and always the one pushing the investigation forward. Beta readers say they admire her but don't care if she lives. I don't want to make her soft. What do I do?
**Should trigger:** `character-proactive-relatable-capable`, `character-relatability-toolkit`
**Must show:**
- Places her on the proactive, relatable, and capable scales and identifies relatability as the low one
- Offers specific relatability moves such as an early act of kindness, another character's view of her, self-awareness about her flaw, or showing competence cracking
- Notes that stakes fall when readers are not bothered by her death
- Does not demand she max out all three scales
**Must not rely on:** "give her a tragic backstory" or "make her more vulnerable" with no scale diagnosis

## cpr-02 - Passive hero, active villain
**Prompt:** In my epic fantasy the dark lord has a clear plan and the heroine mostly reacts to his moves until the final act. My critique group keeps saying the villain is the best character. How do I fix the heroine without rewriting the whole book?
**Should trigger:** `character-proactivity-toolkit`, `character-villain-and-antihero-patterns`
**Must show:**
- Names the problem as the villain being proactive while the hero reacts, so readers bond with the villain
- Suggests planting small acts of initiative early and placing the shift to driving the plot at the end of act one or the midpoint
- Suggests giving her own wants and plan so her choices cause the villain's setbacks
- Mentions making the villain slightly more relatable only as a secondary option
**Must not rely on:** "raise the stakes" or "make the hero stronger"

## cpr-03 - The depressed viewpoint
**Prompt:** My main character is clinically depressed after losing her sister, and for the first third of the book she barely does anything. Readers find her dull. I don't want to trivialize depression by having her snap out of it. Options?
**Should trigger:** `character-proactivity-toolkit`
**Must show:**
- Treats internal limits on action as the hardest case and says stasis is what fails
- Suggests giving the state motion, even downward, or giving her control over a small sphere
- Offers the alternatives of leaning on the other two scales or cutting to another viewpoint who carries action
- Does not recommend a sudden recovery
**Must not rely on:** general advice to "add external conflict" or "show her inner thoughts"

## cpr-04 - The sidekick who keeps doing the thing
**Prompt:** My comic sidekick Dex is afraid of his mother, and it is funny in chapter two. Now it is in every chapter and my editor says Dex has become exhausting. How do I keep him likeable?
**Should trigger:** `character-overused-trait-trap`
**Must show:**
- Names the problem as a single relatable trait overplayed until it becomes the character's only attribute
- Gives a check for how often the trait drives scenes and what else he wants
- Suggests competing wants, resting the trait, and quirks that expand his role
- Does not simply say "cut the joke"
**Must not rely on:** "make him more three-dimensional" with no specific technique

## cpr-05 - Which kind of weakness
**Prompt:** My paladin cannot lie because of her oath, and she has a prosthetic leg from a war injury, and she is also very proud. Readers say these are all just flaws. How should I treat each differently?
**Should trigger:** `character-flaws-restrictions-limitations`
**Must show:**
- Sorts them into restriction (the oath), limitation (the leg), and flaw (pride)
- States that flaws get an arc, limitations get workarounds rather than fixes, restrictions get pressure scenes
- Warns that a flaw loses its early relatability if it goes unresolved too long
**Must not rely on:** "weaknesses make characters human"

## cpr-06 - The unbeatable god
**Prompt:** One of my POV characters is a near-omnipotent sun god. Beta readers say there is no tension and they can't connect with him. How do I keep him as a viewpoint?
**Should trigger:** `character-capability-design`, `character-relatability-toolkit`
**Must show:**
- Says an overpowered figure becomes interesting through what he cannot do and suggests a limit tied to the central problem
- Suggests a motivation that crosses divine nature, such as care or being blocked from a goal
- Advises using divine viewpoints sparingly and notes distinctive prose as an alternative
**Must not rely on:** "nerf his powers" with no relatable want

## present-01 - The hero who never changes
**Prompt:** I'm writing a series of mystery novellas about a retired lighthouse keeper who solves crimes on the coast. My critique group says she has no arc and is the same in book three as in book one. I like her that way, but is it a flaw? What do I do to keep readers invested?
**Should trigger:** `character-iconic-characters`
**Must show:**
- Treats an unchanging lead as a valid choice and names it as an iconic character
- Moves the story question from "will she learn" to whether this is the time the culprit is too capable and what solving it costs her
- Suggests a cost or bittersweet element per book and depth placed in other characters
- Allows a small arc inside one book without making it central
**Must not rely on:** telling the writer to just give her a flaw and an arc

## present-02 - Interchangeable cast
**Prompt:** My three heroes in a heist story all sound the same. They all want the vault open, and in dialogue they all say what they're planning and why. How do I make them distinct?
**Should trigger:** `character-presenting-a-character`
**Must show:**
- Separates motivation, personality (how each pursues the goal), and values (what each will give up)
- Warns against characters stating motives to people who already know them
- Gives the tag-free three-person conflicting-wants scene as a check
- Suggests a quirk that reinforces a core trait
**Must not rely on:** generic "give each a unique voice and backstory"

## present-03 - Why does she do that
**Prompt:** My villain, a harbor-master who sabotages ships, feels arbitrary. I can't explain why she'd choose one action over another in scene. How do I figure out who she is?
**Should trigger:** `character-motivation-discovery-questions`
**Must show:**
- Runs a question set covering backstory, what she hides, a failure she'll never repeat, flaws, and want versus obstacle versus need versus sacrifice
- Repeats "why" on each answer
- Uses conflicting answers and explains any deviation from the pattern in text
- Frames a villain's motive as a sensible one pushed past where a hero would stop
**Must not rely on:** a generic character questionnaire of likes and appearance

## present-04 - Writing outside my experience
**Prompt:** My protagonist is a deaf teenage sailor and I'm hearing and have never sailed. I'm afraid of getting it wrong or writing a stereotype. How do I handle it?
**Should trigger:** `character-writing-unfamiliar-backgrounds`
**Must show:**
- Recommends primary sources, including pieces on what media gets wrong
- Advises making her an individual with some common and some personal traits, flagged as personal
- Recommends several readers with the experience, paid where possible, with specific questions
- Notes that risk grows with readership and expects rewrites
**Must not rely on:** "do research and be respectful" with no concrete steps

## arc-01 - Proud swordsman who never seems to need changing
**Prompt:** My protagonist is a gifted young duelist who is arrogant, and the plan is that he learns humility by the end. But when I reread my first act, his arrogance just makes him look cool and nothing bad happens because of it. How do I make the arc actually work?
**Should trigger:** `character-arc-types`
**Must show:**
- Identifies this as a flaw-to-fix arc and says the flaw needs shown costs early (lost opportunities, growth, or relationships)
- Notes the flaw has an upside (confidence) that is currently all the reader sees
- Asks for or proposes concrete scenes of cost before the lesson, plus the decision point where he applies it
**Must not rely on:** generic "give him a failure" or "raise the stakes" advice with no flaw-cost structure

## arc-02 - Character learns the lesson and still fails
**Prompt:** In my tragedy, the queen figures out halfway that her need for control is destroying her family. In the finale she still seizes control and loses everything. Beta readers say it feels like she never learned anything. What am I missing?
**Should trigger:** `character-arc-types`
**Must show:**
- Names this as the downward version of the flaw-to-fix arc and states she must know the flaw and still hold on at the decisive moment
- Suggests making the decisive choice visible, with the reason she gives herself for refusing the lesson
- Warns that constant arrogance or blindness causing failure is a different, weaker shape
**Must not rely on:** "make her more sympathetic" or "add foreshadowing" with no distinction between the upward and downward versions

## arc-03 - Mystery arc and the character's change
**Prompt:** My detective spends the book uncovering who poisoned the council. The solution is solid but the ending feels like a puzzle solved, not a story about her. How can I make the discovery matter to her as a character?
**Should trigger:** `character-arc-types`
**Must show:**
- Frames it as an ignorance-to-knowledge arc where the learning happens about the world and about herself
- Asks what she must become or accept in order to use the information
- Warns against turning the change into a repeat-until-success pattern, and points to the why of her final decision
**Must not rely on:** advice about clues and red herrings only

## arc-04 - Dialogue scene that just passes information
**Prompt:** I have a scene where my warlord tries to get a rival duke to join his campaign. Every draft turns into the two of them trading facts about troop numbers. How do I plan this scene so it has life?
**Should trigger:** `character-floating-outline-for-dialogue`
**Must show:**
- Proposes a short goals list before drafting: one purpose bullet (recruit, rejected because of who the duke is), then what the scene reveals about each character, foreshadowing, and side-character contributions
- Says to freeform the scene afterward, then leave quick notes about small problems at the top for rereading
- Shows motivation coming through friction between the two speakers instead of narration
**Must not rely on:** generic "give each character a goal and conflict" without the list-then-improvise process
