# Chapter 1: The short count

The lamp oil had come in on the morning ferry, and by the time Ines reached the warehouse somebody had stacked it against the seaward wall, where the damp got into the staves. A dockhand's boy was sitting on one of the barrels, eating an onion like an apple.

"Off," she said.

He got off. She took the chalk from her coat and started at the door end, putting a short stroke on each lid. The guild sent its surveyors to do the quarter tallies because surveyors were the only people in the harbor paid to be pedantic. A guildmaster had once told Ines, kindly, that she was the most pedantic of them, and she had kept the remark the way other people kept a letter.

Eleven.

She went to the wall end and counted back the other way, touching each barrel this time instead of marking it, because a chalk stroke could be doubled by a careless hand and a palm on wet oak could not. Eleven again. She stood in the gap where the twelfth should have been and looked at the floorboards, which were dry. Nothing had stood there today.

Tomas came in with his pilot's jacket open and a coil of line over his shoulder. He dropped the coil on barrel four.

"You've got your face on," he said.

"There are eleven."

"There are twelve. I loaded twelve. I watched Benno roll the last one up the plank and nearly put it through the rail."

"Then Benno can come and count them."

"Benno can't count past ten without taking his boots off." He stood beside her and considered the gap. "It's on the dock at Shellmouth, behind the net sheds. It'll come over on Thursday's boat."

"Then I'll write it on Thursday."

"Ines." He rubbed the back of his neck. "The guild pays on the tally. Eleven is a short count, and a short count is a dispute, and a dispute is three weeks of clerks. Nobody gets paid. Not me, not the boat, not Benno, who is an idiot but has a mother."

She knew how disputes went. They went up to the guild office on good paper in autumn and came down in spring. If she wrote twelve and the barrel turned up Thursday, nobody would ever know the difference. If it had gone off a dock in the dark instead, her name would sit under a number that was false. She thought the net sheds were likelier. Tomas was careless about his boots and his debts and the hour, but never about cargo, and it was because she knew that that she found herself watching his face a moment longer than she meant to.

"There's a column for goods in transit," he said.

"For goods in transit under seal. Is it under seal?"

"It's under a net."

She opened the ledger on barrel seven, which had the flattest lid, and uncapped the ink. Tomas leaned his elbows on the next barrel along and watched her the way he watched weather coming over the bar, without much hope of changing it.

"You're going to write eleven."

"I counted eleven."

"Twice, I'd guess."

She dipped the pen.

"The guild doesn't want twice," he said. "The guild wants it to add up."

She wrote *11*. In the margin, small, she added *one barrel reported left at Shellmouth by pilot T. Reyes*, because that was also true and it was the only favor the page had room for. She blotted it. Tomas read it upside down.

"That's me in the margin."

"You said it was at Shellmouth."

"I said it was probably at Shellmouth." He took his line back off barrel four. "Next time I'll load thirteen, and you can be short the other way."

He went out whistling, and took the boy with him, so she restacked the barrels off the wet wall herself.

---

At low water the side channel behind the salt pans was the width of a lane, and nobody went there but gulls and Ines.

She had drawn it nineteen times since midsummer. It had a different bend on nearly every tide, sometimes hugging the old pan walls, sometimes swinging out toward the breakwater as if it had remembered somewhere else to be. The guild had no name for it. On her own sheets it was the Cut. When she had put it on the quarterly list, the reply had come back in a clerk's hand: *too small to mark; not approved.* That was fair. It was too small to matter to anyone with a ferry.

The marker was in her coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth. It was salt-iron, the size of a fist and heavier than a fist had any business being, with the guild's stamp and a number on the flat face. It had been issued to her for the North Reach survey, and the North Reach survey had been put off until spring, and she was meant to hand the marker back at the end of the quarter, which was Friday.

She unwrapped it anyway, to look. That was all she had meant to do.

The tide was at the slack. The wrack lines lay still on the water, and the Cut ran out past her stakes in a long lazy curve she had not drawn before, wider at the seaward end than she liked. Nineteen sheets, and every one of them was true for about six hours. She could chart a reef that had sat in one place since before the harbor had a name. What she could not do was tell anyone where the Cut went. She could only tell them where it had been.

A marker fixed a channel where it lay. Everyone knew that much, the way everyone knew the tide came in. What nobody had written down, in any book the guild let her read, was what the water did in the moment it happened.

She crouched on the last stone of the pan wall, reached down until her sleeve went wet to the elbow, and set the marker on the bottom at the head of the Cut.

For a while there was nothing to see. Then the flood began, and the wrack lines, which on every other turn of the tide slid sideways and folded over each other, came in straight. They ran along the curve and stayed on it. The eelgrass at the edges lay down all one way. She took her sounding pole to the first stake and found the deep water exactly where it had been at slack, and at the second stake the same, and she stood there with the pole in her hands while the flood rose past her ankles, checking a third stake she already knew the answer to.

It had fixed the curve she liked least. Wide at the mouth and wasteful, swinging toward the breakwater for no reason any pilot would thank it for. It would swing there now on every tide until the salt pans fell into the sea.

She reached for the marker before she thought about it. It had sunk into the silt to its stamp and would not move for her hand, and when she let go the water kept its line.

Nobody was on the breakwater. The lamp-keeper's window at the end of the guild quay was dark. She wrapped the wet oilcloth around nothing, put it back in her pocket, and walked home along the pan walls with her sleeve dripping.

At her table she took out a twentieth sheet and drew the Cut as it lay, every sounding in its place. When she came to the corner where she wrote the date and the hour of the tide, she held the pen over it for some time, and then she left it blank.
