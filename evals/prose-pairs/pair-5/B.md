# Chapter 1: The short count

The guild paid by the barrel, which was why the guild liked its barrels counted by somebody else.

Ines counted them from the door end of the warehouse, two fingers on each lid as she passed it. Pitch, stencilled with the guild's anchor, stacked two high against the wet wall. Eleven. She went back to the door and counted them again from the other side, because a number you got twice the same way was only one number.

Eleven.

"Twelve," Tomas said from the doorway.

He had a coil of wet line over one shoulder and salt dried white in the creases of his sleeves. He had been up since the turn of the tide. Tomas had been late to everything in his life except the tide.

"Eleven," she said.

"There's one behind the nets."

She looked behind the nets. There were more nets.

"Then it's still on the boat."

"Is it?"

He dropped the line on a crate and did not go back to the boat. He came and stood beside her instead and looked at the barrels as if they might be persuaded. Outside, a gull was working at something on the slip and losing.

"The yard loaded twelve," he said. "I watched them load twelve."

"Then the yard should ask you where the twelfth went."

"The yard will ask the guild, and the guild will ask me, and I'll say Ines Varga counted it, and everybody will nod, because you're the one they send." He took the ledger out of her hands, quite gently, and turned it so he could read her column. "They don't pay for a short count. You know that. Not a short barrel. The whole run."

"I know what they pay for."

"The crew doesn't get paid. The yard doesn't get paid. I get a letter." He gave her the ledger back. "You could write twelve and let Oru come down here and look behind the nets himself."

"He'd find nets."

"He'd find nothing, because he wouldn't come." Tomas sat on the crate, on his own line. "Nobody comes. That's the whole reason they send you."

She took the pencil from behind her ear. The barrel she was missing was not in the warehouse and not, she was fairly sure, on his boat; it was somewhere between the yard and here, in a channel that had run one way at dawn and another by noon, and she suspected Tomas knew exactly which channel and was not going to say. That was his business. The count was hers.

"Ines."

She wrote the number down and blotted it and did not look at him.

He sat there a while longer. Then he stood and picked up his line and shook the worst of the water off it onto her boots.

"That's my whole run," he said.

"It's eleven-twelfths of your whole run."

"That's not how they pay."

"No," she said. "I know."

At the door he stopped, as if he had something better, and then didn't have it. "You'll be the only honest woman in the poorhouse."

"I'll count the beds."

He went out laughing, which was worse than if he hadn't. She stood with the ledger against her chest until she heard his boots reach the end of the slip, and then she counted the barrels a third time, for no reason she would have admitted to, and got eleven.

---

The side channel ran behind the net sheds, a cut of brown water too shallow for anything bigger than a skiff and too unimportant to have a number. The guild had never ranked it. Nobody would sign a marker for a channel nobody used.

Ines had been chalking its edge on the quay steps for twenty-two days.

The marks went down the third step in a crooked ladder, one for each low tide, each with the date beside it in her small square hand. A hand's width east. Two hands west. Back again, almost to the first line, and then a lurch the day before yesterday that put the edge of the channel under the bottom of the stair. Tides did that. Everyone knew tides did that. The guild's charts allowed for it with a hatched margin and a note that said *variable*, and Ines had drawn a great many of those margins herself and had never once liked them.

The marker was in her coat. She had signed it out nine days ago for the reef survey, which had been put off, and then put off again, and it was due back in the case tomorrow morning. Salt-iron, a forearm long, the guild number stamped into the collar. It was heavier than it looked. They all were.

The quay was empty. The lamps at the end of the long pier were lit, and the ferry sheds were shut, and the only sound was the water talking to itself under the boards.

She went down the steps until the water was over her ankles, and then over her shins, cold enough to ache. The channel mouth was a darker stripe in the brown where the bottom fell away. She found it with her foot. She set the point of the marker in the silt and held it there, and stood like that long enough to hear her own breathing.

Then she took the mallet from her other pocket and drove it in.

Three strokes. On the third the iron stopped going down, and the water around her legs stopped too. She felt it more than saw it. The small slack pull that had been at her shins all along, the drift that was always there in any channel, simply went away, as if someone had set a hand on the surface and held it.

She waited for it to come back. It did not.

She climbed up two steps and watched the tide come in. It came the way it always came, a slow brown rise over the bottom stair and then the next, and the dark stripe of the channel came with it, and when the water reached the third step the edge of the channel lay along the line she had chalked that afternoon. Exactly along it. It did not ease a finger's width east or west. She stayed until full tide, and it did not move then either.

At the slack she went back down into the water, up to her thighs now, and found the collar of the marker by feel, and pulled.

It did not give. She braced her feet and pulled again, both hands, until the mallet slid out of her pocket and she had to snatch for it. The marker stayed where it was, as though the bottom had closed over it and kept it.

She climbed out. Her skirt was black to the waist and her hands smelled of iron. On the third step, twenty-two chalk lines went crooked down the stone, and one of them was right.

In the morning the marker case would be opened in the office, and the clerk would run his finger along the slots, forty, forty-one, forty-three, and stop.

She rubbed the other twenty-one lines out with the heel of her hand and went home by the back lanes.
