# Chapter 1: Tuesday

I let myself into my father's house at ten to seven in the morning, still in my tunic under my coat. Behind me, on the ward, a woman called Edith Marr had been dead since ten past four. I had washed her, changed the sheet under her, and put her teeth back in, because her daughter would want to see her with her teeth, and I had written it all up before handover. Eleven years of nights. I am quick about it and I am kind about it, and the drive home is where I leave it.

The plan for the house was written on the back of my hand in biro: *Biscuit. Fridge. Bins.* Then home, and sleep until two. I had done the dog every morning since the funeral. The fridge I had put off for four days, which is three days longer than I would let a family put off anything on my ward.

Biscuit was asleep in Dad's chair. He had been deaf for years, so there was no point calling him; you stamp on the floorboards and wait for it to reach him through the legs of the furniture. I stamped. He lifted his head, saw it was me, and put it down on the cushion again. Nobody had told him the chair was off limits now, and I was not going to be the one. I know what a chair is like after a death in it. I looked at the dog and not at the chair, and went through to the kitchen.

I did the freezer first because the freezer would be easy. Freezer food keeps. You can give it to a neighbour with a straight face. I had been cooking for him three times a week for six years, in the square two-portion tubs I bought in packs of fifty, and every Monday, Wednesday and Friday I had put the new ones in the top drawer and found the top drawer empty. I took that as the review. It was the nearest he came to one.

The top drawer was empty now. The second drawer was full. So was the third, and the bottom one, packed so tight the frost had glued the lids together. Every tub was mine, with a strip of masking tape across the lid and my writing on the tape. *Lamb and rice, 12/2.* *Chicken, no salt (I know).* *Lentil soup, heat it PROPERLY.* I took them out and stacked them on the worktop, because once you have started counting you may as well have the number. Sixty-three. The oldest went back to the November before last. He had been moving them down a drawer at a time, every time, for a year and a half, and I had never once opened the bottom of my own father's freezer.

The fridge had nothing of mine in it. It had foil trays with cardboard lids, and on each lid, in marker, the name of the dish in English and in Arabic, and a round sticker: *Beit Sitti, Ferrow.* Fattoush. Kibbeh. A whole tray of something with pine nuts that had been very good a fortnight ago and was a biology project now. The bin had more of them. He had rinsed them before he threw them away, and flattened the lids, and stacked them, because he was who he was.

I know who runs the kitchen at Beit Sitti. Everyone at the funeral knew, which was why nobody had said it when he stood at the back.

Ferrow is forty minutes away and nobody delivers forty minutes. I decided a neighbour had been driving him. I decided this quite firmly, standing there with a tray of kibbeh in each hand. There were no receipts for any of it, either. My father kept the receipt for a pint of milk. I put that away somewhere and did not take it out again.

On the fridge door, under the magnet from the garage, was my shift rota for the month and the whiteboard where we left each other notes. The last note was mine. *N, Thurs: bring bin bags.* I had not brought bin bags. I had been at a funeral on Thursday, which seemed a reasonable excuse until I was standing in front of a full bin with sixty-three tubs of my own food defrosting at my elbow.

He kept everything else in the drawer by the cooker, so I tried there. Rubber bands. Takeaway menus, though not, I noticed, that one. The guarantee for a kettle he'd thrown out in 1998. Batteries, sorted by size, in sandwich bags labelled by size. And at the back, under a folded tea towel, a phone.

It was the kind supermarkets sell by the till for ten pounds, black, with buttons. Dad's real phone was in a box at my flat, with his glasses and his watch, in a bag the undertaker had given me. I knew that phone. I had set it up, and put my number in it under *Noor*, because he refused to have *Daughter*. This one I had never seen.

I put it back under the tea towel and shut the drawer. Patients leave things. Letters in bedside lockers, a name written inside a book, a photograph turned face down. I don't read them. It is a rule, and it is one of the few I have never broken, and I stood in that kitchen with my hand flat on the drawer front for about as long as it takes to pour a kettle.

Then I opened the drawer and took the phone out.

It was on. Two bars of three, a week without a charger, which is what you get for ten pounds and no internet. The contacts list had one name in it, and the name was a letter. *S.*

The call log went back as far as the phone would hold. Tuesday, 20:02, forty minutes. Tuesday, 20:01, an hour and six. Tuesday, 19:58, twenty-two. Tuesday, Tuesday, Tuesday, some from him and some to him, all of them a few minutes either side of eight. Under where the phone had been was a fold of paper, held with a rubber band. Top-up receipts from the Spar at the end of Alder Road, ten pounds, one a month. Of course he had kept them. I counted them, because I count things. Thirty-seven. The first was dated March, three years ago.

Eight o'clock on a Tuesday I was on the ward, taking handover. He knew that. It was on the fridge.

The last call in the log was Tuesday of last week, 20:03, thirty-one minutes. I had rung him at half past seven that night from the hospital car park, to say I'd be over on Thursday. He had said fine. He had said, Don't bring lamb again. Then I had gone in and taken handover, and at some point after half past eight he had put this phone back in the drawer under the tea towel, and gone and sat down in his chair.

I was still standing there working out the minutes when the phone rang in my hand.

It had a thin, cheerful tune, like an ice-cream van. In the front room Biscuit slept through it. The screen said *S*. It was ten past seven in the morning, and it was Tuesday.
