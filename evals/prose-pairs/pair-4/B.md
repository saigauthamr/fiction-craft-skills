# Chapter 1: Tuesday

The man in bed nine asked me the time at four, and again at half past, and at a quarter to five he stopped needing to know. I sat with his daughter until the undertakers came, wrote him up, handed over at six, and drove to Alder Road with the radio off. I was good at nights like that. Everyone on the ward said so, and I had eleven years of cards in a shoebox to prove it.

I had been to the house every morning since the funeral, but only as far as the back porch. Biscuit's food lived out there in a bin with a brick on the lid, and so did Biscuit, mostly, because the porch had the radiator and he had the hips. He was asleep on the mat when I came in. He was deaf enough that I had to tap him on the shoulder, and when he woke up he looked at me with great disappointment and then at the bowl.

"I know," I said. He couldn't hear it. I said it anyway. I say a lot of things to people who can't hear them.

The plan was to feed him, empty the fridge, and be back in my car before the neighbours opened their curtains. A week is a long time for a fridge. I had smelled worse things professionally than whatever was in there, and I had stood in the kitchen doorway on Saturday and Sunday and Monday and not gone in. I went in.

The kitchen was as he'd left it, which was tidy. His reading glasses were folded on the windowsill. His mug was upside down on the drainer. The calendar from the garage on Mill Lane still showed last month, because changing it had been my job.

The fridge was full of foil boxes. They had cardboard lids, with a red stamp on each one that said BEIT SITTI and, underneath, in smaller letters, FERROW. Someone had written on the lids in marker: *no onion*, *extra lemon*, *Y*. Four of them, stacked, from the same night, if the fur was anything to go by. My father had told me for six years that restaurant food gave him heartburn. He said it every time I suggested ordering in, and he said it in the voice he used for county planning officers.

I put the boxes in a carrier bag and tied the handles twice. Then I went through to the utility room, because there was a chest freezer out there that was his, the way the garage and the shed and the filing cabinet were his, and I thought I might as well know what I was dealing with.

I knew what I was dealing with. I had made it.

Every week for six years I had cooked on my days off and driven his dinners over in a cool bag: lentil soup, chicken and rice, the bean stew he called sticky, portioned into tubs and labelled in my capitals with the date and the contents. I had put them in his fridge and taken away the empties. He had been giving me back the empties. The full ones were here, stacked to the lid in rows, the oldest at the bottom under a skin of frost. The top layer was from June. I dug down with my hand until my fingers hurt and found February. I stopped digging.

I am a person who counts. I count tablets and I count breaths per minute and I count the hours I sleep in a week, which is never enough to bother writing down. I counted the tubs on the top layer and estimated the depth and did the sum standing there with the lid open and the cold coming up into my face. It came to somewhere around a hundred and forty dinners. I did it again and got the same answer, which was annoying, because I had wanted to be wrong.

The kitchen bin was full too. More foil, more red stamps. The recycling box by the back door had a stack of flattened lids in it, tied with string, because he tied everything with string. *Y*, *Y*, *Y*.

Ferrow is forty minutes away. Nobody delivers forty minutes for a tub of fattoush.

I needed bin bags. He kept them in the drawer by the cooker, the one with the batteries and the takeaway menus he claimed he never used, and when I pulled it open it stuck, because there was a cable running into it from the socket behind the bread bin. On the end of the cable, on top of the rubber bands, was a phone.

It was the kind of phone they sell at the petrol station, grey, with buttons. The screen lit when I touched the drawer, and it said the battery was full.

I did not want to pick it up. I have been in a lot of rooms after a death where the family finds something, and I have watched them decide whether to look, and the ones who look are never glad and the ones who don't are never sure. I have said, to more than one of them, that they don't have to do anything today.

I picked it up.

There was no lock on it. He never locked anything except the shed. The contacts list had one name in it, which was a letter, which was *S*. The call log was a column of the same letter going down the screen, all of them answered or made, none of them missed. I pressed the button and the column kept going. Tuesday, 18:00. Tuesday, 18:01. Tuesday, 17:58. Every Tuesday. I scrolled until the dates went back three years and the phone ran out of memory before it ran out of Tuesdays.

Six o'clock on a Tuesday was when my shift started. For three years I had rung him from the car park at ten to, to tell him I was going in, and he had always got me off the line quickly. I had thought it was because he didn't like the phone.

The last call was a week ago. 18:00. Forty-one minutes. The neighbour found him at eleven, in his chair, with the television on.

Biscuit came in from the porch and stood against my leg, which he doesn't do, and I put my hand on his head. I stood there in my uniform with the drawer open and the bag of foil boxes at my feet and did not do anything at all, which was a new experience for me.

Then the phone rang in my hand. It was not a ringtone I knew. It was a cheap, cheerful little tune, the kind that comes on the phone and nobody bothers to change, and the screen said *S*.

The clock on the cooker said 06:52. It was Tuesday.
