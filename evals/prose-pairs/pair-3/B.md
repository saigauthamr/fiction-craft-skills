# Chapter 4: The second way out

The water started where the rails stopped. Somebody had taken the rails up years back, for scrap or spite, and the sleepers went on alone into the dark and then went under.

Ada held her lamp low over it. Black and flat, with a skin of coal dust that broke around her boot. Further on the roof came down to meet it, so the roadway narrowed to a slot a man could pass through bent double. She had walked this road dry in her first year, when the Drowned End was only the East District and smelled of nothing worse than horses.

"How deep?" said Harry Fenn.

"Deep enough to wash you." She stepped in. The cold went up through her trousers to the knee, then the thigh. At the bottom of the dip she would be wet to the ribs, with a hand's width between her hat and the roof. Forty yards, maybe. She had never measured it then either.

She turned and put the lamp on them. Fenn. The Bevans, Owen and Mal, who shared one face between them and argued over it. Old Tom Lusk with his good ear turned toward her. Davy Price, who had not said a word since the rails ended, the longest he had kept his mouth shut in the eleven years she had known him. Col with his lamp held up too high.

Seven. She counted them anyway.

"Belts," she said. "Each man holds the belt of the one in front. Nobody lets go for anything. You drop your lamp, let it go. You drop your mother, let her go."

"Mine's dead," said Mal Bevan.

"Then you're set."

She put Davy behind her, where she could reach him, and Col behind Davy, and Lusk behind the boy to stop him hurrying. The rest after. Then she walked in.

The first ten yards were only cold. In the next ten the water came to her belt, and she felt Davy's fist close on the back of it and pull, then pull harder, until he was hanging off her more than following. The roof dropped. She bent her neck. Her lamp threw its light flat across the water and the water threw it back up, so there were two roofs, a dry one and a wet one, and she kept her eyes on the one that could fall.

Halfway, Davy stopped.

He did it in silence, and that was the wrong thing. Davy talked through falls. He had talked through his own wedding, and once for twenty minutes with a prop across his legs, about a dog. Now his hand came off her belt and she heard him breathing through his teeth, and when she turned he was standing chest-deep with his lamp held out in front of him, staring at the water as if it had said his name.

"Davy."

"There's a door," he said. "There was a door here."

There had been. A ventilation door at the bottom of the dip, and a trapper boy on a stool beside it to open it. Ada could see the stool. She could not remember the boy's name. In '09 the water had come down this road faster than a man could run, and Davy Price, eight years old and new on the job, had been somewhere along it.

"Rotted off years since," she said. "Come on."

He did not come. Behind him the line had bunched. Col's lamp swung, and the boy's face hung white above it. Further back Lusk was asking what, what's the hold.

Then Davy's lamp went into the water. She did not see him drop it. The light was in his hand, and then it was a yellow smear going brown under the surface, and then it was out, and Davy made a noise she had never heard a grown man make and turned round to go back the way they had come.

Ada went for him. She got a fist in his collar and he took her off her feet. The water closed over her hat and came in at her ears, cold enough to stop every thought she had, and she came up spitting with her lamp still lit in one hand and the other still in his collar. He had six stone on her. She did not try to win. She hooked her leg behind his knee and let him go under once, short, and when he came up choking she had her mouth at his ear.

"The boy's behind you," she said. "You turn round, you walk him into the deep end. Is that what you want?"

Davy coughed water. She felt him look for Col and find the lamp.

"Hold my belt and shut your eyes. I don't care what you see. Shut them."

He held it. She walked. Davy came after her blind, breathing like a split bellows, twisting her belt into her spine, and the water fell to her ribs, her waist, her knees. Then there were rails under her boots again, and she hauled him up onto dry stone, and he lay on his face with his arms out.

"Number," she called back into the dark. Her wick was guttering; she could not see past the edge of the water. She put a hand flat on Davy's back. "Two," she said for him.

"Three, four," Lusk called, fast, the way he always called. "Five," said Fenn. Owen, six. Mal, seven, and something about his boots.

Seven. Davy was getting up on one knee, and she wanted the crew moving before he had time to look back at where he had been.

"Up the road, then."

The road climbed after the dip, dry and close, the timber gone grey and soft enough to push a thumb into. The air was no better and no worse. She kept the pace hard and nobody complained, and Davy said nothing at all, and she found she would have paid to hear him lie about the dog. They came to the foot of the old upcast shaft in the time it takes to smoke two pipes, which nobody was doing.

It was a round chamber cut in the rock, with a ladderway bolted to the wall going up into black. Air came down it, a thin cold thread of it. She put her face up and it smelled of rain.

"Line up," she said. "Against the wall where I can see you."

They lined up. She went along them with the lamp, face by face, close enough to see the water drying white in their stubble.

Fenn. Owen. Mal. Lusk. Davy, sat down with his back to the rock and his hands open on his knees.

She went along them again. Five, and her. She held the lamp out toward the road they had come by, in case the boy was only slow, in case he was about to come up it with his lamp too high.

"Where's Col?"

Lusk turned his good ear to her. "He went on ahead. When Davy went under. He let go of me and went up front to you."

"He had my belt," Davy said. "He had it the whole way." He looked down at his belt, then at his empty hand. "He had it."

"Who called three, Tom?"

"I called it for him." Lusk's voice went up. "He was in front of me. I heard him."

Nobody else said anything. Mal looked at Owen, and Owen looked at the floor.

Ada stood in the thread of air and smelled the rain on it. Up there was the pit bank, and the row, and a kitchen she had sat in with a cup going cold in front of her, telling Mary Maddox it was the safest district in the pit. She had said it twice, because Mary had asked twice.

She set her lamp on the floor and began to unbuckle her belt.

Davy got up off the rock.
