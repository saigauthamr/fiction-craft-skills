# Chapter 4: The second way out

The dip started where the rails stopped.

Somebody had pulled the rails out after '09 and left the sleepers to rot. Ada stood on the last sound one and held her lamp out over the water. It lay black and flat, and the light sat on it like grease. Twenty yards on, the roof came down to meet it and went under.

"It comes up again," she said.

"How do you know?" said Davy.

"Because I've got the old plan in my head and you've got nothing in yours."

Three lamps left between seven of them. The afterdamp had taken the rest, one flame at a time, back in the Four Foot. She kept hers. Pinner had the second. Col had the third, because he was the lightest and the steadiest and would be at the back.

The rope was on the other side of the fall, with everything else they needed.

"Hand on the belt in front," she said. "Nobody lets go. Col, keep that lamp up where I can see it."

"Yes, Auntie."

"Not down here."

"Yes, Mrs Rourke."

She counted them off the wall. Hob Teale behind her. Walt Lusk, Sam Lusk. Pinner. Davy. Col. Herself. Seven. She went in.

The cold took her at the shins and kept climbing. Thigh. Belt. Under the water the floor was soft with thirty years of muck, and every step sucked at her boots and let go late. The roof was old timber, black and furred with white rot, and it creaked when the water moved against the props. She listened to it the way she listened to everything. It was only complaining.

Halfway, the water reached her ribs and the roof came down to a hand's width above her hat. She tipped her head back and breathed off the top of the air.

Behind her, the line stopped.

"Ada." Davy's voice, too loud, booming back off the water. "Ada, it's the same. It's the same place. This is where it came down. The water came down the road like a wall, like a bloody train, I was ten, I was holding Dad's coat—"

"Davy."

"—and he said run and I ran and there were eleven of them behind me and I never—"

The line jerked. Pinner swore. His lamp went sideways, touched the water, and went out with a small hiss, and then there were two lights in the dip.

"Hob," Ada said. "Hold the wall. Nobody moves."

She went back along the line with one hand flat on the side, past Hob, past the Lusks, past Pinner's dark shape blowing water out of his nose. Davy had stopped dead. He was a head taller than any of them and the roof was pressing his hat down on his skull, and he had both hands on a prop as if it were going somewhere.

She put her lamp close to his face.

"What's my name."

"Ada, I can't, I'm not—"

"What's my name."

"Ada."

"What's yours."

"Davy." He was shaking hard enough to make little rings in the water.

"And what are you."

He looked at her.

"You're the biggest bloody hewer in the Four Foot," she said, "and you're going to drown in four foot of water. They'll put it on your stone. People will come and read it."

Something came out of him that was half a laugh and half a sob.

"Let go of the prop."

He didn't.

"Davy, it's holding up the roof, not you. Let go of it."

He let go. She got her fist in the back of his belt.

"Pinner, take Davy's belt. Col."

"Here, Mrs Rourke." The third lamp, low and steady behind them.

"Keep up."

She did not go back to the front. She went beside Davy, between him and the wall, and walked him. He was dead weight one step and dragging the next. Twice his feet went out from under him in the muck and she held his face up out of the water by the collar, her arm burning, her own mouth full of the taste of rust. She did not look anywhere but at him. At the lowest place she had to tip his head back with her hand on his forehead, like a child at a pump.

Then the floor started to climb.

Belt. Thigh. Knee. The roof lifted off them. Davy went down on his hands at the edge of the water and coughed up what he'd swallowed, and kept coughing after there was nothing left.

"Up," Ada said. "You can do that at the top."

The road beyond was dry and the timber was better. Two hundred yards on it opened into the bottom of the old shaft. Air came down it. She felt it on her wet face before she saw anything, cold and clean, and her lamp flame leaned toward the road behind them and stood up taller than it had in hours. Somewhere far above, there was a grey smudge that might have been the sky or might have been her eyes.

The ladders went up into the dark. Some of them would hold.

"Right," she said. "Count off."

Hob. Walt. Sam. Pinner, with his dead lamp hanging off his finger. Davy, sitting against the wall with his knees up.

Herself.

Six.

She counted again. Hob, Walt, Sam, Pinner, Davy. Herself. There were two lamps, hers and the one in Hob's hand. She had not seen Hob take it.

"Where'd you get that," she said.

"Col's," Hob said. "He passed it up the line when Pinner's went. Said you'd want light at the front."

Nobody said anything. The water dripped off them onto the stone.

Ada walked back to the mouth of the road and held her lamp out. The road ran away toward the dip, dry for a while, then shining, then gone. No light on it. She listened. The roof creaked, a long way off, the way old timber does when water moves against it.

"Col," she said, not loud.

She turned around. Five faces in the lamplight, wet and grey, all of them looking at her. Then, one by one, all of them except Davy looked at Davy.

Hob held out the second lamp. He didn't hold it out to anyone.
