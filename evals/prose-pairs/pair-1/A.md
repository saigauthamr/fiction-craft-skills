# Chapter 4: The second way out

The water in the dip stood to a man's thigh and showed nothing of what was under it. Ada put her lamp to the roof, where the timbers had gone green and soft, and counted the gap between roof and water. Three feet. Four at the crown.

"Walk it single file," she said. "Hand on the wall. If you go in the rail cut, you stay in the rail cut, and I'll fish you out when I'm done with everyone who can swim."

"Who can swim?" said Tam Hollis.

"Nobody. That's why it's a short walk."

She put Reg Ashby behind her, because Reg did what he was told, then Tam. She put Davy Price fourth, where he had a man ahead and a man behind and no room to turn round, and Joss and old Sam after him. Col went last with the spare lamp. He was sixteen, and she wanted him where she could shout back and be heard, and where nobody would notice her doing it.

She went in. The cold took her to the knees and then to the hip and stayed there. The wall under her palm was slick and furred, and every few yards it gave a little, as old coal does when it has been wet since 1909. She did not say so.

"That's not bad," Reg said behind her. "That's not bad at all."

"It's a puddle, Reg."

They were past the crown when Davy stopped. She heard it before she turned: the water quit moving round his legs. Then he began to talk.

"It was up to here, you know. On the lamp hooks. We had to hang on the hooks. My da, he said keep your face up, keep your face up, and I never saw him after, they never got him, did you know that, Ada, nobody ever got -"

"Davy."

"- and there was a door, there was a door just here, I swear to God there was a door."

There was no door. There was a wall of coal and a rail cut and the black. She came back along the line with the water dragging at her coat, past Reg and past Tam, and got her lamp up between his face and the roof, because a man will look at the light if you give him nothing else.

His eyes went everywhere but her.

"You're the biggest man on this shift," she said. "If you sit down in this, I can't lift you. Nobody can. So you won't sit down."

"I can't, Ada."

"You've done sixty yards. That's the hard part."

It was not. The hard part was forty yards ahead and she knew it. He took the lie and hung on to it, which was all she wanted from him.

"Take my belt."

"I'll pull you under."

"You'll pull me under and I'll come up and hit you. Take the belt."

He took it. His fist closed on the leather and she felt every ounce of him. She went forward at the pace of a funeral, talking the whole way about nothing: the canteen tea, which was stewed, the new deputy, who was a fool, Reg's wife's sister, who was no better than she should be. Reg said that was unkind to the sister. Tam said it was accurate. Davy stopped shouting somewhere around the second of these and breathed instead, hard and loud, like a man pushing a tub up a grade.

The floor rose. The water fell to her knees, her shins, her boots. She stood on dry rail and kept the lamp high while they came up out of it. Reg. Tam. Davy, still gripping her belt, so that she had to say his name twice before he let go. He sat down on the sleepers and was sick between his boots, and she stood over him until he had finished, and the others came up past her in the dark without her looking.

"Good," she said. "That's what the floor's for."

The old shaft was another hundred yards on, a round brick throat with a cage frame rusted solid across it and a ladderway bolted to the side. Cold air came down it, thin and sweet. Somebody laughed. Somebody said a prayer, and then said a worse word, which was probably also a prayer.

Ada set her back to the brick and counted.

Reg. Tam. Davy. Joss Pell, wringing his cap. Old Sam Tarbet, who had not said a word since the fall and sat with his lamp in his lap, holding it in both hands.

Five.

She counted again and put herself on the end. Six.

"Where's the lad," she said.

Nobody answered. Reg looked down the road they had come by. Tam looked at Reg. Davy, grey and wet to the ribs, was still wiping his mouth, and he said, "He had the spare lamp. He was right behind me. I felt him behind me."

"When?"

"In the water. I -" He stopped. "I don't know. I had your belt, Ada. I wasn't - I was looking at your back."

Ada looked at the dip. There was no light in it. From here it was only a place where the dark was a little blacker and a little wetter, and the roof talked to itself above it, a small dry ticking she had heard all her life and could not now put words to.

She had told Col's mother it was the safest district in the pit. She had said it in the kitchen, with a cup of tea going cold between them.

"Right," she said. She pulled her belt tight, where Davy's fist had dragged it round. "Reg, you're in charge of the ladder. Nobody climbs until I'm back. Nobody."

"You can't go back in there," Tam said.

"Then which of you is it?"

Nobody said anything to that either. She held out her hand. Joss, after a moment, gave her his lamp, and she hung it on the hook beside her own.
