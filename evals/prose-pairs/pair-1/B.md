# Chapter 4: The second way out

The roadway dropped without warning, and the water came up over Ada's boots, then her shins, and stopped at her knees. It was colder than it had any right to be.

"Lamps up," she said. "Hand on the man in front. Anyone who lets go answers to me."

"Answers how?" said Hale.

"Slowly."

Nobody laughed, but Hale's breathing eased. She'd said it for that.

The dip ran forty yards by her reckoning. Nobody had measured it since '09, and the men who might have were dead or in Canada. The roof here was low and sweating. Where the timber had gone, it had been replaced by black stumps furred white, and the water around them was the colour of tea left overnight. Her lamp made a small yellow room and carried it along with her.

She counted. Seven, herself included. Davy at her shoulder, because she'd put him there. Hale, Orrin, old Wilf Pask, Reuben behind them. Col at the tail, where the smallest went, with his lamp held higher than he needed.

"Fine," said Reuben, to nobody. "Fine. Fine water. Lovely."

"Save your breath," Ada said.

Something rose against her leg, soft, and she did not look down. A sack, she decided. A sack, or a cap. She went on.

At the halfway mark Davy stopped.

She felt it through the rope of hands before she heard it. The line went tight and then slack, and the slack was worse. His lamp had dropped to the surface and was floating on its side, throwing light across the ceiling in long, wrong strokes.

"Davy."

He was looking at the water. His mouth was open. He had a hewer's chest, a barrel on two posts, and it was going in and out like a bellows with a hole in it.

"I'll not," he said. "Ada. I'll not. I was eleven."

"I know how old you were."

"My brother was—"

"Take my hand."

"There's no floor," said Davy. "There's no floor, it's going to go."

"There's a floor. I'm standing on it." She stamped, and the water slapped up and wet her chin. "That's a floor. Feel it."

He didn't. He had begun to turn, the whole great weight of him, toward the way they'd come, and behind him the line of lamps swung, and somebody swore, and somebody said Christ, Davy, and the roof dripped steadily on all of them.

Ada got in front of him. It took her two tries, and the second try cost her a shin on something under the surface. She put her lamp up beside his face so he'd have to see it instead.

"Look at that," she said. "That's a lamp. It's mine. It's the only one in this pit I've not lost, so don't you dare put it out."

"Ada."

"Is it lit?"

"It's lit."

"Then it's lit. Now walk."

He walked. Not well. She kept hold of his belt and he kept hold of nothing, his big hands opening and closing, and he talked the whole way, about his mother's kitchen, about a dog, about the price of a pint at the Colliers' in the year the strike ended, anything, in a voice like a man carrying a plank. Ada let him. Behind her somebody was praying, quickly and without punctuation.

The floor rose. Water fell to her knees, her shins, her boots. Then she was on wet stone, and then dry stone, and the air, which was bad, was at least moving.

"Out," she said. "Everyone off the water. Move up, move up, there's no room to stand here."

The old shaft was another sixty yards on. They came to it in a straggle, lamps swinging, and the foot of it opened above them: a round throat of brick going up into black, a rusted cage frame hung crooked, a smell of cold iron. Somewhere very high there was a grey coin that might have been daylight and might have been wanting.

Davy sat down hard against the wall. He was shaking, and wet to the neck, and he had begun to laugh.

"Don't," said Ada.

"I'm not."

"You are."

"I'm not," he said, laughing.

She left him to it. She put her back to the brick, held up her lamp, and counted, because counting was free.

Davy. Hale. Orrin. Pask. Reuben.

She went down the row a second time, slower, with her finger lifted as if she might touch each one.

Davy. Hale. Orrin. Pask. Reuben.

And herself. Six.

"Who's last across?" she said.

Nobody answered. Hale looked at Orrin. Orrin looked at his boots. Pask, who was sixty and had never in his life volunteered anything, said, "I thought he was with you."

"Who was?"

Pask did not say it. Nobody said it. The cold dripped from the brick onto the stone, one drop and then another, and from the dip behind them, faint and level, came the sound of water settling back to flat.

Reuben had stopped saying fine.

"His mother," said Ada, to the dark in general. "I told his mother."

No one asked what she'd told her. She took a breath of the thin air, and there was no sweetness in it, and she took another.

Then she unbuckled her belt, hung her lamp on the nearest hook, and handed it to Hale.

"Hold that," she said. "Keep it lit. Don't let him"—Davy—"near it."

"Ada," said Hale.

She turned back toward the dip. The water was waiting, black and quiet, with the old timber standing in it like men. Behind her, five lamps went on burning.
