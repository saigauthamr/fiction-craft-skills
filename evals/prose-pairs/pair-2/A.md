# Chapter 1: The short count

Ines counted the barrels from left to right, and then she counted them from right to left, because the second count was the one she trusted.

Eleven.

The warehouse smelled of tar and wet rope. Each barrel sat on its own cradle of planks with the guild's mark burned into the lid, and the lamp on the beam swung a little whenever the door let the wind in. Between the sixth barrel and the seventh there was a gap exactly one barrel wide, with a clean square in the dust where a cradle had stood.

"Twelve on the manifest," she said.

"Twelve on the manifest," Tomas agreed. He was sitting on the eleventh barrel, which he was not supposed to do, with his boots crossed and his cap over one eye. "I brought twelve over. I'd have noticed. They're heavy."

"You counted them?"

"I lifted them. Same thing, with more complaining."

She went back to the gap and looked at the square in the dust. It had been swept. Someone had stood here with a broom and been careful, and then not careful enough at the edges. She left the pen where it was.

"Ines."

"I'm thinking."

"You're counting a third time. I can hear your lips." He tipped the cap back. "The guild pays by the barrel, and it pays for the barrels it can see. Write twelve, the warehouse master signs, I get my fare, and somebody on the upper quay has a bad morning in a week's time when it turns up in someone's cellar. Not ours."

"If I write twelve I've counted twelve."

"You've written twelve."

"That's the same sin with better handwriting." She opened the ledger on the lid of the nearest barrel. The page was ruled in the guild's blue, with a column for the manifest figure and a column for the counted figure, and in all her years she had never once seen the two columns disagree in anyone's hand but hers. She understood why. The second column was where trouble lived, and the guild preferred its trouble kept somewhere else.

"A short count gets the whole delivery held," Tomas said, more quietly. "They'll send a clerk. Then they'll send a second clerk to look at the first one. You'll be in the office all week explaining a square of dust."

"Then I'll explain it."

"You'll explain it badly. You don't lie badly, you just don't lie, which is worse. Nobody can work with it."

She dipped the pen. In the column that said manifest she put the twelve, because that was what the manifest said and the manifest was not hers. In the column that said counted she put eleven, and she made the one tall and the second one straight, so that nobody could ever read it as anything else.

She blotted it and did not look at him.

"Well," Tomas said. He slid off the barrel. "That's the end of my fare, then."

"It's the end of your fare on one barrel."

"It's the end of my good mood on all twelve. Eleven. Whichever." He found his cap and settled it where it belonged. "I'll tell the master you're sick. Something quiet. A cough."

"I'm not coughing."

"You will be, by the time he gets here."

---

The quay was empty at slack water, which was why she liked it. The tide had gone out of the harbor and not yet come back, and the boats lay on their sides in the mud like things that had been told to wait.

She went down the stair at the end of the third pier, where the stone was green and the rail had been replaced twice and was already going again, and she sat on the bottom step with her satchel on her knees and looked at the side channel.

The guild's chart had it only as a faint blue thread drawn by someone who had not wanted to be asked about it, a crooked line between the pier and the shoal that ferries went round. She had walked past it for six years. Every tide it did something different: it widened by a hand, or it bent toward the shoal, or it simply was not there and then was, as if it had been somewhere else and come back. She had a notebook of it. The notebook was not the guild's. It had a cracked spine and coffee on the cover, and every page said *could be* in her own handwriting.

There was a marker in her satchel. There was always a marker in her satchel, because a cartographer who left the office without one was a person with nothing to do. Salt-iron, the length of her hand, with the guild's number stamped on the foot. The number was for the channel it had been issued against. This was not that channel.

A marker could go in once. She knew the rule as she knew her own address. Once, and the water stayed where it was put, and nobody after you could say otherwise. The guild approved each one in triplicate, with a survey, and a second survey, and a seal from the upper quay.

It was a very small channel. It led nowhere she was allowed to go.

She took the marker out and held it up and looked at it. It looked like a tent peg, which she found a relief.

"Just to see," she said aloud, to nobody. It sounded exactly like something a person says before being sorry.

The water was starting back. A line of brown foam crept up the mud toward the step, and the thread of the side channel filled and shivered and began to lean toward the shoal. She waited until it had leaned as far as it would. She waited for the very top of the lean, because that was the number she wanted, the honest number, and then she waded in to her knees and pushed the marker down through the silt until her arm was wet to the elbow and her fingers hit something that was not mud.

The marker took. She felt it take, a small hard click through the iron, like a latch.

The water stopped leaning.

It kept moving, the way a river moves, but the thread of the channel lay along the pier as straight as a rule, and the foam ran down its length in a clean line, and the shoal beyond it, which for six years had sat in the wrong place at the wrong time, was simply and plainly where a shoal ought to be.

It was the best route she had ever seen. She stood in the cold and looked at it and could not find a single thing to correct.

She tried to pull the marker out. She knew it could not be done, and she tried anyway, twice, with both hands, until the iron had left a red line across each palm.

Then she climbed the stair with her boots full of harbor and her satchel dripping. At the top she opened the notebook and found the page that said *could be*, and she looked at it a while, and then she took out her pen and wrote the date, and the hour, and the height of the tide, and nothing else.

Up on the pier, a ferry was coming in along the old route, wide and slow, round the shoal as it always had. The pilot raised a hand to her. She raised one back, and kept it wet in her pocket.
