# Chapter 1: The short count

The guild paid by the barrel, which was why the guild liked its barrels counted by somebody else.

Ines counted them from the left, touching each lid with the flat of her pencil, and got to eleven. She went back to the door and counted from the right. Eleven.

The manifest said twelve.

"Well?" Tomas sat on the lowest crate with his boots off the ground and his hat over his eyes. He had been late to everything in his life except the tide, and the tide was now, so he was here.

"Eleven."

"It's a big warehouse. Some of them wander."

"They're pitch. They don't wander."

"You've never seen pitch at night." He tipped the hat up. "Count them again."

"I've counted twice."

"Then you've got the habit of it. Count a third time and you'll be sure."

She did not need to be sure. She was already sure, and that was the trouble. She stood with the pencil against her lip and looked at the gap along the back wall, where a barrel ought to have been and where the dust lay in a clean pale ring instead. Somebody had stood one there. Somebody had taken it away.

"The guild won't pay for a short count," Tomas said.

"I know."

"I mean it kindly. They pay for twelve. Twelve came off the boat, twelve are in the ledger at the Quay Office, and if you write eleven, somebody up the stairs has to find out where one went. Nobody wants to be the person who finds that out."

"You mean me."

"I mean everyone. You've just got the pencil." He looked at the pale ring in the dust and then, carefully, at the roof beams. "Ferrymen lose a barrel all the time. We call it weather."

"This isn't weather. It's the wrong side of the door."

"Ines."

"It's a ring in the dust, Tomas."

He sighed. It was a long, theatrical sigh, the kind he used on passengers who asked whether the crossing was safe. "Write twelve. Nobody counts the same room twice, and you're the only one who does."

The ledger lay open on the barrel-head she used for a desk. The line was ruled for her already, the number in the last column waiting. The ink was wet from the morning's entries, and her hand, when she took the pen, was perfectly steady.

She wrote eleven.

She blotted it and did not look at him.

"Right," said Tomas, after a while. He put his boots back on. "I'll say I never saw you do that."

"You didn't. You were asleep."

"I was resting my eyes on your behalf." He stood, stretched, and went out into the light. At the door he said, over his shoulder, "It's going to be a wet week at the Office."

She shut the ledger. The cover left a thumb of pitch on her palm, and she wiped it on her skirt.

---

The side channel behind Cooper's Quay had no name on the guild's map. It had a number, forty-one, and a thin grey line that wavered a little at the bend, the way the lines did when somebody had drawn them in a hurry and with no great faith.

Ines came down the steps at slack water with the satchel against her hip. The quay was empty. The gulls had gone off to wherever they went when the tide stopped, and the boats lay on their lines with their noses pointing nowhere in particular.

She set the satchel on the stones and took out the marker.

It was not an approved one. Approved markers came sealed from the guild stores, stamped, signed for, numbered in a book. This was a bar of salt-iron a hand long, rough-cast, that she had bought off a smith in the lower town for two silver and a promise not to say so. It was cold in a way iron was not usually cold. It left a faint taste at the back of the teeth when she held it close.

Channel forty-one had been drifting for three seasons. She had the soundings, six of them, and every one disagreed with the last by a little more than it should. Nobody had asked her to look at it. Nobody was going to.

She knelt at the edge of the quay and wedged the bar into the gap between two stones where the water came up green and slow. Then she waited, because a thing placed was a thing worth watching.

For a while nothing happened.

Then the water at the bend drew in on itself. It did not rush. It was more like a held breath being let go, a smooth sliding pull that took the green with it, and a line opened out across the harbor mouth, straight as a ruled edge, from the quay to the far channel markers and past them. The drifting silt settled and lay down on either side of it like cloth being smoothed.

Ines counted the pilings along its length. Seventeen. They stood in a row now that had not been a row an hour ago.

She did it again, because she could not leave it. She put her hand to the bar and pulled.

It did not move. It was not stuck, exactly. It sat in the stone as though the stone had grown around it, and the cold in it had gone out, and when she let go her palm was dry and pale and quite unharmed.

A route, fixed. The book said so, and the book was the one thing in the guild she had never known to be wrong. A marker was placed once. After that the channel was whatever it was, and nobody, not the guild, not the harbormaster, not she herself, could ask it to be anything else.

She sat back on her heels. Out on the water a ferry was coming in from the east on its usual line, the long way round, and did not turn.

Seventeen pilings. She took out the pencil, found a clean page at the back of the satchel, and wrote the number down before she could think about it. Then she wrote the date, and the hour, and the state of the tide, because that was how it was done.

She looked at the page for some time.

Then she tore it out, folded it small, and put it in her boot. The ledger at the Quay Office would say eleven barrels, and a great many things about it would be true. Channel forty-one would stay a thin grey line on the guild's map for as long as anyone looked at the map.

Behind her a gull came back and landed on the new straight water, and stood on it as if it had always been there.
