# Chapter 1: Tuesday

I have washed more dead people than most undertakers, and I could not make myself empty his fridge.

I stood on the step at ten to seven in the morning with the key in my hand and my night still on me, the smell of the ward in my hair, hand gel and lilies and the particular warmth of a building where nobody is allowed to be cold. It was four days since the funeral. The plan was simple. Feed the dog, empty the fridge, take the bins out, leave. I had written it on the back of my hand at the end of shift, in case I got sentimental on the drive and tried to do something else.

The dog met me in the hall. Biscuit is fourteen and entirely deaf, so he did not hear the key, and so he did not come to the door; he was simply there, in the way that furniture is there, looking at me as if I were late, which I was. He had been fed by the neighbor on Saturday and Sunday and by nobody on Monday. I put down a scoop of the dry food and he ate it with the concentration of a man doing his taxes.

"He's not coming back," I told him. He kept eating. It is a kindness, in my work, when they cannot hear it.

The house smelled the way it always did, of floor polish and the inside of a radiator. My father had run it like a county survey, everything measured and put back to the millimetre, and nobody had told the house yet. His reading glasses were on the arm of the chair, folded. The chair itself I did not look at. I had looked at it for eleven minutes on the Tuesday night, with the neighbor's hand on my elbow, and felt I had done my share.

The kitchen, then. I put on the kettle, because a kettle gives your hands somewhere to be, and opened the fridge.

I was ready for milk. I was ready for the end of a block of cheese, and the pickled turnips he bought by the jar and ate one at a time, like a man being paid per turnip. What I got was a wall of white plastic. Boxes, stacked and slightly leaning, the lids fogged. Takeaway containers with the cardboard sleeves still on, the grease gone translucent at the corners. Eight of them. Ten. In the bin under the sink there were more, flattened, and the bag was tied off, and behind it another bag.

I turned one over. The sleeve had a name on it in green, and a phone number, and a small drawing of a pomegranate. Beit Sitti. Ferrow.

I read it twice. It is forty minutes to Ferrow, two towns over, and my father did not drive after dark and did not, as far as I had ever known, eat anything he had not either grown or been given by a Haddad. I put the box back square to the shelf.

The freezer was worse, or better; I have not decided. I opened it for the ice scraper, because I am a person who thinks of the ice scraper at a time like this, and it was full. Not frozen peas, not a bag of bread. Rows of containers, stacked like bricks, each with a strip of masking tape across the lid and a date in black marker. My handwriting. Lamb and green beans. Chicken with rice. Lentil soup, double batch. Lamb and green beans again. The dates ran back eight months. I took one out and the cold bit through my glove, and underneath the date I had written, every time, *Heat 4 mins, stir, heat 2 more*, because he had once microwaved a stew for twenty minutes and blamed me.

None of them had been opened. The tape was clean. The corners of the lids had that fine grey bloom that frost gives to things nobody is coming back for.

Three times a week for six years. I did the sum standing there with the freezer breathing on my shins, and then I made myself stop, because it was the kind of sum I do too often and have never once liked the answer to.

I put the lamb and green beans back, label out. He was seventy-nine and he had opinions about cumin. I did not take it personally.

The kettle had boiled and clicked itself off. I did not make tea. I wanted bin bags.

He kept them in the drawer by the cooker, the one that stuck. I knew that. I had known it for forty-one years, since before I knew there was anything else to know. I put my hip against the cabinet and pulled, and the drawer gave all at once, the way it does, and the contents slid forward. Rubber bands. Takeaway menus in a rubber band. A tape measure. Receipts in a biscuit tin, sorted by month, which was only him. Under the receipts a roll of black sacks, and on top of the roll, a phone.

It was not his phone. His was in the pocket of the coat they had given back to me at the hospital in a clear bag. This one was small and cheap, the kind sold at a petrol station next to the lighters, with a rubber case that had gone soft. It was plugged into a charger I had never seen, the lead run neatly behind the drawer runner and out through a hole that someone had drilled.

Someone had drilled a hole in his kitchen drawer. He would have measured the bit first.

The screen was lit. Eighty-one percent, and a photograph of nothing: a grey sky over a car park.

I did not touch it. I shut the drawer on it, which was the sensible thing, and it came open again, because that drawer has never once shut on the first try. I stood with my hip against it. I counted the tiles behind the cooker. There are forty-four. I have counted them before, with his dinner on, listening to the radio in the other room.

Then I picked it up. It had no passcode. For the record: it had no passcode. A man who kept every receipt he was ever handed had not thought this worth locking.

There was one contact. A single letter. *S*.

The call log did not need scrolling. It was one name, over and over, a column of them, and I thumbed down through it and the date at the side kept going, and the dates were not scattered. They were a Tuesday, and a Tuesday, and a Tuesday. Evenings. Six fifty-eight, or seven-oh-two, or seven-oh-one. I know that hour the way I know my own pulse. It is when I badge in, and put my bag in the locker, and read the board to see who has gone since I was last there. For three years, at that hour, my father had been on the phone to S, and I had been at work being useful to strangers.

Forty minutes. Fifty. A hundred and ten, one winter. The shortest was forty seconds, and I wondered what you could say in forty seconds.

Behind me Biscuit finished his food and came into the kitchen and lay on my foot. It was a warm weight and I let it stay.

I did not need to ask who S was. He runs a kitchen two towns over, and his boxes have a pomegranate on the sleeve.

I was still holding the phone when it began to ring.

It buzzed against my palm and the screen turned over to the single letter, and the whole kitchen went white and then orange around it, like a bulb. It was seven in the morning, which was not the hour. I checked the date in the corner of the screen anyway. The dog, who cannot hear a thing, lifted his head.

It was Tuesday.
