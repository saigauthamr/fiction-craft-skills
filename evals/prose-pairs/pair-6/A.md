# Chapter 1: Tuesday

The key still stuck in the lock on Alder Road, and I still lifted the door by its handle while I turned it, because for six years that was how you did it and nobody had told the lock. I came off shift at seven the night before and had not been to bed. My hands smelled of the ward, that pink hand gel, and under it of Mrs. Okafor, who had died at ten past four with her daughter holding one hand and me holding the other, and who had said thank you twice. People mostly do. It is one of the perks.

Biscuit was in the hall. He is deaf, so he arranges to be near the door and then looks surprised. I stamped on the floorboard and he turned his whole body around to find me, looked at my hands, and went back to the kitchen to wait for someone better.

"Same," I said. He didn't hear that either.

The plan was forty minutes. Feed the dog. Empty the fridge, because four days of nobody in a house is how you get a smell, and the neighbors had found enough in this family already. Bin the milk, bin the bread, leave. I had the list in my phone in that order.

I fed the dog. Half a tin and the biscuits on top, the way he likes it, the way the vet had told my father not to give it to him. Then I opened the fridge. One carton of milk, in date. One lemon. The rest of the shelves were takeaway boxes, stacked and squared off, the black plastic kind with a gold lid and a name printed in curly letters across it. Beit Sitti. Eleven of them. Some had been eaten out of and put back with the lid pressed on, which is how he put everything back, as if a fridge were a filing system and the dead were coming to audit.

I knew the name because Sami's name was on the sign. Ferrow is two towns over. Nobody delivers from Ferrow to Alder Road; you drive, or someone drives for you. My father had not driven after dark since his cataract.

I put the boxes in a bin bag. Then there was no bin bag, because the roll under the sink was gone, and I opened the freezer on the theory that he had put it there, and that is how I came to be standing in front of the freezer with the door open and the cold coming out around my ankles.

Mine. All of it. The tubs were stacked in rows, the oblong ones for the lentils and the square ones for the stew, each with a strip of masking tape on the lid and my handwriting on the tape. Lentil, 14 Mar. Chicken and rice, 28 Mar. Kofta, do not microwave from frozen. I counted. It was not a decision. Thirty-one tubs, and the rows ran back months, past a bag of peas he had never in his life eaten, to a lasagne with a date on it from before the clocks changed. The one at the front was from the Sunday before he died. I had written *Eat this first* on it, and underlined it, and he had not.

I stood there and did the sum. Three nights a week, four hours each, the shop, the pans to scrub, the drive there and the drive back. I keep that sum in a drawer. I have a good head for it, which is the trouble.

The cold went through my socks. The compressor kicked in with its old cough.

I shut the freezer. Opened it again, checked the lasagne was really a lasagne, shut it.

Bin bags, then. The drawer by the cooker, where my mother kept string and my father kept everything that had once been given to him in a bag. Batteries, a tape measure, a ring binder of receipts with a clip on it that was labelled in his square surveyor's capitals RECEIPTS 2, which meant there was a RECEIPTS 1 and I would be finding that. A roll of the bags, at the back, under a phone.

This was not his phone. His was in a plastic tub at the funeral director's, with his glasses and his watch, because I had signed for it. This one was a small grey handset with buttons, the kind they sell at petrol stations for people who are about to do something they would rather not have on their account. It was plugged into the wall behind the toaster. The little light was green. Charged, and kept charged. Nobody does that by forgetting.

I held it a while with the bin bags in my other hand. I would like to say I weighed the decision. I am a professional; I know what it is to be handed a thing that is not yours, a hospital bracelet, a locket, a letter in an envelope with a name on it that is not the name of anyone at the bedside, and I know the correct thing is to hand it to the next of kin. I was the next of kin. That is the sort of joke that only works if you are not standing in it.

I pressed the button.

There was no code. The screen lit up the color of weak tea. Contacts: one. S.

Just S. Not Sami. Nothing I could have taken for the plumber. I pressed the other button and it opened the call log, and the log opened out and out and kept going, because the phone had a memory like his, and I scrolled with my thumb through a list so even it looked like a ward rota. Tuesday, 7:02 p.m. Tuesday, 6:58. Tuesday, 7:04. Forty minutes, an hour, twenty. One missed in March, returned within the minute. Every Tuesday, back and back. I stopped scrolling when the year changed, and then I did a bad thing and kept going until the year changed again, and again, and I stopped when I ran out of phone.

Three years.

Seven o'clock is when I start. Handover is at seven; I am in the car park at twenty to, in the car park with the engine off and the radio on, being a person who has already left. I did not come here on Tuesdays. I had told him so, and he had said, perfectly pleasantly, that he knew, and that I should not worry about Tuesdays.

I read the log again from the top, in case it had changed.

Biscuit had come to lean on my shin. Dogs do that when something in the room has changed, and I thought, not very kindly, that he might have mentioned it.

The phone buzzed in my hand, loud on the plastic, and the screen went white, and a single letter sat on it.

It was seven in the morning, and it was Tuesday.
